What is an idea worth, and how would we know? 14

Measuring novelty, breakthroughs, and impact - and the wedge between what an invention earns and the good it does.

  • 2026-08-27 · 14 min

    Basic science doesn't get drugs approved on its own

    Fundamental, predictive science by itself does not raise a drug candidate's chance of reaching the market; the candidates that get approved are the ones whose scientific base combines fundamental understanding with contextualized, applied knowledge about how things behave in real bodies.

    innovation science

  • 2026-08-12 · 12 min

    Innovation pays back several times what it costs — and the estimate rests on three numbers

    The aggregate social return to innovation investment is very large — at least several dollars of social value per dollar spent — but the headline figure is not a measurement; it is a ratio of three macroeconomic quantities, and what makes it credible is that it stays large across every defensible choice of those inputs and that an independent micro literature lands in the same neighbourhood.

    innovation growth

  • 2026-07-30 · 16 min

    We can measure what patents are worth in bulk, but not one at a time

    The main methods for valuing a patent agree reasonably well on aggregate patent rents, but disagree sharply about which individual patent is valuable — because they price different objects (the legal right, the invention, or the market's expectation) on differently selected samples.

    innovation measurement intellectual property

  • 2026-06-10 · 14 min

    AI is becoming a general-purpose method of invention — slowly, and unevenly

    Modern AI is best understood not as one more product but as a general-purpose 'method of invention' — a research tool that lowers the cost of search and prediction across many fields — and the early evidence shows it cutting the cost of inventive work and beginning to shift the direction of discovery, even though clean estimates of its effect on the rate of discovery are still scarce and the most famous one collapsed.

    innovation artificial-intelligence

  • 2026-06-09 · 13 min

    A lot of innovation comes from its users - but most of it never spreads

    Users and customers, not just producer R&D labs, are a large and systematically undercounted source of innovation - especially of functionally novel products - but because users rarely have an incentive to diffuse what they build, most user innovation stays private and unseen.

    economics of innovation user innovation

  • 2026-06-08 · 13 min

    Good ideas don't travel as far as you'd think

    Knowledge spillovers between inventors are geographically localized — ideas disproportionately build on nearby work — and although the first estimates were inflated by crude measurement, a generation of better-designed studies confirms the localization is real, runs largely through the movement of people, and fades only slowly with distance.

    innovation economics economic geography

  • 2026-06-07 · 14 min

    Science is getting less disruptive — but not by 90%

    The famous finding that papers and patents have become dramatically less disruptive over time is partly real and partly a measurement artifact: the headline 90–100% magnitudes are mostly inflated by the growth of reference lists, and the patent slump largely dissolves under correction, but a genuine, more modest decline in the *rate* of disruptive science survives the best critiques because independent, non-citation signals point the same way.

    economics innovation science-of-science

  • 2026-06-07 · 15 min

    The most valuable inventions stand closest to science

    Inventions that build directly on recent science are substantially more valuable than otherwise-similar inventions that don't, the value premium fades with citation distance from the science frontier, and the biggest hits cluster near it — a measurable case that public science seeds the most valuable technology.

    economics of innovation science and technology

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    Most patents are worth almost nothing — and a few are worth a fortune

    The private value of patents is extraordinarily right-skewed — a small minority captures most of the total value while the median patent is worth very little — so averages and raw patent counts badly misdescribe inventive output.

    innovation patents economics-of-science

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    “Radical,” “disruptive,” and “breakthrough” are not the same thing

    The words we use for exceptional innovation -- radical, disruptive, breakthrough, discontinuous -- are not synonyms: they bundle together two genuinely distinct properties, how novel an invention is and how much impact it has, and because each label and metric weights those two differently, the one you pick changes which inventions and firms count as exceptional.

    economics of innovation science of science

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    To spot a breakthrough, read the patent — don't just count its citations

    Text-based measures of invention — built from the words of patents rather than their citation counts — capture things citations miss and partly fix citations' documented biases, so which inventions look important depends on which ruler you choose; the two agree often enough to trust and disagree often enough to matter.

    economics innovation science-of-science

  • 2026-06-04 · 13 min

    High-markup firms invent more valuably - and not just for themselves

    Across firms, higher markups go with innovation that is more valuable both to the firm (private value) and to others (knowledge spillovers), so the reflex that markups are pure social waste is too simple - but this is a robust cross-firm association, not a causal license for monopoly, and at the level of aggregate dynamism market power may still slow innovation down.

    economics innovation

  • 2026-06-03 · 13 min

    The money an invention makes is a loose guide to the good it does

    The private value an inventor captures and the social value an invention creates are only loosely linked - inventors keep a small, unrepresentative share - so an innovation system run on private incentives alone under-supplies the most socially valuable inventions.

    economics innovation

  • 2026-06-02 · 20 min

    Novelty is a high-variance bet — at least in patents

    Inventions built from more novel knowledge combinations don't earn higher impact on average -- if anything slightly lower -- but they have much higher variance, fattening both the breakthrough tail and the failure tail. The pattern is well documented in patents; in science it rests on a novelty measure that two validation studies question, and in biotechnology it does not hold at all.

    economics of innovation science of science

Can we steer innovation toward the climate? 4

Whether prices, policy, and knowledge spillovers can redirect invention toward clean technology - and what dirty air costs us.

  • 2026-06-06 · 12 min

    Dirty air doesn't just make us sick — it makes regions invent less

    Fine-particulate air pollution causally lowers a region's inventive output, so air pollution carries an innovation cost — plausibly a growth cost — on top of its well-documented health cost.

    environmental economics innovation

  • 2026-06-06 · 12 min

    The clean-energy innovation slump was cold feet, not lost value

    The decline in clean-energy patenting after the late-2000s was driven mainly by a retreat of risk-bearing capital from long-horizon, capital-intensive bets, not by clean technology becoming less valuable — so the policy lever it points to is de-risking finance, not only a higher carbon price.

    environmental-economics innovation finance

  • 2026-06-05 · 13 min

    A clean research dollar spills over more than a dirty one

    Knowledge spillovers from clean technologies appear to be larger than from the dirty technologies they replace, which gives an innovation-policy case for subsidizing clean R&D that is separate from, and additional to, the carbon externality.

    environmental-economics innovation

  • 2026-06-05 · 13 min

    A temporary push can permanently redirect innovation

    Because the direction of innovation is path-dependent — firms keep inventing in the technologies they already know — a temporary price signal can have a permanent effect on the direction of technical change, so the timing and direction of innovation policy matter as much as its strength.

    innovation economics environmental economics

Does innovation policy actually work? 11

R&D subsidies, public and defense research, and picking winners: what the money actually buys.

  • 2026-06-10 · 13 min

    Patents really do teach — when you can read and reach them

    The disclosure ('teaching') half of the patent bargain does real work: making patent disclosure earlier, more reachable, or more readable measurably raises follow-on invention, and suppressing it cuts invention — so the accessibility of disclosure is an innovation-policy lever separate from how strong patents are.

    innovation policy

  • 2026-06-09 · 14 min

    We invent for the markets that can pay

    Market size causally directs invention - R&D and new products flow toward larger, more profitable markets - so the direction of innovation tracks purchasing power rather than need, which is why the diseases of the poor are systematically under-invented.

    innovation policy health

  • 2026-06-08 · 12 min

    The patent clock bends research toward the short run

    Because the standard patent term is a fixed clock that starts at filing, the patent system steers private R&D toward quick-payoff projects and away from long-horizon research, so patents shape the direction of innovation, not just its amount.

    innovation intellectual property

  • 2026-06-08 · 14 min

    Patents slow the innovation that builds on them — mainly when they lock up access

    Intellectual property on upstream discoveries can slow the follow-on innovation that builds on them, but mainly when it restricts access to a needed input — data, materials, or a thicket of rights too costly to license — and the drag is small or absent where the underlying knowledge stays open.

    innovation policy

  • 2026-06-08 · 13 min

    Some inventions have to be pulled, not pushed

    Demand-side 'pull' incentives - inducement prizes and advance market commitments that pay only for a delivered, pre-specified result - can elicit innovation that supply-side 'push' grants miss, but only for specifiable targets, and as a complement to push rather than a replacement.

    innovation policy

  • 2026-06-08 · 13 min

    The return to public science keeps surviving better methods

    Estimates of the return to publicly funded science — from 1980s state-level correlations through modern natural experiments — converge on a large, positive social return that has survived rather than shrunk as identification improved; what stays uncertain is the precise number, not its sign or order of magnitude.

    innovation science policy

  • 2026-06-07 · 12 min

    Public science pays off in patents — mostly at companies that never got the grant

    Public research grants — NIH biomedical funding above all — causally increase private-sector patenting, and most of the induced patents are taken out by firms that never received the money, so standard accounting badly understates the return to public science.

    innovation science policy

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    You mostly can't spot a breakthrough at birth

    The eventual impact of a paper or invention is only weakly predictable at the moment it appears — early citations and peer scores are noisy, evaluators flinch from the novelty that becomes breakthroughs, and a large share of which work becomes a hit is path-dependent luck — so portfolios, partial lotteries, and second chances beat confident winner-picking.

    economics science-of-science innovation-policy

  • 2026-06-06 · 14 min

    Pick research winners by their spillovers

    Because the social returns to R&D are large and highly uneven across firms and technologies, steering a fixed innovation-subsidy budget toward the highest-spillover units promises materially higher social returns than spreading it uniformly — a disciplined, measurable case for 'picking winners,' bounded in practice by measurement and political capture.

    economics innovation-policy industrial-policy

  • 2026-06-06 · 13 min

    Mission-driven public R&D mostly crowds invention in, not out

    Mission-driven public R&D - wartime crash programs and defense/public research - tends to crowd private invention in rather than out, spilling into civilian patenting well beyond the funded labs, though the magnitude is bounded by the scarcity of scientists.

    innovation public-economics

  • 2026-06-06 · 15 min

    R&D subsidies mostly buy real innovation, not relabeled spending

    Well-designed R&D subsidies - direct grants to young, financially constrained firms and R&D tax credits - mostly cause genuinely additional private innovation rather than crowding out private money or relabeling spending firms would have done anyway; but the additionality is concentrated where finance is the binding constraint and leaks badly for large incumbents and weakly monitored schemes.

    economics innovation

How should we organize people to invent? 5

Team size and breadth, collaboration across distant fields, pivots, and what happens when a key partner is lost.

  • 2026-06-07 · 14 min

    Big teams develop, small teams disrupt (mostly)

    Across science and technology, large teams reliably push existing lines of work forward, while the rarer, direction-changing breakthroughs come disproportionately from small teams and solo authors — a robust correlation that survives within-author controls and the better critiques, though it rests on a contested metric and describes what teams tend to do rather than proving that adding people smothers creativity.

    economics innovation science-of-science

  • 2026-06-07 · 14 min

    Breakthroughs come from bridging distant fields -- but most bridges fail

    Inventions and papers that bridge cognitively distant fields are over-represented among breakthroughs, but the average distant bridge underperforms and most fail outright -- distance buys the tail, not the mean -- so cross-field bridging is a low-hit-rate, fat-tailed bet that funding and evaluation systems, which judge on the average and the short run, systematically under-reward.

    economics of innovation science of science

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    Broader teams don't invent more — they're just where the good inventors are

    The strong correlation between a team's knowledge breadth and its inventive output is mostly selection — abler inventors, better-resourced firms, and more promising projects assemble broader teams — so once you isolate quasi-random variation the causal effect of breadth on the amount a team invents shrinks toward zero, even if breadth still shapes the kind of invention.

    economics innovation teams

  • 2026-06-02 · 13 min

    Some of your productivity isn't yours — it's the team's

    A meaningful share of an individual's measured productivity is team-specific — it lives in particular collaborative relationships, not in the person — so losing a key collaborator causes a lasting, only-partly-recovered decline in output, and what carries the loss is the collaborative tie in idea-space, not physical proximity or the prestige of nearby colleagues.

    economics of science innovation human capital

  • 2026-06-02 · 15 min

    Big research pivots usually underperform — but novelty doesn't

    When researchers pivot away from their established expertise, their new work is much less likely to land — a large, near-universal, and growing 'pivot penalty' — but the culprit is the loss of a conventional home base, not novelty itself.

    economics-of-science innovation

Who gets to do science, and who gets the credit? 11

Talent, mobility, and the gender gaps in productivity, credit, and peer review.

  • 2026-06-26 · 14 min

    Counting citations helped science find its hidden stars

    When citation counts first became visible — with the Science Citation Index in the 1960s — they reduced information frictions in the academic labor market, improving the match between scientists and departments and disproportionately helping highly-cited scientists stranded in low-ranked departments ('hidden stars'), even as the same visible metrics fed Matthew effects in promotions and grants.

    economics-of-science performance-metrics

  • 2026-06-10 · 13 min

    Bad science contaminates the work around it

    A wrong or retracted finding imposes real, lasting costs on the work around it — the author's other papers, their collaborators, and the surrounding field — and the scientific record corrects so slowly that errors keep being cited for years, which makes research reliability a public good.

    science-of-science innovation

  • 2026-06-09 · 14 min

    Clusters make inventors more productive (probably) — and concentrate the gains

    Clustering inventors together plausibly raises their productivity — agglomeration economies are real — though the cleanest inventor-level causal estimate is now contested, so the size is uncertain; meanwhile the same forces reliably bid up local costs and concentrate the gains, so innovation clusters likely raise aggregate invention and spatial inequality together.

    innovation economics economic geography

  • 2026-06-09 · 13 min

    Open data doesn't just grow science — it changes who makes the discoveries

    Lowering the cost of accessing research inputs — especially open data — doesn't only raise the amount of follow-on science; it shifts who produces it toward entrants and outsiders — junior firms, younger researchers, and scientists at less-affluent and developing-world institutions.

    innovation who

  • 2026-06-09 · 13 min

    Who invents shapes what gets invented

    Inventor identity shapes the direction of innovation, not just its volume: women inventors are markedly more likely to invent for women's needs — especially in health — so the inventor gender gap leaves women-focused problems systematically under-invented.

    economics innovation gender

  • 2026-06-08 · 14 min

    When inventors can't leave, their ideas stay put — and innovation slows

    Enforceable non-compete agreements reduce inventor mobility and the cross-firm knowledge flows that mobility carries, and the best evidence is that this slows innovation on net in the regions and industries that enforce them — even though non-competes also protect firms' incentive to invest, a real benefit that turns out to be the smaller one.

    economics innovation labor mobility

  • 2026-06-07 · 15 min

    Women get less credit for group work

    Women receive less professional credit than men for the same collaborative work — in economics, coauthored papers raise men's tenure odds far more than women's — and this credit gap is a real, if bounded, contributor to the gender gap in academic advancement.

    economics gender economics-of-science labor

  • 2026-06-07 · 14 min

    The gender gap in science is partly the environment, not the scientist

    A meaningful share of the gender gap in science — who enters it and how much active scientists are measured as producing — comes from malleable environment (childhood exposure and role models, collaboration networks, family constraints, and the rules of the workplace) rather than from fixed gender differences in ability or preferences, which matters because environmental causes are fixable.

    economics science gender

  • 2026-06-07 · 15 min

    Inventors go where they're treated best

    Inventive talent is mobile and responds to incentives — top inventors relocate in response to taxes and conditions, and immigrant inventors contribute far out of proportion to their numbers — so a country's innovative capacity is not a fixed endowment but partly a policy choice, mostly by changing where invention happens rather than by creating new inventors.

    economics innovation migration

  • 2026-06-07 · 13 min

    Women are held to a higher bar to get published — in economics, at least

    In economics, the best evidence suggests women's papers are held to a higher bar in peer review — they spend longer under review and must clear a higher quality threshold — and a striking side effect is that women's published papers are measurably better written and more cited; but this is a field-specific pattern, not a universal law of refereeing.

    economics gender science

  • 2026-06-02 · 15 min

    Much 'missing' invention is misallocated talent, not missing talent

    A large share of the gap between potential and realized invention is misallocation — talented people who never enter inventing, and inventors stuck in the wrong firm or field — rather than a shortage of talent or an unavoidable exhaustion of ideas, so cutting the frictions that keep inventive talent from its best use could raise idea production.

    economics-of-innovation misallocation

Beyond innovation: work, health, and prices 7

Notes that wander outside the economics of innovation - into labor, health, crime, and the cost of living.

  • 2026-06-02 · 14 min

    Dirty air makes us worse at our jobs — bodies and brains alike

    Short-run air-pollution exposure causally lowers productivity through both a physical channel (manual labor and labor supply) and a cognitive channel (decisions and mental tasks), at the moderate concentrations common in cities today — so cleaner air is a sizeable, under-counted productivity gain.

    environmental-economics labor-economics

  • 2026-06-02 · 13 min

    Automation comes for workers, not for work (mostly)

    When a technology automates an occupation, the lasting losses fall on the incumbents who held those jobs, even though the labor market usually adjusts so future cohorts and total employment are largely unharmed; automation's central problem is the concentrated incidence on displaced incumbents, not a permanent shortage of work.

    labor economics automation technological change

  • 2026-06-02 · 13 min

    The same drug looks more effective when its maker funds the trial

    When the same drug is tested against the same comparator, it is reported as substantially more effective in trials sponsored by its manufacturer than in trials without that sponsorship — and the distortion runs mainly through which results get published and how they are reported, not through faked data, so transparency reforms can blunt it.

    health economics economics of science pharmaceuticals meta-research

  • 2026-06-02 · 13 min

    Deporting criminals can backfire

    Deporting criminals can backfire: removing offenders from a rich country does not destroy crime so much as relocate it, and when transferable criminal capital lands in a weak state it can seed violent organizations whose fallout — including migration — flows back.

    crime migration development immigration-enforcement

  • 2026-06-02 · 13 min

    Treating a fragile newborn helps more than the newborn

    The returns to early-life medical care are larger than standard evaluations suggest, because the benefits spill over to siblings and mothers -- though the best evidence for that spillover is a single Danish study, and it shows up in test scores rather than in how much schooling anyone ends up getting.

    health economics human capital early-life family

  • 2026-06-02 · 14 min

    When the big store comes to town, almost everyone's groceries get cheaper

    The dominant welfare effect of modern retail competition — chain supermarkets, big-box stores, new entrants — runs through the cost of living: it makes shopping substantially cheaper, and those gains reach the poor in absolute terms; the real but concentrated losses fall on incumbent shopkeepers, and the genuinely contested question is whether the consumer gains tilt toward the rich or the poor.

    industrial-organization trade-economics

  • 2026-06-02 · 14 min

    Soft skills, hard returns

    Short, low-cost training in how people communicate and cooperate can raise worker productivity -- sometimes dramatically -- with gains that spread to coworkers; the catches are that it has to be the right kind of training, and the worker is usually not the one who pockets the return.

    labor economics human capital firm productivity

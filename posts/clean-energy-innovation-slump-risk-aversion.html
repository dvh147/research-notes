Between 2006 and 2011, venture capitalists poured more than $25 billion into clean-energy startups — batteries, biofuels, thin-film solar, novel materials — and then lost more than half of it. That's the headline of Benjamin Gaddy and co-authors' autopsy of what's now called the first "cleantech bubble": they aggregate hundreds of investments and find that cleantech delivered venture investors a worse risk-and-return profile than either software or medical startups, dragged down by capital-hungry companies developing new hardware and chemistries that never reached manufacturing scale. By the early 2010s the money had fled.

The inventing followed. According to a joint analysis of patent data by the International Energy Agency and the European Patent Office, patenting in low-carbon energy supply technologies has been falling since 2012; after a brief recovery, low-carbon patenting grew at just 3.3% a year over 2017–2019 — about a quarter of the 12.5% annual clip it managed over 2000–2013. The clean-energy innovation machine, which had been roaring, downshifted hard.

Here's what makes that a puzzle rather than a footnote: it happened at exactly the wrong time, for no obvious reason on the technology side. Climate urgency was rising, not falling. Solar and wind costs kept tumbling and their deployment kept climbing right through the slump. The world plainly wanted more clean energy, and clean energy was plainly getting better. So why did the invention of it stall?

There are two stories, and which one is true should change what we do about it. Story one: clean technology simply got less valuable — the easy inventions were used up, the prize for the next one shrank, and rational inventors moved on. If that's right, the policy fix is on the demand side: make clean innovation pay more, mainly by pricing carbon. Story two: clean technology stayed valuable, but it is the kind of bet — long-horizon, capital-intensive, technically uncertain — that only gets funded when risk capital is feeling brave; and after 2008, and after the cleantech bust burned a generation of investors, it stopped feeling brave. If that's right, the fix is on the supply-of-capital side: de-risk the financing of long-shot clean bets.

This post argues that the weight of the evidence favors story two. The slump was, mostly, cold feet — not lost value. I'll be honest about the "mostly": some of the decline is genuine maturation, and patents are an imperfect yardstick. But the shape of the slump, the independent evidence that clean technology remained valuable, and a clean body of work on how risk capital behaves all point the same way.

The slump is shaped like a money problem

Start with the timing and the texture of the decline, because they already tilt the scales.

A value-collapse story predicts a particular pattern: inventing should fade where the technological opportunities are genuinely exhausted, gradually, across the board. What actually happened looks different. The fall was sharp, it clustered around a financial crisis and a venture bust, and — per Gaddy and colleagues — it bit hardest on "deep technology": the companies trying to commercialize new hardware, materials, and processes, which "consumed the most capital and yielded the lowest returns." Those are precisely the bets that need a long runway and a lot of money before anyone learns whether they work. The capital-intensive end of clean energy didn't get cheaper to invent in; it got harder to finance.

It helps to separate two things a downturn does. It can lower the return to inventing (story one). Or it can raise the cost and scarcity of the capital needed to carry an invention from idea to scale (story two). The cleantech bust is a textbook instance of the second: a wave of capital arrived, discovered that energy hardware takes a decade and a fortune to de-risk, took heavy losses, and withdrew — leaving a financing vacuum exactly where clean energy's hardest and potentially most important inventions live. That's not the signature of a technology running out of road. It's the signature of money losing its nerve.

Did the prize actually shrink?

The cleanest way to adjudicate is to ask directly whether clean inventions became less valuable. They don't appear to have.

We can measure the private value of an invention reasonably well. Leonid Kogan, Dimitris Papanikolaou, Amit Seru, and Noah Stoffman, in a much-cited 2017 paper, use the stock market's reaction to news of a patent grant to put a dollar figure on each patent, and show that this market-based value tracks the patent's later scientific importance and predicts firms' subsequent growth — a credible ruler for what an invention is worth to the firm that owns it. A working paper by Ralf Martin and Dennis Verhoeven, The Private Value of Clean Energy Innovation, turns this lens on clean energy specifically, and the question it poses is the one this whole post circles: did the post-crisis slump coincide with a collapse in the value the market assigns to clean inventions, or only in the number of them? Its reading — that the slump shows up in the quantity of clean inventing, not in a per-invention value that fell out from under it — is the premise I'm stress-testing against the rest of the literature. (It's unpublished work, so I'll lean on the independent evidence below rather than quote it for numbers.)

That independent evidence cuts against a value collapse. On the social side of the ledger, clean knowledge looks unusually generative, not played out: in a companion post I walk through the evidence that clean patents spill over to later inventors more than the dirty technologies they replace, with clean spillovers estimated to rival those from IT. A field whose ideas are still throwing off that much fuel for the next idea is not a field that has run dry. And on the deployment side, the plain facts argue the same way: a technology that keeps getting cheaper and capturing more of the market every year is becoming more useful, not less. If the prize for inventing the next better battery were really shrinking, it's strange that the market for batteries was simultaneously exploding.

None of this proves private value held up — the direct measurement is what the seed paper is for. But the surrounding evidence makes "clean tech quietly became a bad place to invent" a hard sell. Which pushes us toward the other suspect: the money.

Why risk capital gets cold feet

There's a clean, well-identified body of work on how the supply of risk capital shapes which inventions get made — and it almost perfectly describes what would starve clean energy.

The central finding comes from Ramana Nanda and Matthew Rhodes-Kropf. Studying startups funded across hot and cold capital markets, they find that companies first funded in "hot" markets are more likely to go bankrupt but, if they do succeed, are more valuable and more innovative — more patents, more highly-cited patents. Their interpretation is that cheap, abundant capital lowers the cost of experimentation, so investors take more shots on riskier, more novel ideas. The corollary is the part that matters here: when capital gets scarce and skittish, investors retreat to safer, more incremental bets. The riskiness of what gets invented rides up and down on the financing cycle.

A follow-up by Michael Ewens, Nanda, and Rhodes-Kropf sharpens this into something almost custom-built for our puzzle. They show that when the cost of running an experiment falls, venture capital shifts toward a "spray and pray" model — many small bets on things you can test cheaply and quickly. The flip side is the relevant one: capital flows toward innovations "where information on future prospects is revealed quickly and cheaply," and away from complex technologies "where initial experiments cost more and reveal less". Read that description again and try not to think of clean energy: a new battery chemistry or a novel solar material can take years and tens of millions of dollars before you even know if it works. When investors are reaching for cheap, fast, legible bets, that is the exact profile they avoid. Software you can validate in a quarter; a pilot plant you cannot.

Two more pieces complete the mechanism. William Kerr and Nanda, studying entrepreneurship after US banking deregulation, emphasize that creative destruction "requires many business failures along with the few great successes," and that the successes are hard to identify in advance — so a healthy innovation system has to be willing to bankroll a wide, lossy portfolio. And John Cochrane, measuring venture returns directly, documents just how violently skewed those returns are: most investments do nothing, a few go to the moon. High variance with a fat right tail is the whole business model. The binding input, then, isn't ideas — it's an investor's tolerance for variance. When that tolerance drops, the high-variance, long-fuse corner of the opportunity set is the first thing cut. Clean energy lives in that corner.

Here's a toy version that makes the logic concrete. Imagine ten clean-energy bets. Nine will fail and return nothing; one will be a 30× winner. The expected payoff per bet is 3× — a fantastic deal if you can fund all ten and wait a decade for the winner to mature. Now let risk appetite fall: capital gets pricier and more impatient, so you can fund only three bets and you need an answer within three years. The home run is probably not among your three, and even if it is, it hasn't paid off inside three years. The identical portfolio that looked like a 3× bonanza to patient, deep-pocketed capital now looks like a near-certain loss to cautious, hurried capital — so it doesn't get funded at all. Nothing about the technology changed. Only the nerve of the money did. That is the slump, in miniature.

The fix is de-risking, not just a higher price

If the diagnosis is risk appetite rather than fundamentals, then the most powerful lever isn't only making clean energy pay more at the end (a carbon price) — it's lowering the risk of the long, expensive middle, so private capital is willing to travel it.

The best evidence that this works comes from Sabrina Howell's study of US Department of Energy R&D grants to early-stage energy startups. Using the sharp ranking of grant applicants for identification, she finds that winning an early grant roughly doubles a startup's chance of later raising venture capital, with large effects on patenting and revenue — and that the effect is strongest for the most financially constrained firms. Crucially, the grants don't work as a stamp of approval; they work by paying for prototyping — that is, by buying down exactly the early technical risk that makes private investors balk. Public money here doesn't replace private money; it unlocks it. That is de-risking, measured.

The same shape shows up for cleantech firms specifically. Claudia Doblinger, Kavita Surana, and Laura Diaz Anadon, studying 657 US cleantech startups over 2008–2012, find that each additional technology alliance with a government partner is associated with about 74% more patenting, and that a licensing deal with a government organization is associated with roughly 155% more private financing. The estimates are correlational, so I hold them more loosely than Howell's quasi-experiment, but they point the same way: when a credible public partner absorbs some of the risk, private capital and inventive output follow.

This isn't an argument against carbon pricing — it's an argument that pricing alone is the wrong single tool. The directed-technical-change models that economists use to think about clean transitions reach the same conclusion from theory. Daron Acemoglu, Philippe Aghion, Leonardo Bursztyn, and David Hémous show that the optimal policy mix uses both a carbon tax and research subsidies, and that relying only on the tax — or delaying — is costly; Acemoglu, Ufuk Akcigit, Douglas Hanley, and William Kerr, estimating such a model on energy-sector data, find the optimal path "heavily relies on both" subsidies and taxes. Their subsidy is aimed at redirecting innovation; my argument is about absorbing risk so long-horizon clean bets get financed at all. These are different rationales for the same instrument — and they stack. Two reasons to subsidize clean R&D, one carbon price: the policy conclusion is robust even though the mechanisms differ.

Objection! Maybe the prize really did shrink

The strongest counterargument is that I'm under-crediting story one. Some of the post-2012 patent decline is surely genuine maturation. Solar photovoltaics and onshore wind became standardized, mass-manufactured commodities; once a technology's basic architecture settles, you get fewer patentable leaps and more incremental tweaks, and the IEA explicitly attributes part of the low-carbon patent slowdown to exactly this maturity. Cheap natural gas in the United States also blunted the economic case for some clean alternatives for a while. So the prize for some clean inventions did shrink, and a pure cold-feet story would be overclaiming. That's why the title says the slump was mostly cold feet, and why I keep the qualifier.

But maturation can't carry the whole load, for two reasons. First, maturation is gradual and technology-specific, whereas the slump was abrupt and broad, and it coincided with a financial crisis and a venture bust rather than with any particular technology's coming-of-age. Second, maturation should bite least at the frontier — the brand-new chemistries and architectures that are nowhere near commoditized — yet that capital-intensive frontier is exactly where Gaddy and colleagues find the investment collapsed hardest. A standardizing solar panel getting fewer patents is maturation; a next-generation battery startup unable to raise a Series B is cold feet. The slump has too much of the second to be explained by the first alone.

Objection! Patents are a noisy ruler

A deeper worry is the measuring stick. A patent count is a noisy proxy for innovation: firms patent strategically, the propensity to patent drifts over time, and a fall in patents need not mean a fall in useful inventing. Maybe clean innovation continued and just stopped showing up as patents.

I take this seriously, but it cuts less than it seems, because the central evidence here doesn't actually rest on the patent count. The cleantech bust is measured in dollars — money raised and lost — not in patents, and it tells the same story of capital fleeing capital-intensive clean bets. The financing-cycle mechanism is identified on venture outcomes, bankruptcies, and valuations. And the deployment facts — falling costs, rising installation — are physical, not bibliometric. When a citation-based ruler and an entirely independent dollar-based ruler point in the same direction, the most likely explanation isn't that both instruments are broken in the same way; it's that the thing they're both pointing at is real.

Bottom line

I come down on the side of cold feet, with bounded confidence. That clean-energy inventing slumped after the late 2000s is well established. That the slump was mostly a retreat of risk capital from long, expensive, uncertain bets rather than a collapse in the value of clean technology is, I'd say, what the best evidence suggests — supported by the financing-cycle mechanism (clean and well-identified), by the timing and texture of the bust (a capital event hitting capital-intensive tech), and by the independent signs that clean technology stayed valuable. It is not unambiguous: genuine maturation is a real, partial confound, and the direct "private value held up" claim leans on a working paper plus indirect evidence rather than a settled result. That the policy implication is to de-risk clean finance — not to lean on a carbon price alone — rests on firmer ground: a clean quasi-experiment (Howell) and theory that keeps concluding "price and subsidize."

The one-sentence version: clean energy didn't stop being worth inventing around 2012. The money that pays for the long, risky middle of inventing it got scared — and the cure for scared money is to make the bet less scary, not just to fatten the payoff at the end.

What we don't know

  • How big is the maturation share, really? Cleanly decomposing the post-2012 decline into "fewer opportunities" versus "less financing" is the key open question, and the honest answer is that we have suggestive shares, not a settled split.
  • Did private value hold up across all of clean energy, or only on average? The seed claim is about the aggregate; frontier deep-tech and commoditized solar may have moved in opposite directions, and the average could hide that.
  • Does de-risking scale? Howell's grants and Doblinger's alliances work at the level of individual startups. Whether public risk-absorption can be scaled to the size of the climate problem without crowding out private judgment or funding duds is unproven.
  • Has the second cleantech wave changed the verdict? Capital flooded back into climate tech in the 2020s. Whether that money again concentrates in cheap-to-test software-flavored bets and shuns hard hardware — the exact bias the mechanism predicts — is a live test of this whole story.

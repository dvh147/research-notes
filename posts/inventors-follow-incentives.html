2026-06-07 · 15 min read · economics innovation migration

In 2005, Spain introduced a tax break for inbound foreign workers so closely associated with its most famous beneficiary that everyone just calls it the "Beckham Law": move to Spain from abroad, and for several years you could pay a flat, low rate instead of the country's steep top income tax. It was sold as a way to attract executives. What it visibly attracted was footballers. And footballers, it turns out, are a near-perfect laboratory for a question that quietly haunts every finance minister and every economic-development office on earth: if you tax your most productive people too hard, do they leave?

Kleven, Landais, and Saez (2013) studied exactly this, across the top professional football leagues of 14 European countries since 1985, exploiting reforms like Spain's. Their estimate is stark. The number of foreign star players in a league responds about one-for-one to the net-of-tax rate — the slice of a marginal euro a player gets to keep. Cut the tax that top foreign talent pays, and top foreign talent shows up; raise it, and they go play somewhere else. Home-grown players are far stickier (an elasticity around 0.15), held in place by language, family, and fans. But the marginal mobile star has a price, and tax policy sets it.

Footballers are not the point. They are a clean window — their incomes are public, their location is observable, and they can't pretend to live in a tax haven while actually living in Madrid — onto a kind of person who matters enormously for long-run growth and is much harder to watch: the inventor. This post is about what happens when you point the same lens at inventors. The answer, with calibrated confidence, is the claim I want to defend:

Inventive talent is mobile and responds to incentives. Top inventors relocate in response to taxes and conditions; immigrant inventors contribute far out of proportion to their numbers; and where inventors end up has real consequences for how much gets invented. So a country's innovative capacity is not a fixed national endowment — it is, in part, a policy choice. Talent policy is innovation policy.

One scoping note before the evidence, because it is the main thing that keeps this argument honest. Almost everything below is about the location of inventive talent — moving the inventors we already have around the map — and not about the creation of new ones. Those are different problems with different answers, and quietly sliding from one to the other is how this whole story gets oversold. I'll return to it at the end, because there's a beautiful piece of evidence that draws the line exactly.

Superstar inventors have a price

Start with the paper that seeded this post. Akcigit, Baslandze, and Stantcheva (2016) assembled a panel of inventors from US and European patent offices going back to 1977, and asked how their country of residence responds to top tax rates. The clever part is the identification: changes in top rates hit inventors of different quality differently (the very top earners are the ones a top-rate change actually touches), which lets the authors separate a tax response from everything else going on in a country.

The headline: the elasticity of the number of foreign superstar inventors to the net-of-tax rate is around 1, while for domestic superstar inventors it's around 0.03.

Let me unpack "elasticity to the net-of-tax rate," because it carries most of the post. Suppose a country taxes top earners at 50%, so an inventor keeps 50 cents of the marginal dollar — that 50 cents is the "net-of-tax rate." Now cut the top rate to 40%. The inventor keeps 60 cents instead of 50 — a 20% increase in what she takes home at the margin. An elasticity of 1 means the number of foreign superstar inventors in that country rises by about 20% in response. An elasticity of 0.03 means the home-grown stars barely twitch — a 20% better take-home rate buys you less than a 1% increase in their numbers. Foreigners, already uprooted once, will move again for money. Locals mostly won't.

Two refinements matter for what follows. The response is larger for inventors employed by multinationals — unsurprising, since a multinational has somewhere to relocate them to. And an inventor is less sensitive to a country's taxes when her employer already does a lot of its research there: the work anchors the worker, not only the paycheck. Both will matter when we ask whether mobility is just rich-country poaching.

How much should you believe this? It is a large, transparent panel with a credible identification strategy, and — as we're about to see — it does not stand alone. I read it as fairly-to-very convincing for the group it actually studies: the thin, high-end, disproportionately foreign-born tier of inventors. That scoping is not a throwaway; it's the whole ballgame, and I'll keep flagging it.

It's not just inventors, and not just one country

The single most reassuring thing in this literature is how many different ways researchers have asked the question and gotten the same answer.

Stay in the United States and look across states. Moretti and Wilson (2017) track "star scientists" — the most prolific patenters — as they move between states with different tax regimes. They estimate a long-run elasticity of the number of star scientists to the net-of-tax rate of about 1.8 for personal income taxes, 1.9 for the state corporate income tax, and −1.7 for the investment tax credit. Both the tax on the worker and the tax on the firm move people.

A back-of-the-envelope makes that concrete. An elasticity of 1.8 means a state that raises its top rate enough to cut take-home pay at the margin by, say, 5% would in the long run shed on the order of 9% of its star scientists to other states. That's a lot of firepower in one tax knob — though notice the load-bearing words "long run" and "at the margin." Most scientists never move. It is the marginal, mobile few who tip the totals, which is exactly why averages can be sticky while elasticities are large.

Now stretch the time horizon to a century. Akcigit, Grigsby, Nicholas, and Stantcheva (2022) built a panel of the universe of US inventors since 1920 and a hand-collected history of state corporate taxes, and threw several identification strategies at it. Higher personal and corporate taxes reduce the quantity of innovation and shift its location — but, tellingly, they do not lower its average quality. Corporate taxes bite hardest on corporate inventors' output and their cross-state moves. This is the long-run, whole-century version of the cross-country result, and it converges with it.

Then there's the cleanest design money can buy: a sharp discontinuity. Schmidheiny and Slotwinski (2018) exploit a Swiss scheme in which a foreigner's local tax rate changes deterministically after five years of residence, and use a regression-discontinuity design to show genuinely causal tax-induced moves. Widmann (2023) zooms in on immigrant inventors specifically, using sharp top-rate differences across Swiss municipal borders, and finds the probability that an inventor settles in a given border municipality has an elasticity of about 3.2 to the local net-of-tax rate — and that where those inventors land then shapes local entrepreneurship and knowledge spillovers.

Cross-country panel; a century of US history; US states; Swiss border discontinuities; and, back in the hook, a football natural experiment. Five very different settings, five different identification strategies, one direction. By the standards of empirical economics, that mobile high-end inventors respond to taxes is about as close to well established as this field gets. This is the piece of the claim I hold most firmly.

The most mobile talent of all

There is a category of inventor for whom "mobility" isn't a marginal response to a tax change — it's the whole biography. Immigrants.

The flagship number comes from Bernstein, Diamond, Jiranaphawiboon, McQuade, and Pousada (2022), who linked immigration status to US inventor records. Immigrants are about 16% of US inventors but author 23% of patents — and that rises to 24% when you weight patents by citations and 25% when you weight them by the stock-market value created. Then comes the figure that reframes the debate: once you add the spillovers immigrants generate for their native-born collaborators, immigrants account for roughly 32% of aggregate US innovation since 1990. Close to a third. And the spillover is real, not assumed: when an immigrant inventor dies, the lasting drop in his collaborators' output (about 16%) is nearly twice what follows the death of a US-born inventor (about 9%). Immigrants aren't just numerous and productive; they are unusually strong nodes in the network through which ideas travel.

This isn't a single-study artifact. The seminal earlier estimate, Hunt and Gauthier-Loiselle (2010), used a long state panel and found that a one-percentage-point rise in the immigrant college-graduate share of the population raises patents per capita by 9 to 18% — with immigrants patenting at roughly twice the native rate, mostly because they disproportionately hold science and engineering degrees. And when Kerr and Lincoln (2010) used fluctuations in the H-1B visa cap as a natural experiment, cities and firms more dependent on the program saw bigger jumps in invention by Indian- and Chinese-named inventors when admissions rose, with native invention essentially unaffected — they can rule out displacement, and find hints of small crowding-in.

The pattern travels. Bosetti, Cattaneo, and Verdolini (2015), across 20 European countries with an instrumental-variables design, find more skilled migrants raise both patents and research citations. Crown, Faggian, and Corcoran (2020) find an Australian post-study graduate visa lifted regional patent applications. And Ferrucci and Lissoni (2019) point at why: migrant inventors are linked to higher-quality patents through the cultural diversity they add to teams — it's not only headcount, it's the mixing. That immigrant inventors over-contribute to host-country innovation is, like the tax result, supported across many countries and designs.

Does moving talent create invention, or just move it?

Here is the objection that should be nagging at you. Maybe all of this is musical chairs. If there's a roughly fixed amount of invention in the world and mobile inventors just relocate it, then one country's clever talent policy is another country's loss, and globally we've accomplished nothing. Two findings say it isn't that simple.

First, where inventors cluster changes how productive they are. Moretti (2021) documents how astonishingly concentrated invention is — the ten largest clusters hold 69% of US computer-science inventors, 77% in semiconductors, 59% in biology — and then shows that when an inventor moves to a larger cluster in her own field, her output rises in both quantity and quality, on the order of 12%. If talent is more productive when it's concentrated, then moving inventors toward where they're most productive can raise the total, not merely shuffle it. (I hold the exact magnitude loosely: a published AER Comment has since questioned the robustness of these estimates. The direction — agglomeration helps — is widely supported; the precise number is contested.)

Second, movement spreads know-how rather than just relocating a fixed stock. Bahar, Choudhury, Miguelez, and Signorelli (2024) document that "global mobile inventors" have risen more than tenfold in two decades, and that an inventor who has already patented in a technology is 70% more likely to be among the pioneering inventors of that technology once she arrives somewhere new — with returnees (who go home after a stint abroad) twice as likely again. Mobility is a vector for diffusion. Kerr and Kerr (2018) find the same diaspora-network channel operating inside multinational firms, through global collaborative patents. So even the country that "loses" an inventor isn't a pure loser, and the world isn't obviously playing a fixed-sum game.

This is the part of the argument where "it's not just redistribution" goes from assertion to evidence — but it's also where the evidence thins, so let me be careful about the objections rather than wave them away.

Objection! It's still partly zero-sum

Conceded — partly. At the level of raw headcount, a lot of inventor migration is redistribution: the inventor who leaves Bangalore for the Bay Area is, mechanically, no longer inventing in Bangalore. The agglomeration and diffusion channels above are what could make the global sum positive, and they have real evidence behind them, but the net global welfare calculation — does concentrating the world's inventors in a few clusters raise total human invention, or just the clusters' share of it? — is genuinely unsettled. I'd put it no higher than "the best evidence is suggestive that it's positive-sum." Anyone who tells you the global number with confidence is overreaching.

Objection! Immigrants crowd out natives, and firms would've invented anyway

This is the strongest counter, and it comes with the cleanest design in the whole literature. Doran, Gelber, and Isen (2022) compared firms that won versus lost H-1B visa lotteries — random assignment, the gold standard — and found that winning one additional H-1B crowds out about 1.5 other workers at the firm, with effects on the firm's own patenting that are insignificant to, at most, modest. Firms that hire H-1Bs do grow and innovate more, but mostly because they differ in other ways — they were on a different trajectory to begin with. In a word: selection.

I won't soften that, because it's true and important: "every visa buys more patents" is not supported, and the marginal H-1B at a given firm is not the same animal as the average immigrant inventor. But notice that this null and the big aggregate numbers can both be right. Kerr and Lincoln find no native invention displacement at the city and firm level; Bernstein and Hunt–Gauthier-Loiselle are measuring the contribution of the stock of immigrant inventors and their spillovers, not the causal effect of one extra lottery seat at one firm in one year. A tight firm-level null on the marginal visa is compatible with immigrants explaining a third of national innovation.

The selection point deserves its own concession. Parey, Ruhose, Waldinger, and Netz (2017) show that high-skilled German emigrants to unequal, high-return destinations like the US are strongly positively selected and concentrated in STEM — so part of the measured "immigrant contribution" reflects who chooses to move, not a treatment effect of moving. That's a real haircut on the causal interpretation. But two things resist it: the tax and mobility elasticities are behavioral responses to incentive changes, which is a causal statement about the same people before and after; and the immigrant-death spillover is hard to explain by selection alone. The over-contribution is real; the marginal-visa-to-patent link is weaker than the average makes it look. Hold both.

Objection! Taxes don't make Einsteins

Now the objection that forces the scoping note I promised at the top. If cutting taxes pulls inventors across borders, shouldn't a country just slash top rates to mint more inventors? No — and the cleanest statement of why is Bell, Chetty, Jaravel, Petkova, and Van Reenen (2019), the "Do Tax Cuts Produce More Einsteins?" lecture. Using 1.2 million inventors linked to tax records, they argue that financial incentives have limited power to raise aggregate innovation, because tax cuts mostly reward people already in the pipeline and barely move the star inventors who matter most for the total. What determines who becomes an inventor in the first place is overwhelmingly childhood exposure — mentors, role models, growing up around invention — not the after-tax prize.

Concede it completely, and then see that it doesn't dent the claim — it sharpens it. There is no contradiction between "top-tax cuts barely create new inventors" (Bell et al.) and "top-tax cuts noticeably relocate existing ones" (Akcigit et al.; Moretti and Wilson). Moving an inventor who already exists is cheap; manufacturing a new one is expensive and mostly happens in childhood. The two literatures answer different questions. This entire post is about the first — the location and allocation of the inventive talent that exists — where taxes and immigration policy bite hard. It is not about the second, where they don't. Keep those straight and the apparent paradox dissolves.

A rough sense of the stakes

Put a number on it, carefully. Immigrants account for something like 32% of US innovative output (Bernstein et al.). Suppose you're a hardened skeptic and you haircut that by half to absorb the selection and crowd-out objections above — you assume that fully half of the measured immigrant contribution would have been produced anyway by someone else. You are still left with high-skilled immigrants explaining on the order of 15% of US innovation. That residual, deliberately conservative, is larger than the plausible effect of almost any R&D-subsidy or tax-credit lever economists routinely debate. The exact counterfactual is unknowable; the order of magnitude of the stakes is not.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. Inventive talent is mobile, sensitive to incentives, and distributed across the world largely by the accident of where people are born — so a country's innovative capacity is, to a meaningful degree, a policy choice. Immigration policy and the taxation of top earners are innovation policy, whether or not anyone labels them that way.

Now the bounds, on a confidence ladder, because the pieces are not equally solid. That mobile, high-end inventors respond to taxes and conditions is well established — five independent designs across countries and a century agree. That immigrant inventors over-contribute to host-country innovation is well established — many countries, many designs. That moving talent raises aggregate rather than merely local output, through agglomeration and diffusion, is the best evidence suggests so, but hold it loosely — the key agglomeration magnitude has a published rebuttal, and the global sum is unsettled. And that talent policy is therefore a first-order innovation lever is fairly convincing, with three honest caveats: it works mostly by changing where invention happens and is partly zero-sum across borders; the marginal visa is not the average immigrant; and taxes relocate inventors but do not manufacture them.

A final discipline: mobility being real does not imply that the right tax on inventors is zero. Moretti and Wilson's own work on billionaires finds that even when 35% of affected billionaires flee a state's estate tax, the revenue raised still beats the revenue lost for the large majority of states. Elasticities are an input to tax policy, not a verdict on it. The lesson isn't "cut taxes and open the borders, QED." It's that the bill for ignoring where talent wants to be is paid in invention — and that bill is now measurable.

What we don't know

  • The global sign. Is inventor mobility positive-sum for the world (via agglomeration and diffusion) or mostly redistribution from poorer to richer places? The mechanisms exist; the net number doesn't, yet.
  • Marginal versus average. The visa-lottery null and the one-third-of-innovation aggregate need a fuller reconciliation. Which immigrants, on which margin, add the most — and how much is selection — is unresolved.
  • Retention versus attraction. Almost every clean design studies who comes. Far less is known about what keeps inventors from leaving, which is the lever a worried government actually pulls.
  • Who is mobile. The elasticities are driven by a thin, high-end, often foreign-born or multinational-employed tier. How far any of this extends to the median inventor is weakly identified.
  • Beyond patents. Nearly all of this evidence is patent-based. Whether the same holds for software, drug discovery, or science writ large is largely untested.

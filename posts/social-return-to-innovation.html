2026-08-12 · 12 min read · innovation growth

If you have ever read an argument for spending more on research, you have met this number: a dollar of research and development buys about five dollars of social value. It shows up in think-tank briefs, in congressional testimony, in the opening paragraph of grant proposals. It is the single most load-bearing statistic in innovation policy.

Here is what surprised me when I went looking for where it comes from. It is not the output of a regression. It is not an average of study estimates. It is one line of arithmetic with three inputs, and you can do it on the back of an envelope in about thirty seconds. The "$5" is not even the headline of the paper it comes from — it is a rounding of that paper's most conservative case.

That sounds like a debunking. It isn't. My claim is this: the social return to innovation really is very large — several dollars per dollar at the low end — but the reason to believe it is not the headline figure, which moves between about $4 and more than $20 depending on choices a reasonable person could make either way. The reason to believe it is that the number stays large no matter which of those choices you make, and that a completely separate body of micro-level evidence, built from different data with different methods, arrives in the same neighbourhood.

That distinction matters. A number that is precise and fragile should be quoted carefully. A number that is imprecise and robust should be quoted confidently, but as a range and an order of magnitude — never as $5.00.

The line of arithmetic

The modern version of the calculation is Benjamin Jones and Lawrence Summers (2021), in the NBER's Innovation and Public Policy volume. Their method is deliberately transparent, which is why it is worth walking through rather than citing.

They run a thought experiment. Take the US economy on its actual path. Now imagine switching off innovation investment for one year — no R&D, no new ventures — and then switching it back on. The economy does exactly the same innovating afterwards, so it reaches every future productivity level it would have reached, just one year later. The cost of that pause is one year of saved innovation spending. The benefit forgone is being permanently a bit poorer, in every future year, by one year's worth of growth.

Put those two present values side by side and almost everything cancels. What is left is:

benefit-to-cost ratio = (growth rate ÷ discount rate) ÷ (innovation spending as a share of GDP)

Three numbers. Jones and Summers plug in a long-run US per-capita growth rate of 1.8%, total public and private R&D of 2.7% of GDP, and a discount rate net of growth of 5% (that is a fairly impatient 6.8% discount rate). That gives

(0.018 / 0.05) / 0.027 = 13.3

So $13.30 of social benefit per $1 of R&D, and an implied internal social rate of return of 67% — that second figure is just the growth rate divided by the research share, 1.8 ÷ 2.7.

Notice what the calculation does not require. It does not require you to trace which project caused which productivity gain. It nets out imitation, business stealing, and congestion automatically, because it works at the level of the whole economy: if one firm's gain is another firm's loss, both are already inside the aggregate growth rate. That is the real appeal of the approach, and it is a genuine advantage over adding up project-level returns.

But it also means the answer is only as good as three macro numbers and one counterfactual. So let us push on all four.

The counterfactual is doing a lot of work

The thought experiment assumes that without innovation investment, per-capita income simply stops growing. No R&D, no growth.

This is standard growth theory — Solow says per-capita growth needs productivity gains, and endogenous growth theory says productivity gains need deliberate investment — but as a description of a one-year experiment it is heroic. Would US productivity really have been flat in 1985 if the country had skipped a year of research? Almost certainly not: there is a stock of unexploited ideas, there is diffusion from abroad, there is learning by doing on the factory floor that nobody books as R&D.

Jones and Summers know this, and their response is the honest one: the assumption is transparent, and you can adjust for the pieces you think it gets wrong. Which is what the rest of their paper does. So let us take the two biggest adjustments in each direction.

Two ways to make the number smaller

Delays. The baseline assumes the benefits arrive immediately. They don't. Jones and Summers work through the micro evidence on how long R&D takes to show up — roughly 3 to 6 years for product development, considerably longer for basic research, with citation-network evidence suggesting even remote basic research starts paying off within about 20 years. Weighting by how the US actually spends its research money (the National Science Foundation's split is about 62.5% development, 20% applied research, 17% basic), they get an average delay around 6.5 years, with 10 years as a conservative figure.

Here is what delay does to the answer:

Delay before benefits arrive Benefit per $1 Internal social rate of return
none (baseline) $13.30 67%
3 years $11.50 29%
5 years $10.40 23%
6.5 years $9.60 20%
10 years $8.10 16%
20 years $4.90 11%

The thing to take from this table is the asymmetry. The benefit-cost ratio is stubborn: even a 20-year average delay, which is far longer than the evidence supports, still leaves nearly $5 per $1. The internal rate of return is not stubborn at all — it collapses from 67% to 11%, because a high internal rate of return is precisely the thing that heavy discounting destroys. If you see someone quote "a 67% social rate of return on research," that is the no-delay number, and it is the fragile one.

Capital. The baseline credits all growth to research spending. But new ideas usually have to be built into new machines before anyone benefits: a better microprocessor design is worth nothing until microprocessors get made. If you take that embodiment seriously, the cost side has to include the investment, not just the research. Jones and Summers add net domestic private investment — which has averaged 4.0% of GDP since 1960 — to the 2.7% of GDP spent on R&D, giving total innovation costs of 6.7% of GDP. That multiplies the whole answer by 2.7/6.7, or 0.40.

If instead you think productivity gains arrive independently of new capital, the correction is gentler: multiply by one minus the capital share of income, roughly two-thirds.

Multiply those two corrections together yourself — a 20-year delay and full capital embodiment — and you land near $2 per $1. Jones and Summers do not go there; their stated floor is $4, and they would not accept stacking two worst cases that are partly substitutes for each other. But it is worth knowing where the pessimist's corner of the parameter space is, and even there, innovation roughly doubles its money.

Two ways to make it bigger

We measure growth badly, in a direction that flatters the sceptic. Price indices have a long-standing bias: they overstate inflation, which means they understate real growth. The Boskin Commission put the overstatement in the consumer price index at 1.10% a year, with a plausible range of 0.80% to 1.60%; after the measurement reforms it prompted, Robert Gordon's later estimate was about 0.65% (both figures as reported by Jones and Summers). The largest single component of that bias came from new goods and quality improvement — which is to say, from innovation itself. The benefits of innovation are systematically missing from the growth statistics we use to value innovation.

Correct for it and the numerator moves:

Assumed inflation bias Benefit per $1 Internal social rate of return
none $13.30 67%
0.40% per year $16.30 81%
0.65% per year (Gordon) $18.10 91%
1.10% per year (Boskin central) $21.50 107%
1.60% per year $25.20 126%

Health is not in GDP. US life expectancy went from 47.3 years in 1900 to 69.7 in 1960 to 78.5 in 2018. Vaccines, antibiotics, cardiovascular medicine, imaging and oncology are all outputs of research, and almost none of the value of a longer life shows up in measured output. Add international spillovers — American innovation raises living standards outside America too — and the case that the baseline understates the return is at least as strong as the case that it overstates it.

Jones and Summers land where the arithmetic leaves them: "Even under very conservative assumptions, it is difficult to find an average return below $4 per $1 spent. Accounting for health benefits, inflation bias, or international spillovers can bring the social returns to over $20 per $1 spent."

That is the honest version of the famous statistic. Not $5. Somewhere between about $4 and $20-plus, and the spread is not sampling error — it is a disagreement about what to count.

Objection: maybe research just doesn't do that much

The strongest published disagreement I found comes from Diego Comin (2004), and it deserves to be stated at full strength rather than waved at.

Comin's argument runs through free entry. If innovators can enter freely, the resources flowing into R&D have to be tied to the growth rate of technology by an equilibrium condition — and because innovators are individually small, that condition doesn't depend on how big the externalities are. Add the fact that most innovations are embodied in new capital, which bounds the size of the production externality, and he concludes that R&D contributes less than three to five tenths of a percentage point to US productivity growth. If that is right, the whole baseline collapses, because the baseline hands research the entire 1.8%. His normative conclusion is the uncomfortable one: actual US research intensity may already be socially optimal.

I do not think this settles the question, and here is why. Comin's bound is derived from a model, not measured, and it inherits that model's structure. He says so himself: the optimality conclusion holds "if the innovation technology takes the form assumed in the literature." It is one theoretical result against a convergence of several empirical ones. But it is a real result from a serious growth economist, and anyone quoting $5 per $1 should know that the proposition "we are already spending about the right amount on research" has been argued in print, in a good journal, on grounds that are internally coherent.

A different road to roughly the same place

What moves me more than any single calculation is that you can get to "the social return greatly exceeds the private return" from three directions that share almost no assumptions.

From case studies. Mansfield and coauthors (1977) went firm by firm through industrial innovations, costing out what the innovator earned and what everyone else gained. The social rates of return they computed ran well above the private ones. This is old, small-sample, hand-built work, and it is not causal in any modern sense — but it is measurement of a completely different kind than a growth-accounting identity.

From growth theory meeting the productivity regressions. Charles Jones and John Williams (1998), in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, did something clever: they derived, inside a growth model, exactly what the coefficients from the empirical productivity literature imply about the social rate of return. Their finding is that those estimates are a lower bound — the standard regressions systematically miss part of the social return. Feed a deliberately conservative social return of about 30% into that mapping, against a private return to capital of 7%, and you get optimal R&D investment at least two to four times actual investment. Note what this shares with the aggregate calculation — a growth model — and what it does not: it starts from three decades of independent productivity regressions rather than from a single balanced-growth identity.

From firm panels that separate the two spillovers. Bloom, Schankerman and Van Reenen (2013) is the cleanest identification in this literature. Any firm sits in two spaces at once: a technology space, where a rival's research helps you, and a product-market space, where a rival's research steals your customers. They measure a firm's position in both, and identify the causal effect using changes in federal and state tax incentives for research — firms that happened to face a tax change get compared with otherwise similar firms that did not. On a panel of publicly traded US firms from 1981 to 2001, technology spillovers win by a wide margin: they estimate a marginal social return of 58% against a private return of about 21%, so the social return runs two to three times the private one.

And from the other end entirely. William Nordhaus (2004) asked how much of the social surplus from innovation innovators actually keep. He measures Schumpeterian profits — profits over and above a normal return on capital — in the US non-farm business sector from 1948 to 2001, and backs out what share of the present value of innovation's social returns they represent. His answer is 2.2%, with a range of 1.3% to 3.3% across his specifications. It comes out that low because two things multiply: firms appropriate only about 7% of the gains to begin with, and those profits then erode at roughly 20% a year as imitators arrive. Innovators keep something like one fiftieth of what they create; consumers get the rest. That is a wedge measured with national accounts data rather than growth theory, and it points the same way.

Four methods, four sets of assumptions, one conclusion: the private return is a small fraction of the social return. On that proposition the evidence is pretty unambiguous. It is the specific multiple that is soft.

What the number cannot tell you

Two limits are worth being blunt about, because they are where the statistic gets misused.

It is an average, not a margin. Everything above values the whole stock of innovation spending against the whole of growth. Policy never asks that question. It asks what the next dollar buys. Jones and Summers argue the marginal return is also high, drawing on micro and macro evidence; I read that argument as suggestive rather than settled, and it is noticeably weaker than the average calculation it accompanies. It is entirely coherent to believe the average return is $10 and the marginal return on a badly designed programme is negative.

It is silent about composition. Akcigit, Hanley and Serrano-Velarde (2021) fit a growth model to French firm-level research spending from 2000 to 2006 — data that, unusually, record each firm's basic and applied research separately — and then ask inside that model what different subsidy designs would do. Their answer is that uniform research subsidies can make things worse, by over-subsidising applied research relative to basic. That is a model-based counterfactual rather than an observed policy experiment, so treat the sign as the lesson rather than the size: a large average social return is not an argument for spraying money at research in general.

There is also a live question about whether the gap has been overstated by assuming that ideas leak out for free. Arqué-Castells and Spulber (2022), in the Journal of Political Economy, build a dataset of the technology deals between publicly held US firms and let research diffuse both through unintended spillovers and through those voluntary transfers, again using changes in R&D tax incentives for identification. Counting the deals pushes the measured private return up and the measured social return down, so the distance between them narrows. Some of what we have been calling a spillover is a transaction, and transactions are already priced. It does not close the gap: for the most prominent technology providers they still find social and private returns diverging by something like 80 percentage points.

Bottom line

I believe the aggregate social return to innovation is large — comfortably several dollars of social value per dollar of research spending, and plausibly much more once health and mismeasurement are counted. I would defend "at least $4 per $1" as a conservative floor, and I would not defend any particular point estimate at all.

What earns that belief is not the arithmetic itself, which is fragile in the ordinary sense that its answer moves by a factor of five across defensible inputs. It is that the arithmetic's sign and order of magnitude survive every one of those choices, and that case studies, growth-model-corrected regressions, firm panels identified off tax changes, and Schumpeterian-profit accounting all say the same thing about the wedge between private and social returns.

The right way to use this number is as an order of magnitude with a floor: research spending is very likely to return several times its cost, we cannot say whether the multiple is 4 or 20, and the average tells you almost nothing about whether any specific programme is worth funding.

What we don't know

  • The marginal return. Almost everything here is an average. The policy-relevant quantity — what the next dollar of public research money buys — is estimated far less well, and the estimates that exist come from specific programmes that may not generalise.
  • How much growth research actually causes. Comin's bound and the Jones-Summers baseline differ by roughly a factor of four on the same underlying question. Nothing in the current evidence adjudicates that cleanly, and it is the single assumption the answer is most sensitive to.
  • How large the mismeasurement correction should be. The inflation-bias adjustment swings the answer by nearly a factor of two, and the modern digital-goods version of the problem is unresolved.
  • Whether the wedge is shrinking. If more knowledge moves through priced technology markets than we assumed, the private-social gap is smaller than the classic estimates suggest — and the policy case changes from "subsidise research" to "make markets for technology work better."

What would move me: a credible estimate of the marginal social return to an additional aggregate dollar of research spending, identified off something other than a single programme. That, rather than a better average, is the number innovation policy actually needs.

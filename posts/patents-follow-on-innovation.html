2026-06-08 · 14 min read · innovation policy

In the late 1990s two teams raced to sequence the human genome. One was the public Human Genome Project, an international consortium that poured every base pair it read into a free public database within twenty-four hours. The other was Celera Genomics, a private firm that sequenced much of the same genome and put its results behind a paid database, with contracts restricting what subscribers could do with the data. By 2001 both had largely finished. For a few years, then, the same scientific object -- a human gene -- existed under two property regimes at once: some genes were free for anyone to build on, and some were, in effect, fenced.

That accident is one of the cleanest natural experiments we have on a question at the center of innovation policy: when knowledge builds on knowledge, does owning the upstream piece help or hinder the people trying to invent the next piece? Heidi Williams collected data on exactly which genes Celera held and traced what happened to research on them. In a 2013 paper she finds that Celera's intellectual property "led to reductions in subsequent scientific research and product development on the order of 20-30 percent" -- and the drag persisted even after the genes had long entered the public domain (Williams 2013). Concretely, Celera-held genes drew about 1.2 follow-on publications by 2009 against 2.1 for comparable public genes, and ended up used in diagnostic tests at roughly half the rate.

That is a big number, and it tells a clean story. The textbook case for patents is itself a dynamic argument: grant the first inventor a temporary monopoly so she has a reason to invent at all. But when invention is cumulative -- when today's discovery is the raw material for tomorrow's -- there is an equally dynamic case on the other side. A right that lets the first inventor tax or block the second can slow the whole chain. Suzanne Scotchmer put the problem crisply three decades ago: the social task is to divide the reward between the pioneer and the improver so that both have a reason to work, and a patent broad enough to fully reward the pioneer may be broad enough to deter the improver (Scotchmer 1991). James Bessen and Eric Maskin pushed the point harder: when innovators build on one another and each takes a different research line, stronger patents can lower total innovation, and firms may end up better off -- even in their own profits -- in a world with more imitation (Bessen & Maskin 2009).

Here is the tension in one toy example. Picture a foundational discovery -- a gene, a software method, a research tool -- that is worth nothing sold on its own but that unlocks a follow-on product worth 100 dollars that somebody else will invent. If the pioneer holds a broad patent, the follow-on inventor has to license it first. When the two can strike a deal -- split the surplus, say -- both invest and the product gets made. But if bargaining is slow, costly, or breaks down (the pioneer holds out for too much; or there are twenty pioneers to negotiate with at once), the product never appears. The patent didn't reward invention. It taxed the invention that would have built on it.

So the theory is genuinely two-handed, which makes this an empirical question. After reading the evidence, here is where I land: upstream IP does slow follow-on innovation, but mainly when it restricts access -- to data, to materials, or through a thicket of rights too costly to license -- and the drag largely vanishes where the underlying knowledge stays open. The word doing the work is access, not patent. Let me lay out the pieces.

The gene-patent puzzle, and what resolves it

Start with the crux, because it nearly sank the whole claim. Williams found a 20-30 percent drag on the genes Celera fenced. But a second study, on the very same genes, found almost nothing. Bhaven Sampat and Heidi Williams linked the exact gene sequences claimed in more than a hundred thousand patent applications to later research, and used two quasi-experiments -- comparing granted applications to rejected ones, and exploiting the luck of which patent examiner you happen to draw -- to ask whether actually holding a gene patent reduced follow-on work. Their answer: "on average gene patents have had no quantitatively important effect on follow-on innovation" (Sampat & Williams 2019). And the null is precise, not merely underpowered: for follow-on publications and clinical trials they can statistically reject effects -- in either direction -- larger than about 6 percent.

How can both be true? Because they are not measuring the same thing. Williams's "IP" was not a gene patent at all. It was Celera's database -- a contractual, trade-secret-like restriction on access to sequence data the public project had not yet released. Sampat and Williams's IP was the formal gene patent, which by the time it issues sits on top of a sequence already deposited in a public database for anyone to read. The first regime fences the input. The second fences a use while leaving the input open. The follow-on penalty showed up only when the input itself was hard to get -- a reconciliation the authors themselves reach. Once you see it, the contradiction dissolves, and it points straight at access as the operative margin. The genome, it turns out, was patented heavily -- one survey found around a fifth of human genes claimed in a US patent (Jensen & Murray 2005) -- and yet those patents, sitting over public data, left follow-on research essentially untouched.

The cleanest experiment: when a court erases a patent

The genome story is vivid but particular. For something closer to a controlled trial, Alberto Galasso and Mark Schankerman found one inside the legal system. When a patent's validity is challenged at the US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, the case is heard by a three-judge panel drawn essentially at random from the court's roster -- and judges differ enormously in how readily they invalidate, from about a quarter of the time to over three-quarters. That lottery lets you ask a clean causal question: take a patent, erase it by court order, and does follow-on innovation on it speed up? Measuring follow-on by later citations to the focal patent, they find that invalidation produces a 50 percent increase, on average (Galasso & Schankerman 2015).

Fifty percent sounds like a verdict that patents broadly block the next inventor. They don't -- and the texture of the result is the whole point. For most invalidated patents the effect is statistically zero. The average is carried by a minority: patents held by large firms, in complex technologies -- computers, electronics, medical instruments -- where a single product stacks together many patented components. In drugs, chemicals, and mechanical technologies, where a product tends to map to one clean patent, knocking it out changes nothing. And the entire effect runs in one direction across the size gap: invalidating a big firm's patent unleashes follow-on work by small firms. Where the technology is simple, or one motivated party can strike one license, the patent blocks nothing.

That is exactly what the toy example predicts. Patents bite when bargaining fails -- when there are too many rights-holders, or too much holdup between a big incumbent and a small entrant, for access to be cheaply bought -- and not otherwise. The same authors had earlier shown that fragmented patent ownership gums up the market for ideas more generally, with disputes harder to clear when rights are scattered (Galasso & Schankerman 2010). The blocking is a tail phenomenon, and the tail is precisely the high-friction, low-access corner of the technology space.

Forcing the door open

If restricting access slows follow-on, forcing access open should speed it. Three natural experiments, reaching across a century, say it does.

The bluntest tool is compulsory licensing -- the state stripping or opening a patent by fiat. During World War I the United States seized German-owned patents under the Trading with the Enemy Act and licensed them to American firms, heavily in organic chemistry. Petra Moser and Alessandra Voena compared the technology subclasses that got a license to those that did not, before and after, across roughly 130,000 chemical inventions, and find that compulsory licensing raised US domestic invention in the treated subclasses by about 20 percent -- with a clean placebo check that French inventors, who could not license the seized patents, show no such jump (Moser & Voena 2012). Four decades later, a 1956 antitrust consent decree forced AT&T's Bell System to license its entire portfolio of roughly 7,800 patents royalty-free. Martin Watzinger and coauthors, using closely matched non-Bell patents as a control, find the decree produced a lasting roughly 17 percent increase in follow-on innovation building on Bell's patents, driven mostly by young and small companies (Watzinger et al. 2020). But -- and this nuance is load-bearing -- the boost appeared only outside telecommunications. Inside telecom, where Bell remained a vertically integrated monopolist, opening the patents did nothing. Freeing the IP speeds follow-on only where the downstream product market is also contestable. Open access to the idea is necessary; it is not sufficient.

The third experiment is about research tools rather than patents per se, and it is the constructive flip side of the whole story. Jeffrey Furman and Scott Stern studied biological resource centers -- think a certified public library of cell lines, microbes, and other living materials that any lab can order. Depositing a discovery in one of these centers, which lowers the cost for everyone else to get and use it, raises the discovery's later follow-on citations by somewhere between 50 and 125 percent (Furman & Stern 2011). And openness does more than raise the volume of follow-on work; it changes its character. When a set of late-1990s NIH agreements freed academic researchers to use certain patented genetically engineered mice on cheap, unencumbered terms, Fiona Murray and coauthors find the result was not just more follow-on research but markedly more diverse follow-on -- new researchers entering, and a wider range of experimental directions explored -- with no drop in the creation of new mouse strains (Murray et al. 2016). Their phrase for the cost of strong upstream IP is precise: not less exploitation of existing lines, but less exploration of new ones.

Objection! Patents also disclose

The strongest objection to all of this is that I have described a patent as only a fence, when it is also a publication. To get the monopoly, an inventor must teach the world how the invention works, in print, on a public registry. That disclosure is a gift to the next inventor -- it is how the patent system is supposed to grease cumulative innovation.

And the gift is real. Deepak Hegde, Kyle Herkenhoff, and Chenqi Zhu exploit a 1999 reform that made US patent applications publish about eighteen months after filing instead of only at grant, and compare affected US patents to their "twin" European patents. Earlier disclosure produced more and faster follow-on citations, fewer abandoned and duplicative projects, and -- among firms facing longer grant delays -- a 4 percent increase in R&D once their rivals' disclosures arrived sooner (Hegde, Herkenhoff & Zhu 2023). So the patent system has a genuinely pro-cumulative channel built into it: it converts secrets into public knowledge on a schedule.

Concede the point fully -- and then notice what it is. It is a force that improves access, to the knowledge, earlier. It does not rescue the exclusion right; it shows the other half of the same system speeding follow-on by opening information. A patent is, at the same moment, a tax (you cannot use the protected invention) and a subsidy (you can read how it works) on the next inventor. Which one dominates depends on what the follow-on inventor actually needs. If she needs the information -- the design, the method, the result -- disclosure tends to win, and the patent may be a net help. If she needs access to the protected input itself -- the gene data, the research mouse, the one component her product cannot route around -- exclusion bites. Gene databases, materials, fragmented component stacks: those are inputs. The access spine holds, and the disclosure result actually sits on top of it.

Objection! Why not just license it?

There is a second, more theoretical objection, and it is the ghost of Ronald Coase. If a patent stands between a willing improver and a valuable follow-on product, both sides have every incentive to make a deal; the surplus is right there to be split. In a frictionless world, upstream rights would never block value-increasing follow-on -- they would only redistribute who gets paid. So why do we see any blocking at all?

Because bargaining is not frictionless, and the data show its fingerprints. The Galasso-Schankerman pattern -- blocking concentrated in exactly the large-holder, small-entrant, many-component situations -- is the signature of failed bargaining: high transaction costs, asymmetric information, holdup. Two decades ago Michael Heller and Rebecca Eisenberg warned that biomedical research might choke on a "tragedy of the anticommons," too many fragmented upstream rights, each owner able to say no, adding up to underuse of the very discoveries everyone needs (Heller & Eisenberg 1998). In practice the choke has been gentler than feared. Surveys by John Walsh, Wesley Cohen, and Charlene Cho find that academic biomedical researchers are rarely stopped by patents -- they license, invent around, or quietly use the technology -- but that they are routinely slowed by restricted access to materials and data, which is a different lever entirely (Walsh, Cohen & Cho 2007). And Fiona Murray and Scott Stern, tracking pairs where the same discovery is both published as a paper and claimed in a patent, find a real but modest "anticommons tax": once the patent issues, citations to the paired paper fall by roughly 9 to 17 percent (Murray & Stern 2007). The friction is real; it is usually a tax rather than a wall; and, once again, it operates through access.

Bottom line

Read together, the evidence supports a bounded claim. Upstream intellectual property can slow the innovation that builds on it, and in the right setting the effect is large: a 20-30 percent drag on follow-on genomics research when the data were fenced; a 50 percent jump in follow-on when a blocking patent is struck down. But it is not a general law that patents retard cumulative innovation. The drag concentrates where rights restrict access to a needed input -- proprietary data, physical research materials, or a stack of components owned by large firms and too costly to license. Where the input stays open -- where a patent merely protects a use while the underlying knowledge sits in a public database, or where one motivated party can strike one clean license -- follow-on barely moves, and the patent's mandatory disclosure may even help. The most honest one-line version is: it's the access, not the patent.

That carries a clean policy reading, and it is one economists from Scotchmer onward have urged: treat the upstream and the downstream differently. For foundational discoveries and research tools -- the inputs that everything else builds on -- lean toward openness: public databases, research exemptions, materials repositories, early disclosure, even compulsory licensing where a monopolist is foreclosing a whole field. For downstream products, the ordinary patent bargain does less cumulative damage, because there is less standing on top of them. I hold this with fair confidence for biomedicine and genomics, where the cleanest studies live, and more tentatively elsewhere.

What we don't know

  • External validity. Almost all the sharp evidence is biomedical (genes, mice, cell lines, drugs) or historical (wartime chemicals, mid-century telecom). The frontier today is software, semiconductors, and AI models and datasets -- where the crucial "input" (training data, model weights, foundation models) looks much more like Celera's fenced database than like a single drug molecule, and where we have far less clean causal evidence. Bessen and Maskin's software story is suggestive, not settled.
  • Counts versus welfare. Nearly everything here is measured in follow-on citations, publications, or patents. Those are inputs to innovation, not its value to people. A 50 percent jump in citations after invalidation is not a 50 percent gain in welfare, and we rarely know the exchange rate.
  • The pioneer's incentive. These studies watch follow-on after a discovery exists. They mostly cannot tell us whether weakening upstream rights would have stopped the foundational discovery from being made in the first place. The access argument says the foundational layer is special -- but if openness there dries up the incentive to build the foundation, the calculus shifts.
  • Can the frictions be engineered away? Patent pools, clearinghouses, and standardized licenses are all attempts to lower the transaction costs that make access expensive. We have surprisingly little clean evidence on how well they actually work.

A patent on an upstream idea is a tollbooth on a road that other inventors need to travel. Sometimes the toll is trivial and the road stays busy. Sometimes -- when the booth sits on a chokepoint, when there are twenty booths in a row, when a small traveler is turned back while a big one waves through its own toll -- traffic backs up for years. Most of the empirical work of the last decade has been the patient project of finding out which roads are which. The answer, so far, is that the toll bites when it blocks access, and mostly waves traffic through when it doesn't.

2026-06-10 · 13 min read · science-of-science innovation

Here is an uncomfortable fact about the way science accumulates. When economists Marta Serra-Garcia and Uri Gneezy (2021) lined up published experiments that had been put through formal replication tests, they found that the papers that failed to replicate were cited more than the papers that held up — on the order of sixteen extra citations a year, and in the very top journals (Nature and Science) the non-replicable papers were cited several times as often as the replicable ones. Worse, the gap didn't close once the failed replication was published. In their data, only about one in eight of the citations that came after a public replication failure so much as mentioned that the original result hadn't held.

Sit with that. A finding can be wrong, be shown to be wrong, and keep being cited as though nothing happened. The error doesn't get quarantined. It keeps circulating, and the work that leans on it inherits the mistake.

This post argues one claim: a wrong scientific result is not a private problem for the people who produced it. Its cost radiates outward — to the author's other papers, to their collaborators, and to the surrounding field — and it radiates forward in time, because the record self-corrects slowly and incompletely. Put those two together and you get the framing I'll defend at the end: research reliability is a public good. Like clean air or a stable currency, everyone who builds on the literature benefits when it's trustworthy, nobody who cuts a corner pays the full downstream cost, and so — predictably — we get less reliability than we'd collectively pay for.

Let me build it up in rings, starting closest to the error and moving out, then add the time dimension, and then deal with the two objections that a careful reader should already be forming.

Ring one: your own other work

Start with a retraction — a journal formally withdrawing a paper it published. The obvious casualty is the retracted paper itself. But it turns out the damage doesn't stop at the paper's edge; it bleeds into everything else the author has written.

Susan Feng Lu, Ginger Jin, Brian Uzzi, and Benjamin Jones (2013) tracked 1,400-plus retractions through the Web of Science and asked what happened to each retracted author's earlier, non-retracted papers. Their design is a matched-control comparison: line up each prior paper against near-identical papers that never had a retraction in their lineage, and watch the citation paths diverge after the retraction date. The earlier work takes a hit of roughly 7% of its annual citations — and the penalty grows, from about 4.7% in the first two years to 12.5% a year once five years have passed. It reaches papers published a decade before the offending one, and it travels out to four "degrees of separation" in the citation network. One bad paper, in other words, quietly devalues a scientist's entire back catalogue, and it does so more as time goes on, not less.

Pierre Azoulay, Alessandro Bonatti, and Joshua Krieger (2017) — one of the two papers that seeded this post — find the same pattern in biomedicine and sharpen it. Using a difference-in-differences design (comparing retracted authors' pre-retraction papers to those of matched control authors from the same journals, before versus after the event), they estimate that a retraction lowers citations to the author's other work by about 11%. And the size of the blow depends on why the paper was withdrawn: retractions for misconduct cost roughly 18%, retractions for honest mistakes only about 8%. The community is doing something like Bayesian updating about the person: fraud says "distrust everything this lab touched," an honest error says "be a bit more careful."

Two details make this more than a morality tale. First, the penalty is permanent and widening, not a transient embarrassment. Second — from Lu et al. — when authors self-report the problem rather than getting caught, the spillover onto their prior work essentially vanishes. The system punishes concealment, not error per se. I read this evidence as fairly convincing on its own terms: several independent matched designs, consistent direction, sensible gradients. The main caveat is that it's measured almost entirely in the life sciences, a point I'll keep flagging.

Ring two: your collaborators

Now step one ring out, to people who didn't write the flawed paper at all.

Katrin Hussinger and Maikel Pellens (2019), in the post's most quietly damning result, follow the prior collaborators of researchers found guilty of misconduct by the U.S. Office of Research Integrity. These are co-authors from earlier projects — and the authors go out of their way to strip out anyone who worked on the fraudulent papers, and to drop every paper actually co-written with the fraudster. What's left is pure association: you once published with someone who later turned out to have cheated, on unrelated work. That alone costs you about 9% of your citation rate, an effect that shows up the moment the investigation becomes known and persists for years.

Why does merely knowing a fraudster tax your reputation? Hussinger and Pellens make a clean argument that the mechanism is stigma, not lost productivity: the collaborators' publication output doesn't fall, so this isn't demoralized people writing less. It's the community quietly marking down work that was already on the shelf. The damage is also regressive. Sorted by the collaborator's own standing, the lowest-reputation quartile loses about a third of its citations while the top quartile is statistically untouched — and the spillover is driven by cases where the fraudster was himself low-status. Being eminent is a kind of insurance; being junior is the opposite.

That asymmetry isn't a fluke of one study. Ginger Jin, Benjamin Jones, Susan Feng Lu, and Brian Uzzi (2019) call it a reverse Matthew effect. The ordinary "Matthew effect" in science is that credit flows to the already-famous (to those who have, more is given). Retraction runs it backwards: when a team paper is pulled, the citation penalty to the team's prior work lands on the junior members — an average author loses about 11 percentage points of annual citations, an effect that shrinks by roughly 3 points for every standard deviation of seniority, until for the most eminent coauthors it disappears. The big name on the paper walks away clean; the postdoc eats the loss. So the cost of one error is not only spread across innocent people — it's distributed in the least fair way imaginable.

Ring three: the neighborhood

The outermost ring is the whole intellectual area around the error — including work by people with no personal tie to the author at all.

This is the contribution of Pierre Azoulay, Jeffrey Furman, Joshua Krieger, and Fiona Murray (2015), in a paper confusingly titled, simply, "Retractions." (It's in the Review of Economics and Statistics, not — as even careful readers sometimes assume — in a science-policy journal.) They identify the papers that are intellectual neighbors of a retracted article — related prior work in the same niche — and compare their citation trajectories to matched controls when a nearby paper blows up. The neighbors take a lasting hit of 5 to 10% of their citation rate. And it's not just citations: after a retraction, fewer new papers and less funding flow into the affected area. A single discredited result can put a chill on an entire line of inquiry, deterring exactly the follow-on work that might have sorted the wheat from the chaff.

Here the authors are admirably honest about interpretation, and so should I be. A 5-10% decline in the neighborhood is consistent with two very different stories. One is reputational contagion: scientists avoiding a "tainted" topic to protect their own standing — a genuine distortion. The other is sober learning: if a field's foundational result was fake, maybe the surrounding work really was built on sand and should be discounted. The data can't fully separate these, which is why I'll treat the welfare interpretation of this ring more cautiously than its existence.

The error has a long half-life

So far the rings are about space — who near the error pays. The other dimension is time, and it's where the public-good problem really bites, because the obvious rejoinder — "fine, but surely the system eventually cleans this up" — turns out to be largely false.

Retracted papers don't die. Tzu-Kun Hsiao and Jodi Schneider (2022) read the actual sentences in which biomedical papers cite retracted work, and found that of more than 13,000 citations made after a retraction, only 5.4% acknowledged that the cited paper had been retracted. Ninety-five percent of the time, the citing author either didn't know or didn't say — and the way the papers were cited barely changed before versus after the retraction. The literature keeps treating withdrawn results as live knowledge. A recent survival analysis of 25,480 retractions by Marc Joëts and Valérie Mignon (2026) puts numbers on the lag: it takes on average around three years just to issue a retraction, a sliver of papers persist as "zombies" that keep getting cited indefinitely, and the citations to a retracted paper take something like 1,200 days in the quantitative sciences — and over 2,000 in the social sciences and humanities — to fall by half. Tellingly, the more serious the misconduct, the longer the retraction takes to arrive.

The canonical case makes it vivid. Andrew Wakefield's 1998 Lancet paper linking the MMR vaccine to autism is probably the most thoroughly discredited paper in modern medicine — partially retracted in 2004, fully retracted in 2010. Yet when Ivan Heibi and Silvio Peroni (2021) mapped its citations, they found that a majority — about 54% — arrived after the full 2010 retraction, and that as late as 2015 only a third of citing papers even mentioned that it had been retracted. I want to be fair about this one: most of those later citations were critical — only about 2% were positive. So Wakefield is less "people still believe it" and more "the paper refuses to leave the conversation, and many who invoke it don't flag that it's been pulled." That's still a cost. Every one of those papers spends words relitigating a settled error, and every reader who doesn't follow the footnote carefully comes away muddier than they should.

Put the space and time dimensions together and the picture is bleak in a specific way: the bad result keeps getting cited for years, while the good work next to it gets discounted immediately. The correction is both too slow where it should be fast and too fast where it should be careful.

It isn't just fraud

You might think all of this is about a rare, lurid event — outright fraud, the stuff of news stories. It isn't, and this is the step that turns a collection of retraction studies into a claim about the literature as a whole. The retracted papers are just the cases dramatic enough to trigger the immune system. The much larger population is ordinary published work that is simply wrong — and we now have a decent sense of how big that population is.

When researchers run large, pre-registered replication audits — independently re-running published studies at high statistical power — a sobering fraction don't hold up. The Open Science Collaboration (2015) replicated 100 psychology studies and got a significant result in the same direction only 36% of the time, with effect sizes about half the originals. Economics does better but not well: Camerer and colleagues (2016) replicated 11 of 18 lab experiments from the AER and QJE (61%), and a follow-up (2018) replicated 13 of 21 social-science experiments from Nature and Science (62%), again with surviving effects roughly halved. And the failures are partly predictable: in the 2018 study, prediction markets of fellow researchers could pick out in advance which results would fall. That means the fragility was visible in the original work — this is structural, not bad luck.

Even setting aside outright non-replication, the published record is bent before anyone cheats. Abel Brodeur and coauthors (2016) collected some 50,000 test statistics from the top economics journals and found a "two-humped camel": a suspicious dearth of results just shy of statistical significance and a pile-up just past it, with on the order of 10-20% of marginally significant findings looking misallocated — nudged across the line by specification search (trying variants until one clears the bar) on top of journals' taste for significant results. A later study (2020) shows this varies by method — worst for instrumental variables, least for randomized trials and regression discontinuities — and, depressingly, that it isn't cured by publishing in a top-five journal, by the peer-review revision process, or by the passage of time. None of this is fraud. It's the ordinary machinery of careers and publication, and John Ioannidis's (2005) famous argument explains why it's enough: when you chase weak effects, with flexible analyses, in crowded fields, a "significant" result is often more likely false than true — no villain required. As Garret Christensen and Edward Miguel (2018) put it in their survey of the credibility of economics, publication bias, non-replication, and specification search "remain widespread in the discipline."

So the contamination channels of the previous sections don't apply to a handful of scandals. They apply to a meaningful slice of normal science.

Objection! Isn't this just efficient pruning?

The sharpest objection is that I've described a feature, not a bug. If a result is false, society should stop building on it. The citation losses to descendants are the system working: bad ideas lose support, as they ought to. Where's the externality?

Two things break the "efficient pruning" story. First, look at who the discount falls on. Rings two and three land on work that was never retracted and may be perfectly sound — innocent collaborators, neighboring papers, an entire subfield's funding. If a fraud in lab A causes citations to lab B's unrelated, valid work to drop 9%, that is not the market correctly devaluing a false idea; it's collateral damage to a true one. Efficient pruning cuts the rotten branch; what we see also nicks the healthy ones nearby.

Second, the timing is exactly backwards from efficient. Genuine pruning would hit the false result fast and spare everything else. Instead we get under-correction on the thing that's actually wrong — zombie papers cited for a decade, 95% of post-retraction citations failing to note the retraction, non-replicable findings out-cited their replicable peers — running simultaneously with over-correction on the innocent neighbors. A pruning shears that cut slowly through the dead wood and quickly through the living would not be called efficient. So I'll concede the kernel — some of the descendant citation loss is the system rightly abandoning a bad idea — while insisting it can't account for the regressive incidence or the long half-life.

Objection! Maybe the authors were just weak

A second objection: perhaps "spillover" is an illusion of selection. Maybe people who commit fraud, and the people around them, were doing shaky work all along, and the retraction just reveals what the community would have discovered anyway. The citation drop wouldn't then be caused by the error; it would be a delayed reaction to pre-existing weakness.

This is exactly what the matched difference-in-differences designs are built to rule out, and they handle it well. The treated papers track their matched controls flat before the retraction and diverge sharply at the event — not a slow pre-existing slide. The fraud-versus-honest-mistake gradient, the self-reporting effect, and the finding that collaborators' output is unchanged while their citations fall all point to a reputational shock at the moment of revelation, not to latent quality finally surfacing. I find the causal reading convincing for the existence of a spillover. What remains genuinely unsettled — and Azoulay and coauthors say so themselves — is how much of the neighborhood effect is correct Bayesian updating ("that foundation was shaky, discount it") versus excess stigma ("avoid the cursed topic"). That distinction matters enormously for welfare, and the current evidence doesn't pin it down.

Reliability is a public good

Step back, and the economics writes itself. A reliable result is non-rival (my building on it doesn't use it up) and effectively non-excludable (once published, anyone can rely on it). Those are the defining marks of a public good. The mirror image is what this whole post has documented: the cost of an unreliable result is an externality. The author captures the private benefit of a flashy finding; the downstream costs — to collaborators, neighbors, and everyone who later builds on it — are borne by others, and mostly later. No individual doing the cost-benefit calculation on cutting a corner internalizes the 9% their old co-authors will lose, or the decade of zombie citations, or the chilled subfield. So we get the textbook result for public goods: reliability is under-provided.

A rough back-of-the-envelope shows the scale of the wedge — illustrative, not precise. Take one retracted paper by an author with the sample-average of roughly 25 prior papers and, say, a dozen past collaborators. The author's own back catalogue sheds on the order of 7% of its citations; a dozen collaborators shed ~9% each; the surrounding neighborhood of related papers loses 5-10%. The single withdrawn paper is one object; the citation damage is smeared across dozens of others, the large majority of which did nothing wrong. Now remember the base rate: most "wrong" papers are never retracted at all — they're just the ~30-40% that quietly wouldn't replicate. The retraction studies measure the visible tip; the contamination of the literature is the iceberg.

If reliability is an under-provided public good, the remedy is the usual one: stop treating verification as a private chore and fund it as shared infrastructure. The credibility-reform agenda Christensen and Miguel survey — pre-registration, pre-analysis plans, mandatory data and code sharing, registered reports that are accepted before the results are known — is exactly an attempt to make the production of reliability less privately costly and its absence harder to hide. The persistence studies suggest a humbler complement: make retraction propagate. If 95% of post-retraction citations don't flag the retraction, then wiring retraction status into reference managers and databases is low-hanging fruit.

Bottom line

I'm fairly confident of the core claim: a wrong result imposes real, measurable costs on the work around it — the author's other papers, their collaborators, and their field — and those costs persist for years because the record corrects slowly and unevenly. That conclusion rests on several independent, well-identified studies that agree in direction and roughly in size, backed by large bibliometric audits of how retracted and non-replicable work keeps getting cited. The public-good framing follows almost mechanically once you accept the spillovers are real.

Two honest bounds. First, almost all the spillover magnitudes come from biomedicine and the broader life sciences; the cleanest economics evidence here is on the base rate of unreliable work, not on how far its contamination spreads. Whether a retraction in economics chills its neighborhood by 5% or 15% — or barely at all, given a smaller and differently-organized field — is genuinely open. Second, I won't claim a signed welfare number. Some of the downstream citation loss is the system rightly abandoning a bad idea; some is collateral damage and stigma. Until we can separate correct updating from excess avoidance, "reliability is under-provided" is a strong qualitative conclusion, not a quantitative one.

What we don't know

  • Does economics behave like biology? The spillover estimates need to be rerun in fields with different team structures, citation cultures, and retraction norms.
  • How much of ring three is right? Splitting the neighborhood effect into "correct re-evaluation" and "excess stigma" is the single most valuable thing a future study could do, because it decides the welfare sign.
  • Do the reforms actually reduce contamination? Registered reports and open data are spreading; we don't yet know whether they shrink the rings or just relocate them.
  • Can we kill zombies? If retraction metadata were piped into every database and reference manager, would the 5% acknowledgment rate jump — and would the zombies finally die?

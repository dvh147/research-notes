Here is one of the more deflating numbers in the economics of innovation. The Yale economist William Nordhaus once tried to add up, for the whole U.S. economy from 1948 to 2001, how much of the value created by innovation was actually captured by the innovators themselves - the firms and inventors who did the work and carried the risk. His answer was about 2.2%. For every hundred dollars of value that new and improved products delivered to society, the people who created them kept a little over two dollars. The other ninety-eight or so flowed to everyone else: to consumers paying less for better things, and to rivals and successors who built on the idea (Nordhaus 2004).

That number should make you suspicious of a habit we all have. We tend to read an invention's commercial success - its profits, its founders' fortunes, its company's market value - as a measure of how much good it did. The blockbuster drug, the hit gadget, the unicorn startup: surely these are the ones that mattered most.

The claim of this post is that the reading is unreliable. What an invention earns its owner and what it is worth to society are only loosely linked. They are positively related - valuable-to-society inventions do tend to make more money than worthless ones - but the link is loose enough, and the inventor's slice small enough, that the market's reward signal is a poor guide to social value. The practical consequence is the one Nordhaus's number hints at: leave innovation entirely to private incentives and you get systematically too little of it, concentrated in the wrong places.

I want to be careful, because "loose" is doing real work here. This is not the cartoon claim that profit and social value are unrelated, or opposed. They move together. The argument is about how loosely they move together, and about a gap large enough to matter for policy. Let me build it up in pieces.

Two yardsticks for the same invention

Start by separating the two things we're measuring.

The private value of an invention is what its owner can capture: the extra profit, the licensing income, the bump in the firm's stock-market value. It is bounded by appropriability - how much of the value you create you can actually fence off and charge for before it leaks to others.

The social value is the whole pie: the private value plus everything that leaks out. Economists usually split the leakage into two streams. The first is consumer surplus - buyers getting more than they pay for, because competition and imitation push the price below what the thing is worth to them. The second is knowledge spillovers - other inventors learning from your work and standing on it to make their own advances, without cutting you a check.

A toy example fixes the idea. Suppose a firm spends to develop a better battery and, over the patent's life, earns $100 million in extra profit from it. Suppose the cheaper, longer-lasting batteries are worth $400 million to the consumers who buy them above what they pay, and suppose the chemistry the firm published teaches three other labs something that lets them make $200 million of their own advances. Then the private value is $100 million; the social value is $700 million. The inventor captured about one dollar in seven of the value it set in motion - and the $100 million it kept tells you almost nothing about the $600 million it didn't.

The question is whether the real world looks like that example. Two facts have to hold: the gap between social and private value has to be big, and the size of an invention's private slice has to be a noisy indicator of the whole pie. Both, it turns out, have a fair amount of evidence behind them.

The gap is real, and it is big

The cleanest old evidence comes from a 1977 study by Edwin Mansfield and colleagues, who did something laborious: they took seventeen specific industrial innovations and, with the cooperation of the firms involved, estimated both the private return the innovator earned and the social return once you counted savings to users and to the rest of the economy (Mansfield et al. 1977). The median social rate of return was 56%; the median private rate of return was 25% - social returns ran about double private ones, and the average gap between the two was 47 percentage points. The detail I find most telling: in roughly 30% of the cases, the private return was so low that no firm, even with hindsight, should rationally have made the investment - yet the social return was high enough that society was glad they did. Those are inventions a purely private calculus would have killed.

Mansfield's sample was tiny and hand-built, so on its own it is suggestive, not decisive. But the same pattern shows up in modern work using completely different methods. The anchor here is Nicholas Bloom, Mark Schankerman, and John Van Reenen's 2013 paper in Econometrica, which is the paper that taught the field how to measure this carefully (Bloom, Schankerman & Van Reenen 2013). Their insight is that a firm's R&D casts two shadows on other firms at once. There is a positive technology spillover: firms working on similar problems benefit from each other's discoveries. And there is a negative business-stealing effect: when a product-market rival innovates, it takes your customers. The two had always been tangled together. Bloom and coauthors untangle them by placing each firm in two different spaces - a technology space (who works on similar science) and a product-market space (who sells to the same customers) - and using changes in R&D tax incentives to pin down the causal effect.

The headline: the positive spillover dominates the negative business-stealing, so the gross social returns to R&D are at least twice the private returns - their point estimates put the marginal social return near 60% against a private return near 20%. Business-stealing is real but smaller; netting it out shaves only about ten percentage points off the gap. A follow-up extending the data to 2015 finds the wedge if anything wider, with the ratio of social to private returns rising to about four (Lucking, Bloom & Van Reenen 2018).

Translate that into the language of "how much R&D should we be doing." If the social return to research is several times the private return, then the amount of research that is privately worth doing is far below the amount that is socially worth doing. Charles Jones and John Williams put a number on it in 1998: mapping the return estimates into a growth model, they conclude that optimal R&D investment is at least two to four times actual investment, and that this is a conservative lower bound (Jones & Williams 1998). A more recent back-of-the-envelope by Benjamin Jones and Lawrence Summers comes at it from the macro side and lands in the same territory: even under deliberately cautious assumptions, each dollar spent on R&D returns at least about four dollars of social value, and plausibly much more (Jones & Summers 2020).

So the first fact is on solid ground. Across hand-built case studies, firm panels, and growth accounting - three methods with very different weaknesses - the social value of inventive activity comes out as a large multiple of the private value. This part of the claim is, I think, well established.

Why the inventor keeps so little

The reason is older than any of these papers, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the engine under everything else. Knowledge is non-rival (your using an idea doesn't stop me using it) and only partly excludable (it is hard to stop people from learning it). Kenneth Arrow laid this out in a 1962 essay: because an inventor cannot charge everyone who benefits, the inventor captures less than the value created and therefore invests less than would be ideal. Patents exist precisely to claw back some excludability - but they are leaky and temporary by design, which is why Nordhaus's 2.2% is small rather than zero. He estimates that the instantaneous share an innovator can appropriate is only about 7%, and competition erodes even that over time.

The mechanism matters for what comes next. If inventors captured, say, 80% of the value they created, the private reward would be a fairly faithful (if scaled-down) image of social value, and you could mostly trust the market's verdict. It is precisely because the captured share is small that the private signal has room to come apart from the social one - which is the second, and more contestable, half of the claim.

Down at the level of a single invention, the link gets loose

Everything above is about averages: R&D in general earns far more for society than for the firm. But my claim is sharper and more uncomfortable - that even the ranking of inventions by private value is a shaky guide to their ranking by social value. Here the evidence is good but more mixed, so I'll calibrate as I go.

The natural place to look is patents, where we can sometimes measure both a private-value proxy (the stock market's reaction to a patent) and a social-value proxy (how much later inventors cite it). Leonid Kogan, Dimitris Papanikolaou, Amit Seru, and Noah Stoffman built exactly this, estimating the dollar value of tens of thousands of patents from the stock-price jump when they were granted (Kogan et al. 2017). Their private-value estimates are positively but imperfectly related to citation counts. The two proxies agree in direction - good patents tend to score on both - but the relationship is loose, and the distribution is wildly skewed, with a typical patent worth a few million and a thin tail worth orders of magnitude more. "Positively but imperfectly" is the honest summary, and it is exactly what "loosely linked" predicts.

Two studies push harder and find the link can bend the wrong way. Sharon Belenzon, looking at how patent citations map into firms' market value, finds that the market rewards a firm mostly for the follow-on innovation it can internalize - citations from the firm's own later patents - and rewards it far less for citations coming from other firms (Belenzon 2012). In other words, the stock market prices the knowledge a firm keeps, not the knowledge it gives away - even though the knowledge given away is the socially valuable part. And David Abrams, Ufuk Akcigit, and Jillian Grennan, using actual revenue data from patent-licensing firms, find that the relationship between a patent's private value and its citations is an inverted U: past a point, the most privately valuable patents are cited less than middling ones (Abrams, Akcigit & Grennan 2013). Their explanation is "strategic" patents - ones held partly to block or extract from others rather than to advance the art. This last result comes from a specific and unusual population (patent-assertion entities), so I'd file it as a vivid illustration rather than a general law. But it points the same way as the rest: private value and social contribution are different enough that, at the extremes, they can move in opposite directions.

You can even watch the wedge move in real time. Fabian Gaessler and coauthors study European patents that get challenged and revoked in post-grant opposition. When a patent is struck down and freedom-to-operate is restored, follow-on innovation by other firms rises (Gaessler et al. 2025). The private right had been suppressing socially valuable building-on by others - a direct, if narrow, picture of private and social value pulling against each other.

And recall Mansfield's hand-built sample: in it, the rank correlation between the expected private return and the social return was not statistically distinguishable from zero (with the loud caveat that he had only seventeen cases). Put the strands together - a positive-but-loose patent-value/citation link, a market that prices only internalized knowledge, a top tail where the relationship inverts, blocking patents that throttle follow-on - and the picture is consistent. The best evidence suggests private value is a real but noisy proxy for social value, and the noise is largest exactly where the stakes are highest.

Objection: maybe inventors get paid after all

The strongest push-back is that the "spillovers" in all of this are partly an illusion of measurement. When economists see firm B's productivity rise after firm A's research, they record an uncompensated spillover. But what if A sold the knowledge to B - licensed a patent, spun out the technology, signed a research deal? Then it isn't a leak at all; the inventor was paid, and the social-private gap is smaller than the spillover studies imply.

This is a serious objection, and a 2022 paper by Pere Arqué-Castells and Daniel Spulber takes it seriously (Arqué-Castells & Spulber 2022). They rebuild the Bloom-Schankerman-Van Reenen framework but allow knowledge to diffuse through voluntary technology transfers - markets for technology - as well as through involuntary spillovers. Their finding is exactly what the objection predicts: once you account for paid technology transfers, the measured gap between social and private returns shrinks relative to the spillovers-only approach. There are real gains from trade in markets for technology, and inventors capture more of their value through those markets than the classic spillover story allows.

I take two things from this. First, it is a genuine qualifier: some of the wedge is narrower than the canonical estimates suggest, and the honest version of the claim has to say so. Second, the gap shrinks but does not close - Arqué-Castells and Spulber still find social returns exceeding private ones, and their result speaks to the spillover-to-other-firms channel, not to the consumer-surplus channel that drove Nordhaus's 2.2% and half of Mansfield's gap. Consumers do not, by and large, send the inventor a check for the surplus they enjoy. So the objection trims the claim; it doesn't overturn it.

(A second, smaller objection: maybe the social returns are overstated because business-stealing isn't fully netted out. But Bloom and coauthors estimate the business-stealing term explicitly and still find spillovers dominate - so this is already inside the headline number, not missing from it.)

What this does and doesn't justify

Let me commit to a bottom line and then bound it.

I read the evidence as fairly convincing that society captures most of the value of innovation while inventors capture a minority of it, and that the inventor's private slice is a loose, noisy guide to how much an invention is worth to the world. The averages are well established across methods; the looseness at the level of individual inventions is supported but resting on noisier proxies; and the size of the wedge is a real point of contention that recent work on technology markets has narrowed.

What follows from this is the standard, and I think correct, case for public support of research - subsidies, tax credits, prizes, public science - to close part of the gap between what is privately and socially worth inventing. The evidence that public money does buy additional private innovation is decent: a study of NIH grant rules finds that a $10 million bump in funding yields about 2.7 additional private-sector patents, several of them at firms that never got a grant (Azoulay et al. 2019). Alfonso Gambardella has argued, relatedly, that we should think of patents as serving distinct private and social functions - protecting the inventor and organizing markets and new firms - and design them with both in mind (Gambardella 2023).

What does not follow is the temptation to read this as "the market is wrong, so let bureaucrats pick the inventions." The wedge tells you the private signal is biased downward and noisy, not that it is backwards. Profit is still positively correlated with social value; we are arguing about how tight that correlation is and how to top it up, not about replacing it. And which inventions have the biggest wedge - whose spillovers are largest, who should get the subsidy - is a separate, hard question I'm deliberately not answering here.

What we don't know

The honest gaps are these. First, we still don't have a clean estimate of the cross-invention correlation between private and social value, because both are measured with proxies that are themselves contested - citations are an imperfect stand-in for social value, and stock-market or model-based dollar values are imperfect stand-ins for private value. The "loosely linked" claim rests on several pieces of evidence pointing the same way, not on one decisive measurement.

Second, we don't know how much of the apparent spillover is actually priced through licensing and technology markets. Arqué-Castells and Spulber say more than the classic literature assumed; the magnitude is unsettled, and it bears directly on how big the policy problem really is.

Third, we don't know much about how the wedge varies - across fields, across firm sizes (Bloom and coauthors find smaller firms generate lower social returns because they work in technological niches), across basic versus applied work. The average gap is the easy part. Knowing where it is largest is what you'd actually need to spend public money well - and that is a post for another day.

Almost everything we claim to know about which inventions matter rests on a single number: how many times a later patent cites an earlier one. That number became the currency of innovation research in 2001, when Bronwyn Hall, Adam Jaffe, and Manuel Trajtenberg released the NBER Patent Citations Data File — more than 16 million citations, finally machine-readable — and launched a thousand papers. Want to know if an invention was important? Count its forward citations. Want to trace where an idea came from? Follow its backward citations. The citation became a proxy for influence, for knowledge flow, for quality, for value.

Here is a fact that should give us pause. On the average US patent, the inventor did not choose roughly 63% of those citations. A patent examiner added them. And on 40% of patents, every single citation was put there by the examiner, not the applicant. That is the central finding of Alcácer, Gittelman, and Sampat (2009), who could measure it for the first time after the USPTO began, in 2001, to record who added each reference. So when we rank inventions by the citations they receive, we are often ranking what overworked examiners happened to dig up in a prior-art search — not what inventors actually built on, and not what the world actually used.

This matters because of a worry that runs underneath all empirical work: if your ruler is bent, some of what you "discover" is a property of the ruler, not of the thing you measured. The claim I want to defend is that we now have a different ruler — one built from the words of patents rather than the footnotes — and that it is good enough to take seriously. Text-based measures capture things citation counts miss, they sidestep several of citations' documented biases, and crucially they disagree with citations often enough to change which inventions look important. Read the patent. Don't just count its citations.

The ruler we built the field on is leaky

Start with the case against citations, because it is the strongest part of the story — multiple clean studies, pointing the same way, so I'll use confident language here.

Most citations aren't the inventor's. The 63% examiner share above is not a quirk of one sample; it rises with foreign applicants and high-volume filers, and the broad pattern recurs in European data, where Criscuolo and Verspagen (2008) show inventor- and examiner-added citations behave like different animals. A citation count is a blend of two very different signals, and we usually can't tell them apart.

Citing is strategic. Inventors and their attorneys cite to establish patentability, to hedge against litigation, to avoid tipping off rivals. Corsino, Mariani, and Torrisi (2019) compared patent citations against what inventors said in a survey about where their knowledge actually came from, and found the measurement error is systematic — it depends on the firm's patenting strategy, the kind of knowledge, and the legal jurisdiction. The error isn't random noise that averages out; it correlates with exactly the things researchers want to study.

Citations miss whole categories of knowledge. In a study I find especially clarifying, Roach and Cohen (2013) — titled, perfectly, "Lens or Prism?" — matched patents to a survey of the R&D labs that produced them. Backward citations did capture codified, public knowledge flows. But they badly missed knowledge that was private, contract-based, or used in basic research. Relying on citations alone, they show, leads you to substantially underestimate how much public science feeds private invention. A lens shows you what's there; a prism bends it.

Citations are a weak guide to value. Using patent-renewal data to estimate what patents are actually worth, Bessen (2008) found that citations "explain little variance in value." They are correlated with value, yes — but loosely, which is a thin foundation for a measure we treat as a stand-in for importance.

And the coverage is thin where history is deep. The NBER file is only machine-readable for citations made from 1975 onward. For the entire 19th and early 20th century — the age of the dynamo, the telephone, the internal-combustion engine — there are essentially no citation counts to analyze. Worse, when citation structure gets turned into fancier metrics, the thinness bites: the famous "disruption index" decline turns out to be 88% an artifact of truncating backward citations before 1976, according to Macher, Rutzer, and Weder (2024). (That debate is its own rabbit hole, which I dig into in a companion post.)

None of this means citations are worthless — I'll come back to their genuine virtues. It means they carry large, non-random measurement error and can't see the past. If a different measure avoids those problems, it's worth a hard look.

Read the words instead

Every patent is a few thousand words of text describing what the invention is and does. That text is a measurement instrument we left lying on the floor. The idea behind text-based measures is simple: compare a patent's words to the words of every other patent, and let the overlaps tell you how novel it is and how much it shaped what came after.

Here's the intuition with a toy example, no math. Take a patent and boil its text down to the words it uses. Compare it backward, to everything filed before it: if almost none of its vocabulary shows up in prior patents, it is novel — it is saying something genuinely new. Now compare it forward, to everything filed after: if later patents start using its vocabulary heavily, it was impactful — the field started talking the way it did. A patent that is dissimilar to its past but similar to its future is, by this measure, important: it introduced the language everyone later adopted. Notice what just happened — we measured novelty and influence without a single citation.

That is precisely the construction in the flagship paper here, Kelly, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Taddy (2021) in AER: Insights. They define novelty as backward text dissimilarity and impact as forward text similarity, and combine them into a measure of patent "importance." Three things make it convincing. First, the measure predicts future citations — so it's not orthogonal to the old ruler, it anticipates it. Second, it correlates strongly with firms' market value — it tracks something the market is willing to pay for. Third, and most striking, it runs from 1836 to the present, and on its own it reconstructs the great technological waves: electricity and transportation surging in the 1880s, chemicals and electricity again in the 1920s and '30s, computing and communications after 1980. It draws the history of American invention for a century in which, remember, citation data simply does not exist.

Is the text measure actually picking out good inventions, or just unusual prose? This is where validation matters, and the cleanest evidence comes from Arts, Hou, and Gomez (2021). They build text measures of novelty (new words and new word-combinations) and impact (later reuse of those words) for the whole population of US patents — then validate them against two outside benchmarks. On one side, patents tied to major awards (the Nobel, the National Inventor Hall of Fame), which ought to score as important. On the other, patents that the USPTO granted but that the European and Japanese offices both rejected — which ought to be trivial. The text measures separate the important from the trivial better than the traditional citation- and classification-based metrics do. That's an apples-to-apples horse race, and text wins it.

The same team's earlier paper, Arts, Cassiman, and Gomez (2018), did the patient validation work for similarity itself: they had technology experts judge whether text-matched patents really were more alike than patent-classification matches, and the text measure improved on the old classes. Then they did something I love — they re-ran a classic result (that knowledge spillovers are geographically localized) using text-matched control patents instead of the usual citation-matched controls, and recovered it. Text didn't just describe; it could carry the weight of a real research design. Both teams put their code and data online, which is why the approach spread fast. (This is not a patents-only trick, either: text-based measurement has a strong track record in economics — Hoberg and Phillips (2016) famously redrew industry boundaries from the product descriptions in firms' 10-K filings.)

On the weight of this evidence, my confidence is fairly high that text-based measures are a real advance: they add discriminating power citations lack, they survive head-to-head validation, and they reach where citations can't.

Same patents, two rulers, different verdicts

A new ruler is only interesting if it sometimes disagrees with the old one. If text and citations always ranked inventions identically, we'd have a cute robustness check and nothing more. So: do they disagree?

The most direct test I found is Yang (2025), who put the two head-to-head on more than six million US patents. On one side, a text-based breakthrough index (novelty × impact, built from words). On the other, the citation-based disruption index (built from the structure of who-cites-whom). The headline is reassuring: the two are highly correlated, and both genuinely pick out breakthroughs. The old ruler and the new one mostly agree — which is exactly why we can trust either.

But they diverge in ways that are not random:

Text-based index Citation-based index
Over time tracks economic cycles steady secular decline
Predicts future citations monotonically (more is more) U-shaped
Small, remote teams score higher score lower

Look at that last row, because it's the tell. Small and remote teams look more breakthrough-y by their words but less disruptive by their citations. Yang's explanation is that larger teams cite newer, widely-recognized references — so the citation-based metric partly measures how a team cites, a social and strategic act, rather than what it invented. The text doesn't know or care how famous your references are. When the two rulers disagree about whether small teams punch above their weight — a question people actually argue about — the disagreement is not noise. It's the citation metric importing the sociology of citing.

A second study sharpens the point from the science-and-technology angle. Liu and coauthors (2025) compared two ways of linking patents to the science they draw on: actual citations to research papers versus text similarity between the patent and the papers. The two signals only partly converge. Many citations turn out to be weakly relevant — present for legal or informational reasons rather than real knowledge transfer — and the text catches connections the citations miss. Their practical advice is telling: use the full text, not just titles and abstracts, and combine both signals, because each sees something the other doesn't.

This is the heart of the claim, and I'll keep the confidence honest: that text and citations diverge systematically — on time trends, on team size, on the long tail — rests heavily on a couple of recent, careful studies. I'd treat the direction of these divergences as solid, because each has a mechanism you can articulate, and the exact magnitudes as provisional until more papers pile in.

Objection: maybe citations are fine and text is just fashion

The strongest pushback is that I've been unfair to citations. Text analysis is having a moment — embeddings, transformers, large language models — and it would be easy to mistake novelty-of-method for validity. So let me make the other side's best case.

Citations do track real quality. The most convincing demonstration is Moser, Ohmstedt, and Rhode (2018), who had something almost no one has: an objective measure of invention quality. For hybrid corn, they linked patents to actual field-trial yield performance — how much more corn the patented variety grew. Citations were robustly correlated with that real-world performance. Yes, attorney citing inflated counts for early patents and examiner citing added noise, but underneath the distortions, citations were tracking something true. Where you can check citations against ground truth, they pass. In the same spirit, Chen (2017) showed that citing–cited patent pairs really are more textually similar than random pairs — so a citation does, on average, encode a genuine knowledge link.

And text has its own sins. Word-overlap measures linguistic similarity, which is not the same as conceptual similarity: two patents can describe the same idea in different vocabularies, or different ideas in the same buzzwords. Patent prose is full of attorney boilerplate and deliberate obfuscation. Vocabulary drifts over the very long horizons where text's coverage advantage is supposed to shine. And "novelty" and "importance" are definitional choices — backward similarity is one way to operationalize new, not the only way. The field is now wrestling with how much these measures depend on which language model you use; a recent literature on patent quality (Higham, de Rassenfosse, and Jaffe, 2021) warns that every quality proxy, text or citation, is inconsistent across the outcomes you might validate it against.

So the honest synthesis is not "text good, citations bad." It is: citations carry real signal wrapped in systematic, non-random error; text carries different signal wrapped in different error. Neither is the truth. They are two instruments pointed at the same object, and the object — an invention's real importance — is something we never observe directly.

Bottom line

Read the words and count the citations. Text-based measures of invention are a genuine advance, and I'm fairly confident about why: they fix documented citation biases (the examiner problem, strategic citing, the blindness to private and basic knowledge), they pass head-to-head validation against outside benchmarks, and they extend our reach back roughly a century to invention that predates any citation record. That much I'd defend with conviction.

But the title's promise is a bounded one. The rulers agree far more than they disagree — which is the good news, because it means the citation-based literature isn't built on sand. Where they part ways, neither has earned the right to be called correct, because we almost never have the ground truth that would settle it. What I'd retire is the reflex of reporting a single citation-based number as the measure of importance and moving on. When a finding about innovation depends on the ruler — and we now know some do, like whether small teams disrupt more — that dependence is itself the result. The discipline move is to compute your headline both ways and treat the gap as a finding, not a nuisance.

If a chunk of what we "know" about innovation was, all along, partly a fact about patent examiners and citing strategy, then the arrival of a second, independent ruler is one of the more useful things to happen to the empirics of innovation in twenty years. Not because it's right. Because it's different, and the difference is legible.

What we don't know

  • Which ruler for which question? Text wins at long-horizon coverage and at separating award-winners from duds; citations may be better where the legal-economic act of citing is itself what you care about. Nobody has a clean map of which measure to trust for which task.
  • Are LLM-era measures better, or just differently biased? Embeddings and large language models can read patents more subtly than word-counts — but they may inherit their own opacities (training data, model choice). Whether they reduce bias or relocate it is an open, fast-moving question.
  • Does text inherit drafting bias? Patent prose is shaped by attorneys and strategy as much as citations are. We have good evidence on citation gaming; we have much less on how attorney boilerplate and deliberate vagueness distort text measures.
  • How far does it generalize beyond patents? The same backward/forward-similarity logic is being applied to scientific papers and grants. Whether text measures behave as well there — where the writing conventions and incentives differ — is largely untested.
  • What would settle it? More datasets like hybrid corn: inventions with an objective, outside measure of quality, against which text and citations can both be scored. Until we have more ground truth, we are comparing two proxies to each other, not to the thing itself.

In 1714, the British Parliament had a problem it could not buy its way out of. Ships kept wrecking because nobody could reliably tell how far east or west they were at sea. The Crown was already funding astronomers, observatories, and naval expertise -- the eighteenth-century equivalent of an R&D budget -- and it had bought a great deal of science. What it had not bought was a solution. So Parliament tried something different. Instead of paying clever people to work on the problem, it promised a fortune -- up to GBP 20,000, an aristocrat's lifetime income -- to anyone, of any rank or nation, who could measure longitude to within half a degree (about 30 nautical miles) after an ocean crossing. It did not say how. It did not pick who. It simply named the result and put money on the table.

The prize was eventually all but won by John Harrison, a self-taught Yorkshire carpenter and clockmaker whom no committee would ever have funded, with a sequence of marine chronometers culminating in his "H4" in 1759. (In a foreshadowing of every pull instrument since, the administration was a mess: Harrison fought for decades, collected roughly GBP 23,000 in installments, and the official prize was, technically, never formally awarded to anyone.)

That contrast -- pay for the input versus pay for the result -- is one of the oldest distinctions in innovation policy, and one of the most underused. Economists call the two approaches push and pull. Push subsidizes the input, up front: research grants, R&D tax credits, university science. You pay whether or not anything works, and you have to decide in advance who gets the money. Pull rewards the output, after the fact: inducement prizes, advance market commitments, patent buyouts, guaranteed markets. You pay only on delivery of something you specified, and you never have to pick the winner. As Bryan and Williams (2021) lay out in their survey of innovation market failures, almost all of the money in modern innovation policy is push. We fund the input and hope.

My claim in this post is narrow: for some problems, pull reaches inventions that push misses -- because it pays for results, shifts risk onto the inventor, and targets a need without requiring anyone to know in advance who will meet it or how. But "some" is doing real work in that sentence. Pull is not a free lunch, it is not generally better than push, and the cases where it has clearly delivered are narrower than its enthusiasts suggest. By the end I want you to believe both halves: that pull is a genuine and wasted opportunity, and that it is a scalpel, not a hammer.

First, does demand pull invention at all?

Before debating clever mechanisms, the prior question: if you increase the reward for solving a problem, do you actually get more solutions? Or do inventors just invent whatever they were going to invent regardless?

Here the evidence is reasonably strong, and it comes from settings where demand moved for reasons that had nothing to do with the science. The cleanest case is pharmaceuticals. In a much-cited study, Acemoglu and Linn (2004) used the predictable aging of the U.S. population to forecast which drug categories would see their markets grow -- demand shifts driven by demography, not by lab breakthroughs -- and found that a 1% increase in a category's potential market size led to a 4-6% increase in the number of new drugs entering it. Costinot, Donaldson, Kyle and Williams (2019) sharpened the logic with a global twist: countries whose demographics make them demand a particular drug the most tend to become net exporters of it -- demand pulls not just the quantity of innovation but its direction.

The pattern isn't confined to medicine or to demographics. Aghion, Bergeaud, Lequien and Melitz (2024) exploited exogenous export-demand shocks to French firms and found they responded by patenting more -- with the patents arriving two to five years after the shock, a nice reminder that innovation takes time to answer a call. And Gerarden (2023) studied something much closer to deliberate policy: consumer subsidies for rooftop solar. Estimating a structural model of solar-panel manufacturers, he found the subsidies didn't just sell more panels, they induced manufacturers to innovate and drive costs down -- and, strikingly, a subsidy in one country pushed international firms to innovate, lowering prices for everyone else too.

So the foundation is solid: engineering demand changes the rate and direction of invention. That is the whole premise pull instruments are built on. The interesting question is what happens when, instead of waiting for demand to shift, a government or donor manufactures it on purpose.

The prize revival

The Longitude Prize was not a fluke of the eighteenth century; prizes have a measurable track record. The best causal evidence I know comes from Brunt, Lerner and Nicholas (2012), who assembled a century of data -- 1839 to 1939 -- on technology prizes awarded by the Royal Agricultural Society of England. The threat to any prize study is reverse causation: prizes get offered in already-"hot" areas, so of course you see invention there. The authors get around it by exploiting a strict rotation scheme the Society used to set prize categories, which decoupled the prizes from where the action already was. Their finding: prizes caused large increases in competitive entry and a detectable rise in patenting in the targeted areas, and -- crucially -- the boost was not explained by inventors merely redirecting effort they'd have spent anyway. It was additional.

Two details from that paper stick with me. First, the additionality result is exactly what a skeptic demands and rarely gets. Second, and more surprising: the medals mattered more than the money. Recognition, not the cash, did much of the pulling. That tells you something about who enters prizes -- people for whom being seen to have won is itself the reward -- and it recurs in the modern era, from the $10 million Ansari X Prize that launched private spaceflight to Netflix's recommendation contest.

Modern "grand innovation prizes" have been studied directly. Murray, Stern, Campbell and MacCormack (2012) dissected the $10 million Progressive Automotive X PRIZE for a hyper-efficient car and came away impressed by the pull but sober about the plumbing: writing the specifications, deciding how patents and prizes interact, and governing the award fairly are genuinely hard. I'll return to that, because it is the Achilles' heel of pull. For now, note the upbeat half: a well-designed prize visibly draws in entrants -- including outsiders -- that no grant committee would have funded.

The big one: paying for a vaccine that doesn't exist yet

Prizes are charming, but the instrument that has moved the most lives is the advance market commitment (AMC). The idea is to make a binding, credible promise to buy a product -- at a guaranteed price, in guaranteed volume -- before it exists, so firms will invest in inventing and, especially, manufacturing it.

To see why you'd ever need such a thing, here is the problem an AMC solves, in one toy example. Suppose a vaccine would save millions of children in poor countries. Developing it and building capacity costs a firm, say, $500 million. The social value is enormous -- call it $5 billion. But the buyers are poor-country governments and aid agencies, and once the factory is built and the R&D is sunk, they can bargain the price down to just above the cost of making one more dose -- pennies. The firm can see that coming. It knows it will never recoup the $500 million, so it never builds the factory. A vaccine worth $5 billion to the world goes unmade -- not because the science is impossible, but because no one can credibly promise to pay for it. Economists call this a hold-up problem, and it is the heart of the first formal analysis of AMCs, by Kremer, Levin and Snyder (2022). An AMC is a pre-commitment device: donors put money in escrow and pledge to top up the price per dose until the fund is exhausted, which restores the firm's incentive to build.

This is not a thought experiment. In 2007, five governments and the Gates Foundation committed $1.5 billion to an AMC for pneumococcal vaccines -- the kind that prevent a leading cause of childhood pneumonia. Two manufacturers, GlaxoSmithKline and Pfizer, signed on to supply at a capped price; later a third, the Serum Institute of India, qualified with a cheaper product. By 2016 the program was delivering on the order of 160 million doses a year, immunizing more than 50 million children annually, and it is credited with helping save roughly 700,000 lives since 2010. Poor countries got the vaccine about five years sooner than they had gotten the previous big vaccine (for rotavirus), which had no AMC behind it.

But here is where calibration matters, and where Kremer, Levin and Snyder are admirably honest. The pneumococcal vaccine was already far along in development when the AMC launched. So the AMC's real achievement was not conjuring a new molecule out of nothing -- it was solving the hold-up problem on manufacturing and access: getting firms to build capacity for poor markets, and getting the product to those markets fast. That is a genuine and large win. It is just a different win from "pull invents things that didn't exist," and we should not let the vaccine's glow inflate the claim.

The COVID-19 pandemic ran the experiment again at hyperspeed. Governments used AMC-like advance purchases -- the U.S. commitment to Pfizer, for instance, promised about $2 billion for 100 million doses contingent on the vaccine actually being authorized. And the economics of doing so were lopsided in a way worth sitting with. Athey, Kremer, Snyder, Tabarrok and colleagues (2021) estimated that the value of speed -- of having vaccine capacity a few months sooner -- ran into the trillions. On their numbers, governments spent on the order of $20 billion procuring vaccines, while moving global capacity from two billion to three billion courses was worth something like $2.4 trillion. Even granting wide error bars, that is a benefit-cost ratio of roughly a hundred to one. When the prize is large and the target is clear, pull is almost embarrassingly cost-effective.

"So just always use pull?" No.

If you've followed this far you might conclude that we should shovel money from grants into prizes and AMCs. That would be the wrong lesson, and the theory is surprisingly emphatic about it.

Start with the most direct head-to-head. Che, Iossa and Rey (2021) ask what the optimal mechanism looks like when a sponsor wants both to motivate an innovation and to implement it efficiently, and when research effort can't be verified and costs are private. Their answer is not "use a prize." It is a combination of a cash prize and a follow-on contract -- and the optimum relies primarily on the contract, handing the innovator the implementation work when the innovation is valuable and handicapping them when it isn't. The pure cash prize shows up only as a supplement, deployed when the value at stake is very high. In other words, the best pull design has a big push-like component baked into it.

The portfolio logic gets sharper in Fu, Lu and Lu (2012), who model a sponsor -- they explicitly name the U.S. Department of Defense and the World Health Organization -- splitting a fixed budget between an inducement prize and efficiency-enhancing subsidies to contestants. Prizes and subsidies turn out to be complements, and the optimal mix tilts in an intuitive direction: the harder and more uncertain the problem, the more you should lean on subsidies (push) and the less on the prize (pull). Pull works best when the target is close enough that firms can credibly race for it; push is for the long, foggy frontier.

Even the relationship between prizes and the patent system, long taught as a straight either/or, turns out to be complementary. Galasso, Mitchell and Virag (2018) show that when innovation is multi-dimensional and only some of it is contractible, patent rights and cash prizes reinforce each other -- and that grand prizes can correct a specific pathology of patent races, namely their tendency to reward whoever is fastest over whoever is best. The upshot across all three papers is the same: pull belongs in a mixed portfolio, not on a pedestal.

"Fine, but don't prizes waste effort -- and can you even specify the target?"

Two more objections, both real, both limiting.

The first is duplication and discouragement. A winner-take-all race can have dozens of teams sprinting toward the same finish line, and all but one of their efforts is, in a narrow sense, wasted. Worse, the mere presence of strong rivals can sap everyone else's motivation. The best evidence here is a field experiment by Boudreau, Lakhani and Menietti (2016), who studied 2,775 contestants across 755 software contests with randomly assigned levels of competition. Adding competitors made most participants try less -- the discouragement effect -- while only the highest-skilled tried harder. So a prize's yield depends delicately on how it's structured: how many compete, how the rewards are split, whether there are divisions. Design is not a detail; it is the whole game. This is the granular, contest-level echo of the X PRIZE governance headaches that Murray and coauthors flagged.

The second objection is deeper: you can only pull what you can specify. An AMC or prize needs a contractible target -- a vaccine meeting defined criteria, a clock accurate to half a degree, a car hitting a fuel-economy number. For open-ended basic science -- where you don't know what the answer looks like, or even what question will turn out to matter -- there is nothing to write into the contract, and push (fund smart people and wait) is the only game in town. And note that push has its own design failures, so this is not a morality tale of clean pull versus dirty grants: Akcigit, Hanley and Serrano-Velarde (2021) show that uniform R&D subsidies tend to over-subsidize applied research and starve basic research, distorting the very frontier we most need. Both tools require careful hands.

There is also a cost that even a perfectly designed pull instrument can't escape: it redirects scarce talent. Lu (2025) studied California's zero-emission-vehicle mandate -- a demand pull toward electric vehicles -- and found it worked as intended and had a shadow. As EV firms ramped up, they bid away scarce inventors, and firms in adjacent technological domains produced 22% fewer inventions as a result. Pulling invention toward one target pulls it away from others. In general equilibrium, you are not only creating innovation; you are reallocating it.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. The claim that pull instruments can elicit innovation that push misses is fairly convincing for a specific class of problems: where the goal is concrete enough to write into a contract, and where the binding constraint is not scientific difficulty but the absence of a credible buyer. Poor-country vaccines are the textbook case, and the pneumococcal and COVID AMCs are a real, documented track record -- though their clearest achievement is accelerating and scaling near-ready solutions and curing hold-up, more than originating brand-new science. For well-specified performance targets, the prize evidence (additional entry, additional patents, the pull of recognition) is genuine if historically narrow.

The stronger claim -- that pull is generally better than push -- I think is false, and the theory agrees: the optimum is a portfolio that leans toward push as problems get harder and fuzzier, and toward pull as targets get sharper and the missing piece is demand. The right question is never "prize or grant?" It is "for this problem, which mix?" And the genuinely actionable point is that, given how lopsided the COVID speed math was, we are almost certainly under-using pull at the margin -- not that we should swing the whole budget over to it.

What we don't know

  • Can pull originate, not just accelerate? Our best AMC evidence is about a near-ready vaccine. We have little clean evidence on whether a large enough commitment can pull a genuinely new technology into being from an early stage. The design that works for late-stage products is, per Kremer-Levin-Snyder, the wrong design for early-stage ones -- and the early-stage version is largely untested.
  • How big is the duplication tax, really? We know contests can discourage and duplicate, but we lack good aggregate estimates of how much parallel effort is "wasted" versus valuably exploratory across an entire prize program.
  • What's the general-equilibrium bill? Lu's crowding-out result suggests pulling one technology starves others. Nobody has added up the net effect of a large pull program once you count what didn't get invented elsewhere.
  • Does recognition scale? If medals do much of the pulling, prizes may be cheaper than we think -- but it's unclear whether status incentives that work for hobbyist inventors and elite contests generalize to the firms that do most commercial R&D.

A pull instrument is a promise: name the result, guarantee the reward, and let the world figure out the rest. The history -- from a carpenter's sea-clock to a vaccine for children who would otherwise never have gotten one -- says the promise can work wonders. The economics says it works only when you can say exactly what you're promising, and only as one half of a well-balanced policy. Push and pull are not rivals. They are the two hands you need to move an idea from nowhere into the world.

2026-06-07 · 12 min read · innovation science policy

In February 2025, the U.S. National Institutes of Health announced that it would stop reimbursing universities for the "indirect costs" of research — the buildings, the freezers, the safety officers, the administrative spine of a lab — at the rates it had negotiated, and would instead cap them at 15%. Most universities had negotiated rates closer to 50–70%. The agency estimated the cap would save about $4 billion a year. Twenty-two lawsuits, a district-court injunction, and an appeals-court ruling later, the cap was blocked, and Congress went on to reject a proposed 40% cut to the NIH's roughly $48-billion budget. But the underlying question never went away, and it will be back: what does the public actually get for the money it pours into science?

That question is genuinely hard, and the honest reason is an accounting problem. When the government funds a grant, the things you can easily count — the grantee's papers, the grantee's own patents, the students trained — capture only a sliver of what the money does. The bigger payoff is diffuse, delayed, and lands somewhere else entirely: in the patent portfolio of a pharmaceutical company three states away that never saw a dollar of the grant, built on a discovery it read about in a journal. If your ledger only has a column for the grantee, you will conclude that public science is a worse deal than it is.

Here is the claim I want to defend: public research grants causally increase private-sector patenting, and most of the induced patents are taken out by firms that never received the funding. The benefit is real, it is large enough to matter for the budget fight, and it is mostly invisible to the people doing the budgeting. I'll also be honest about the wrinkle that complicates the headline — because there's a clean recent paper that, read naively, seems to say the opposite.

Three things to march through: the experiment that pins down the causal effect; the spillover that makes most of the payoff invisible; and the reason the private sector won't just do this itself. Then the objection, and the bottom line.

The natural experiment hiding inside the NIH

The obvious problem with "does funding science cause private innovation?" is that the NIH doesn't sprinkle money at random. It funds promising areas — and promising areas are exactly the ones where companies would have patented anyway. Correlate NIH dollars with downstream patents and you measure the agency's taste in science, not the causal effect of its checks.

The cleanest way around this is the centerpiece of Azoulay, Graff Zivin, Li, and Sampat (2019), in the Review of Economic Studies. They build a bibliometric crosswalk — roughly 106,000 NIH grants, the papers those grants funded, and the patents that later cite those papers — and then exploit the rigidities in NIH peer review to find variation in funding that isn't about commercial promise. The NIH funds applications by score, within institute-specific paylines, and quirks in how proposals get sorted across study sections and institutes hand some research areas an idiosyncratic windfall and others an arbitrary shortfall. Compare patenting across areas that got luckily-more versus luckily-less money, and the agency's taste washes out.

The result: a $10 million increase in NIH funding to a research area produces on the order of 2.3 to 2.7 additional private-sector patents (the published estimate sits around 2.7; some specifications give 3.1) — call it roughly one private patent for every two grants. That this shows up at all, with an identification strategy this careful, is the foundation everything else rests on. I'd call the existence and direction of the effect fairly convincing: it's a credible natural experiment in a top journal, and — as we'll see — it's not alone.

Two details from that paper matter more than the headline, and I'll take them in turn.

The patents you can't see

The first detail: the patents are not the grantees'. Azoulay and coauthors are explicit that "academic patenting corresponds to only a small fraction of the impact of NIH-funded research on patenting more generally." The professor who got the grant occasionally patents something herself, but that is a rounding error next to the patenting her science induces in the private sector. Even more striking, the funding raises private patenting in an area whether or not the patents actually cite NIH-funded work — the knowledge has diffused more broadly than any citation trail can capture. The money goes into a public-university lab; the patents come out of corporate R&D departments that were watching the literature.

How big is this invisible part? The best single number comes from a different program and a different team. Myers and Lanahan (2022), in the American Economic Review, study Department of Energy grants to small firms and — using state-level matching policies as their source of exogenous variation — measure spillovers without relying on a citation paper trail at all. Their finding is the one to remember: for every patent a grant recipient produces, about three more are produced by others who benefit from the spillover. The recipients, they estimate, capture only 25–50% of the value of the innovation their own grant-funded research sets off. About 60% of the spillovers stay inside the United States, and many land in technological fields quite different from the one the grant was aimed at.

Sit with the arithmetic for a second. If the funded firm captures something like a third to a half of the value, then the majority of the return to a public research dollar is, by construction, showing up on someone else's books. An evaluation that credits the grant only with what the grantee did — the natural thing for a program officer or a budget hawk to do — is looking at the smaller half of the ledger. That is the accounting blind spot from the opening, made quantitative. And the fact that two very different programs (NIH basic biomedical science; DOE applied energy grants), studied by two different teams with two different identification strategies, both land on "most of the patenting happens outside the funded lab" is what moves me from "interesting result" to fairly convincing pattern.

There's a longer lineage here, too. A decade before Azoulay, Toole (2012) linked NIH basic-research investments to the entry of new drugs and found that public basic research has an economically significant effect on the pharmaceutical pipeline — concentrated, tellingly, at the earliest stage of drug discovery. That mattered because an influential earlier paper, Acemoglu and Linn (2004), had found that market size pulled drugs into existence but had found no evidence of science-push from public research. Toole showed the science-push was there once you accounted for the long lags between a basic discovery and a marketed drug. "Shows up later" is not a throwaway in my title; the effect can take years, which is part of why it's so easy to miss.

Why the market won't just do this itself

A natural retort: if publicly funded science is so valuable to companies, why don't the companies fund it themselves? The answer is the oldest idea in the economics of innovation, and the spillover numbers above are exactly why it bites.

Richard Nelson (1959) and Kenneth Arrow (1962) made the case in the abstract: basic research produces knowledge that is hard to keep to yourself, so whoever pays for it captures only part of the benefit, so the market underprovides it. For sixty years that was a blackboard argument. The recent literature has put numbers on it. Arora, Belenzon, and Sheer (2021), studying 800,000 corporate publications, show that firms do more research when they can use it internally and less when rivals can use it — and that as firms have grown more exposed to rivals capturing their science, they have been retreating from open research altogether. The very appropriability problem Nelson described is, on the data, getting worse: the private sector is pulling back from exactly the kind of science whose spillovers it can't fence in.

This is the gap public funding fills, and it tells you something about how to fill it. Akcigit, Hanley, and Serrano-Velarde (2021) build and estimate a growth model in which basic and applied research generate different spillovers, and reach a sharp policy conclusion: because basic research spills over so much more, a uniform research subsidy — treating every R&D dollar alike — actually makes things worse by over-rewarding applied work that firms would fund anyway. What's welfare-improving is support aimed specifically at public basic research and its handoff to the private sector. The spillover that makes public science look like a bad deal in the grantee's ledger is precisely the thing that makes it a good deal for everyone else.

Worth noting: this is the same wedge that runs through the rest of the innovation literature. Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013) estimate that the gross social returns to R&D are at least twice the private returns; Zacchia (2020), tracing spillovers through networks of scientists, puts the marginal social return at around 112% of the private one. Public science is the part of that wedge the market is least equipped to capture on its own.

"But cutting funding raises patenting!"

Now the honest complication, and it's a good one. Babina, He, Howell, Perlman, and Staudt (2023), in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, link person-level federal grants at 22 universities to patents, publications, and startups inside Census data, and study what happens when a researcher's field suffers a sharp, temporary funding cut. Their finding seems to torpedo my claim: when federal funding falls, university patenting goes up.

If you stop there, the headline is "public money reduces patents." But read the rest. The patents that appear after a cut are lower quality, less general, and more often privately assigned — appropriated by corporate funders. What falls when the federal money dries up is high-tech entrepreneurship and publications, and the lost publications are the higher-quality, more basic ones. The mechanism is substitution: deprived of federal support, researchers take private money instead, and their output shifts from open science toward closed, applied, privately-owned IP.

So the paper doesn't overturn the claim — it sharpens it, in two ways. First, it warns against the naive output count: if your metric of "innovation" is the raw number of university patents, you will badly misread a funding cut, because you'll score the shift toward cheap, appropriable patents as a gain. Second, it tells you what public funding's distinctive product actually is — open, basic science, the high-spillover input — which is exactly the thing Azoulay, Myers-Lanahan, Toole, and Akcigit identify as the source of the downstream private patents. The patents that public science drives are corporate patents built on freely available discoveries; the patents that appear when you cut public science are the professor's own, hawked to a private sponsor. They are different objects, and conflating them is the trap.

What it's worth, roughly

Let me put an explicitly rough number on the stakes, with the caveat stated up front: Azoulay's estimate is a marginal one, identified off idiosyncratic variation, and scaling it to the whole budget is an illustration, not a forecast.

Take the marginal figure at face value — $10 million buys about 2.3–2.7 private-sector patents — and a hypothetical $1 billion swing in NIH-funded research is associated with something like 230–270 directly-linked private-sector patents. Apply Myers and Lanahan's rough 3-to-1 spillover ratio as an upper guide and the total, counting the firms that never got a dollar, could be several times larger. On the value side, Azoulay and coauthors estimate that $1 of NIH funding generates between $0.70 and $2.13 in lifetime sales of FDA-approved drugs — and they are emphatic that this omits consumer surplus, medical devices, non-patented clinical methods, and the actual health benefits. Even the bottom of that range is a serious private return before you count any of the value to patients.

Could the indirect-cost cap from the opening have been the efficiency win its proponents claimed? On these elasticities, shaving billions off the effective research budget would forgo hundreds of private-sector patents and, more importantly, the basic science underneath a multiple of that — most of it value that would have accrued to firms and patients, not to the universities taking the nominal cut. The savings are visible and immediate; the losses are diffuse and delayed. That asymmetry is the whole problem.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That public research grants causally raise private-sector patenting, mostly at non-grantee firms, is fairly convincing: it rests on two credible natural experiments (NIH funding-rule rigidities; DOE state matching), spanning basic and applied programs, corroborated by an older science-to-drugs literature and underwritten by a coherent appropriability story that the corporate-science data independently confirm. On the direction, I'm comfortable: the return to public science is real, and standard grantee-level accounting captures the smaller share of it.

The bounds are mainly about size, not sign. The dollar value of a patent is notoriously slippery, so I trust "the social return is high and undercounted" far more than any specific ROI. The cleanest estimates are marginal, so I distrust grand extrapolations to the entire budget — including my own back-of-the-envelope, which is there to size the stakes, not to forecast. And Babina and coauthors are a standing warning that the composition of innovation responds to funding in ways a single patent count will mislead you about. My confidence ladder runs fairly convincing on "public grants cause private patents via spillovers," suggestive-to-solid on the rough magnitudes, and deliberately humble on any precise return on a dollar. The reflex that public science is a poor investment because you can't see its payoff in the grantee's output gets the accounting exactly backwards — but "therefore fund everything" is an overreach the evidence doesn't license either; Akcigit's point is that what you fund (basic, and its handoff to industry) is where the leverage is.

What we don't know

  • The true ROI. Patents are a proxy, and the patent-to-value mapping is rough. We have a defensible direction and order of magnitude, not a number you'd want to write into a budget rule.
  • How far it generalizes. The cleanest evidence is NIH/biomedical and DOE/energy. Whether the same spillover ratios hold for NSF basic science, defense research, or climate R&D is mostly untested, and the spillover share surely varies by field.
  • Where the optimum is. "Public basic research has high spillovers" is not "more is always better." Nobody has pinned down the marginal return at current funding levels, which is the number the budget fight actually turns on.
  • The composition problem, quantified. Babina et al. show funding changes the mix of innovation (open vs. appropriable, basic vs. applied). We don't yet have a clean welfare accounting that nets the lost basic publications against the gained private patents — and that accounting is exactly what would settle how much a cut really costs.
  • The long lag. Toole's drugs took years to appear. The full patent and product consequences of the 2025 funding turbulence won't be measurable until the 2030s, which is precisely why the decisions get made without the evidence.

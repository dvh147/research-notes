Until the 1970s, finding a new gold deposit was a story about boots, luck, and incumbency. The big mining houses had the geologists, the drilling budgets, and the proprietary maps; a prospector without them was mostly guessing. Then NASA's Landsat satellites began photographing the entire surface of the Earth and giving the images away. Suddenly anyone could pull up the surface geology of a remote basin from a desk, for free.

Abhishek Nagaraj asked what that open data did to gold mining, and the answer comes in two numbers. First, once a region had been mapped by Landsat, the rate of significant new gold discoveries there nearly doubled (Nagaraj 2022). That part you might have guessed: better information, more strikes. The second number is the one to sit with. The share of those discoveries made by junior firms — small new entrants rather than the established mining houses — rose from about 10 percent to about 25 percent. The open map didn't just produce more gold strikes. It changed who got to make them.

That second effect, not the first, is what this post is about. The open-versus-proprietary choice is now everywhere — the human genome, satellite imagery, weather data, and most loudly the trained weights of AI models. We almost always argue it as an efficiency question: does giving the data away grow the pie, or does it kill the incentive to bake the pie at all? That's a real question, and it's the subject of a companion post on patents and follow-on innovation. But it's the less interesting half. The more robust — and more surprising — regularity in the economics of cumulative research is distributional. Lowering the cost of getting at a research input — data, materials, tools, even the published literature — doesn't only raise the amount of science built on top of it. It changes who does the building, tilting discovery toward entrants and outsiders. Openness is a leveler, not just an accelerant.

I hold the two halves of that claim with different confidence, and I'll keep them separate throughout. That openness raises the level of follow-on work is, by now, pretty well established. That it shifts who does it toward the periphery is the striking part, and the evidence for it — while strong, because it shows up across wildly different settings — is younger and worth weighing carefully. And there is one serious objection, that open data might instead entrench whoever already has the most compute and cash, which I think is genuinely unsettled at the AI frontier. Let me take the pieces in turn.

Open the map, and outsiders strike gold

Start with the satellites, because the design is unusually clean. The worry with any "open data helped" story is reverse causation: maybe the regions that got mapped were the promising ones, so the discoveries would have come anyway. Nagaraj's fix is to lean on two accidents. Landsat coverage rolled out across the globe on an idiosyncratic schedule, and — crucially — the satellites can't see through clouds, so a perpetually cloudy region got worse usable imagery for reasons that have nothing to do with its gold. Using that variation, the doubling of discoveries and the jump in entrants' share read as causal effects of the data, not of the underlying geology.

Why would open data favor entrants in particular? Nagaraj's framework is that public data reduces investment uncertainty. An incumbent miner already sits on private geological surveys and decades of drilling records; a free satellite map adds little it didn't already know. A newcomer has none of that, so the same free map is the difference between flying blind and having a shot. Open data is worth most to the player who had the least to begin with — and so it pulls that player in.

The same researcher, with Esther Shears and Matthijs de Vaan, then ran the academic version of the experiment and found the same shape. Looking at roughly 24,000 Landsat-enabled scientific papers by more than 34,000 authors, they show that when the cost and sharing restrictions on the imagery fell, the quantity and the quality of Landsat science rose — and the gains landed disproportionately on scientists from the developing world and from lower-ranked institutions (Nagaraj, Shears & de Vaan 2020). A free map is a bigger deal to a geographer in Nairobi than to a lab at Stanford that could already buy whatever data it wanted. Open the input, and the new contributors are the ones who couldn't afford the closed version.

It isn't just satellites

If this were only a fact about remote sensing, it wouldn't be worth a post. The reason to believe it is a mechanism rather than a quirk is that the same pattern appears for completely different inputs, in completely different centuries.

Go back to 1942. Barbara Biasi and Petra Moser study the US Book Republication Program, a wartime measure that let American publishers reprint exact copies of German-owned science books — breaking, in effect, the copyright on a chunk of the world's best chemistry and mathematics. The immediate consequence was that the affected books got about 25 percent cheaper. The downstream consequence was more follow-on science: comparing republished titles to similar ones left alone, each 10 percent drop in a book's price is associated with roughly a 43 percent increase in citations to it (Biasi & Moser 2021). And — the now-familiar twist — the extra follow-on came disproportionately from scientists at less-affluent institutions, the people for whom an expensive imported monograph had been a real barrier. Cheaper access to knowledge didn't just produce more science; it widened the set of people producing it, a result the authors confirm with two independent yardsticks, new PhDs and new patents.

Now jump to a setting with no copyrights, no satellites, and a near-experiment built into the policy itself. The US Air Force runs an R&D grant program that, for a stretch, held two kinds of competition simultaneously: a "conventional" track, where the government specified the technology it wanted, and an "open" track, where firms pitched whatever idea they liked. Sabrina Howell and coauthors compare the two (Howell, Rathje, Van Reenen & Wong 2025). Open awards raised the firm's later venture-capital funding by about 12 percentage points (against a mean so low this is a near-tripling), patenting by about 9 points, and the military's actual adoption of the technology by 11 points, while conventional awards did none of those things — their only measurable effect was to lock the winner into more future contracts. And the firms the open track reached were younger, less likely to hold a prior defense contract, and more likely to sit in entrepreneurial hubs than the defense-establishment regulars the conventional track drew. Letting outsiders propose, rather than telling insiders what to build, both produced more innovation and routed it through firms that the conventional process would never have funded.

Three settings — gold maps, wartime books, defense grants — and one direction. Stack on top of them the evidence from the patent side of the literature, which I treat more fully in the companion post but which rhymes precisely: when a court strikes down a patent, the surge of follow-on innovation that follows is driven entirely by small firms building on what large firms had fenced (Galasso & Schankerman 2015); when a 1956 antitrust decree forced AT&T to license its entire patent portfolio for free, the follow-on it unleashed came mostly from young and small companies (Watzinger et al. 2020); when late-1990s agreements opened access to a set of patented research mice, the result was not just more follow-on but markedly more diverse follow-on, with new researchers entering and new directions explored (Murray et al. 2016). Five independent literatures, four kinds of input — data, literature, procurement, patented tools — and the gains keep landing on the same people: the entrants, the juniors, the newcomers.

Why openness leans toward outsiders

The convergence is the argument. When five studies using cloud cover, wartime price shocks, randomized-feeling procurement arms, and the luck of the judicial draw all point the same way, you're probably looking at a mechanism, and the mechanism here is simple: the cost of access is a regressive tax on discovery.

Think about what a $5,000 proprietary dataset actually is to different researchers. To a well-funded lab or an incumbent firm it is a rounding error — and more to the point, they probably already have their own version of it, plus the staff, the subscriptions, the legal department, and the back catalogue of past work to lean on. To a graduate student in a poor department, or a two-person startup, that same $5,000 is a wall. The barrier was never binding for the insider; it was only ever binding for the outsider. So when you knock it down, you don't hand everyone an equal gift — you disproportionately free the person who was stuck behind it. That is why "more access" keeps coming out as "more outsiders" rather than "more of the same people doing more."

You can see the logic run in reverse, too, in what happens when access is taken away. When the First World War and the boycott that followed severed scientists' access to frontier research from the other side of the front, Alessandro Iaria, Carlo Schwarz, and Fabian Waldinger show that scientific production fell — fewer top-journal papers, less Nobel-nominated work, fewer genuinely new scientific ideas — and it fell hardest for the scientists who had depended most on that frontier knowledge: those reliant on the very top 1 percent of foreign research lost about twice as much productivity as those reliant on the top 3 percent (Iaria, Schwarz & Waldinger 2018). Cut off the input and the most dependent fall furthest; open it up and the most excluded gain most. Same lever, both directions.

Objection! Isn't this just selection?

The sharpest worry about everything above is that openness and good fortune travel together. Maybe open data attaches itself to the most promising problems; maybe the open procurement track happened to attract better firms; maybe the cheap books were the ones destined to matter. If "open" is just a label that lands on winners, then "open inputs produce more outsider discoveries" could be a statement about which inputs get opened, not about openness doing anything.

This is exactly the objection the designs were built to kill, which is why I leaned on them. Cloudy skies don't choose gold-rich regions, so Nagaraj's mining result isn't picking promising ground. The Air Force ran its open and conventional competitions at the same time over the same technology space, and Howell and coauthors show directly that openness raises outcomes over and above the differential selection of firms — the open track does work even holding fixed who applies. Biasi and Moser's price variation comes from a wartime copyright accident, not from publishers betting on winners, and they match republished books to comparable untreated ones. And the patent studies ride on random judge assignment and a blanket antitrust decree that swept in every patent at once. None of these is a survey of things that happened to be open. Each manufactures a reason for openness that is unrelated to the quality of what's behind it — and the outsider-tilt survives every time. The pattern is too consistent, across too many independent sources of as-good-as-random variation, to be selection wearing a disguise.

Objection! Doesn't open data just help whoever owns the supercomputer?

Here is the objection I take more seriously, and where I think the honest answer is "it depends, and at the frontier we don't know." A literature on the "data divide" argues that releasing data can entrench advantage rather than spread it, because using data takes complementary capabilities — compute, skills, money, data literacy — that are themselves unequally held. Hand everyone the same open dataset and the player with the server farm and the PhDs extracts ten times the value; the gap widens. Reviews of open science worry, with reason, that nominally inclusive openness can quietly reproduce "cumulative advantage" (OECD 2022; Ross-Hellauer et al. 2022).

I don't think this is wrong; I think it's a statement about when the leveling result holds. Two conditions do the work. The first is the mode of openness. The cases above are all genuinely free-to-access-and-build: a downloadable map, a public-domain reprint, a royalty-free license. "Openness" that still charges at the door — the author-pays publishing model, where the fee to publish open-access can be its own barrier to a poorly funded lab — can push the other way, excluding exactly the people free access would include. The second is how much scarce complementary capital the input demands. Reading a cheap book, ordering a cell line, or downloading a satellite tile asks little beyond the input itself, so opening it levels. Training a frontier model on open weights asks for a data center that only a few institutions on Earth possess — and there, openness might hand the biggest gift to whoever already has the compute.

So I'll flag the speculation plainly: the leveling result is established for inputs you can use with modest complementary resources, and I would not bet the AI case on it. Open model weights are the live example, and they are precisely the setting where the complementary input — compute — is most unequally distributed. It is entirely possible that "open" foundation models democratize tinkering at the edges while concentrating real capability further. We don't yet have the clean causal study that would settle it, and anyone who tells you the satellite-and-books evidence obviously extends to AI is reaching past the data.

Bottom line

Read the evidence as a whole and a bounded claim stands up well. Lowering the cost of accessing a research input reliably raises the follow-on science built on it — that much is close to settled across data, materials, tools, and literature. And in every clean setting we can examine, it does something more: it widens the circle of who gets to make the discoveries, pulling in junior firms, younger researchers, and scientists at the institutions that could never afford the closed version. The mechanism — access cost is a tax that only ever bound the outsider — is simple enough to travel, and the fact that the result recurs across satellites, wartime books, defense grants, research mice, and struck-down patents is the best reason to believe it's real rather than local.

I hold this with fair confidence for the domains where the sharp studies live: remote sensing, research materials, historical science, and government R&D, where the input is something you can actually use without a fortune in complementary kit. I hold it tentatively, and with an explicit asterisk, for the compute-gated frontier — AI models and the data behind them — where the same openness might concentrate as easily as it spreads. The one-line version: open inputs are a leveler when "open" means free to build on and the building doesn't require a supercomputer. Where both conditions hold, giving the data away is not charity to incumbents or a threat to incentives so much as an invitation to everyone who was priced out.

What we don't know

  • Does it carry to AI? The whole argument's external validity now hinges on the one case we can't yet identify cleanly: open data and model weights in a world where the complementary input, compute, is extraordinarily concentrated. This is the hook and the open question at once.
  • Counts versus welfare. Almost everything here is measured in discoveries, publications, patents, or citations. A doubling of gold strikes or a 43 percent rise in citations is a rise in activity, not a measured gain in human welfare, and we rarely know the exchange rate.
  • Does openness dry up the incentive to create the input? These studies watch what happens once an input exists and is opened. Murray and coauthors find no drop in the creation of new research mice under openness, which is reassuring, but it is one case. If mandated openness blunts the incentive to build the foundational dataset in the first place, the calculus shifts.
  • How do you build the complementary capabilities? If open data only levels for those who can use it, the binding policy question may not be "open or closed" at all, but how to fund the skills, tooling, and compute access that let a newcomer turn an open input into a discovery. We have far less evidence on what works there.

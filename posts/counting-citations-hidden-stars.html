Almost everything we say about measuring science now comes with a warning label. In 2013, a group of editors and funders signed the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment, telling the world to stop using journal impact factors to judge individual scientists in "hiring, promotion, or funding decisions." Since 2022, more than 800 institutions have lined up behind the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment, pledging to demote the numbers in favor of human judgment. A small shelf of books — Jerry Muller's The Tyranny of Metrics, Mario Biagioli and Alexandra Lippman's Gaming the Metrics — catalogs the gaming, salami-slicing, and citation rings that bloom once careers ride on a count. The presiding spirit is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's tidy restatement of Goodhart's law: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

I think the critics are largely right about the world we live in now. But the consensus skips a prior question: what did citation metrics actually do to science when they first arrived? For all the worry, there was — until recently — essentially no causal evidence on whether performance metrics change how scientific talent gets allocated. That is the gap a 2024 paper in the American Economic Review sets out to fill, and its answer is the opposite of the warning label.

Here is the claim I want to defend: when citation counts first became visible — in the 1960s, with the world's first citation index — they did not corrupt the allocation of scientific talent across departments. They improved it. By making merit legible to people who couldn't see it before, the new metric helped good scientists in the wrong places get found — disproportionately scientists with weak pedigrees, and, more tentatively, women. The same visibility also fed a Matthew effect, piling extra rewards on the already-recognized. Both things are true. But the first one is the one the modern debate forgets.

Why the allocation of talent is worth caring about

Start with the stakes, because they are large. Where a society's ablest people end up is not a private matter. The classic statement is Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny (1991): when talent flows into productive work it drives growth, and when it flows into rent-seeking it merely redistributes. The carefully-measured modern version is Hsieh, Hurst, Jones, and Klenow's 2019 paper in Econometrica, which estimates that between 20% and 40% of all growth in US output per person from 1960 to 2010 came from simply letting women and Black Americans into occupations they'd been kept out of — the same people, doing work they were better suited for.

Science is a concentrated version of that problem. A handful of researchers produce most of the field-changing work, so getting the right person into the right lab, with the right collaborators, matters enormously — the people around a scientist measurably shape what they produce. Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang (2010) found that when a superstar coauthor dies unexpectedly, their collaborators' output falls a lasting 5–8%. And the talent that is hardest for the market to see tends to be exactly the talent that's unevenly distributed. Bell, Chetty, and coauthors (2019) found that children of top-1% families are about ten times as likely to become inventors as below-median-income children, a gap that survives controlling for childhood math scores — their famous "lost Einsteins." When a market can't observe ability directly, it falls back on pedigree. And pedigree is precisely what's rationed by class, race, and gender.

So here's the question the 2024 paper makes answerable. Before 1960, a scientist's true citation impact was effectively invisible outside their own subfield. What happens to the allocation of talent when, suddenly, you can count?

A citation you could see, and one you couldn't

The natural experiment runs on a piece of plumbing. In 1955, Eugene Garfield proposed a citation index for science — a way to trace who cited whom across the whole literature. In 1963 his Institute for Scientific Information shipped the first Science Citation Index (SCI), covering citations made in 1961. For the first time, you could look a scientist up and read off how often their work had been cited. Administrators pounced: within a few years the SCI was being used in hiring, tenure, and grant decisions. (The same apparatus later spawned the journal impact factor and, in 2005, the h-index — the machinery that the modern backlash is reacting against.)

The crucial accident is that the early SCI was incomplete in a very particular way. For technical and computing reasons, it only indexed citations appearing in a subset of journals and years. It covered 1961 — and then, because preparing it took two years, skipped 1962 and 1963 entirely before resuming in 1964. And what determined whether a citation showed up was the journal and year of the citing article, not the cited scientist's own work.

So picture two scientists, Anna and Ben, identical in every way. Each published a paper that was cited five times. Anna's five citations happened to land in journals and years the SCI indexed; Ben's landed in journals or years it missed — say, citations made in 1962. To a hiring committee in 1969 paging through the SCI, Anna looks like a five-citation scientist and Ben looks like a zero-citation scientist — even though their actual records are identical. Anna's citations are visible; Ben's are invisible. Today, with the complete modern Web of Science, we can see both. Hager, Schwarz, and Waldinger reconstruct exactly which citations were visible to contemporaries and which were not, and then ask a clean question: did the visible citations move careers more than the invisible ones?

If metrics were just noise — if the academic community already knew who was good — visible and invisible citations should matter equally. If, instead, the act of measurement reveals something the market couldn't otherwise see, visible citations should matter more. The beauty of the design is that the invisible citations are a built-in control group: equally real, equally predictive of quality, but hidden from the people making decisions at the time.

The market started pricing citations

The raw picture moves first. Between 1956 and 1969 — across the SCI's introduction — the correlation between a scientist's citation rank and the rank of their department rose by 61%. Over the very same years, the correlation between a scientist's publication count and their department rank fell by 46%. Hiring was visibly shifting its weight off "how much have you published" and onto "how much are you cited" — exactly what you'd expect if a new citation metric had entered the room.

Then the causal core. Comparing visible against invisible citations in the same scientists, the authors find that visible citations are three to four times as predictive of which department a scientist ends up in. In concrete terms: a scientist with a 10-percentile-higher visible citation count landed at a department roughly 3 percentiles higher in the rankings; the same edge in invisible citations bought only about 1 percentile. The difference is the part of a scientist's record that became legible, and the gap between visible and invisible holds in every specification the authors try.

This is where most "metrics matter" stories would stop, but the careful work is in ruling out the alternatives, and it's worth one paragraph because it's what makes the result causal rather than suggestive. Maybe the SCI just indexed better journals, so visible citations were higher-quality? No: indexed and non-indexed journals had near-identical average impact factors (0.83 versus 0.86). Maybe the visible citations simply came later and so mattered more? The authors build a placebo from citations in SCI-indexed journals but from years the SCI never covered (1956–60, and the 1962–63 gap) — citations that would have been visible had they only fallen in a covered year. These "pseudovisible" citations behave just like invisible ones. It's not the journal, and it's not the timing. It's whether the citation was actually in front of the people making decisions. On the confidence ladder, this is about as good as historical causal inference gets: I'd call the core result convincing.

Finding talent the market couldn't see

Why did visibility matter? The mechanism turns out to be specifically about information, and the authors pin it down with a cut that is hard to argue with.

First, they trace two channels. Scientists with more visible citations became less likely to leave academia (a 10-percentile edge cut the exit rate by about 5%) and more likely to move up to a better-ranked department. Invisible citations did neither. Then comes the telling part. The move-up effect appears only for moves to departments that were geographically far away (more than 100 km) and that had never cited the scientist's own work before the index appeared. Where a hiring department was nearby, or had already encountered the scientist's work, the metric added nothing — those committees already had the information. The citation count only mattered when it was telling people something they couldn't otherwise have known. That is the signature of a genuine information friction being relaxed, not of metrics mechanically rewarding the well-connected.

This fits a much broader pattern across labor markets: a cheap, credible, legible signal of quality reallocates opportunity toward people the market was failing to see. When Heller and Kessler (2024) gave randomly-chosen youth a credible recommendation letter, their employment the following year rose by 3 percentage points. When an online labor platform added standardized, verified skill scores, the workers who gained most were exactly the ones employers had been overlooking — applicants from less developed countries (Agrawal, Lacetera, and Lyons, 2016). The same logic shows up in markets with nothing to do with labor: when John Moody started rating railroad bonds in 1909 — using only public information and carrying no legal force — the ratings still tightened bid-ask spreads and re-priced the bonds, because a legible score reduced what buyers didn't know (Bernstein, Frydman, and Hilt, 2026). A citation count is the academic version of a credit rating. Its first job is not to corrupt; it's to reveal.

The people the network overlooked

If metrics reveal hidden quality, the biggest winners should be the people whose quality was most hidden. They are, and this is the heart of the paper.

The benefit of citation visibility was concentrated at the top of the distribution — and, within the top, it went disproportionately to scientists who were stuck in low-ranked departments. The authors call them "hidden stars": highly-cited researchers whom the pre-metric system had stranded somewhere beneath their ability. A top-decile-cited scientist in a low-ranked department gained far more from citation visibility than an equally-cited scientist already in an elite one. The metric was, in effect, a search beam for talent the prestige network had missed.

You can see why they were missed by looking at who they were. Among the field's genuine stars (the top 5% by citations), those sitting in elite departments were the pedigreed ones: 38% held a PhD from a top-10 department. Among the hidden stars in lower-ranked departments, only about 18% did. The old system rewarded where you trained; the citation count rewarded what you'd done. The paper's emblem is Hans Hecht, a Swiss-born physician who fled the Nazi regime, rebuilt a career through Wayne and Utah, and — in the mid-1960s, as his citation record became visible — was hired by the University of Chicago. A contemporary put it plainly: the SCI was "especially useful to find people who would otherwise be overlooked."

The hidden stars were also about twice as likely to be women as the stars in elite departments. I want to be careful here, and so are the authors: with so few women in 1960s science, that difference is not statistically significant, so treat it as suggestive rather than established. But it points exactly where a large literature says it should. Women's contributions are systematically under-credited by the very recognition machinery a citation count reads from. Marlène Koffi (2025) finds that the papers citation-tracing algorithms omit are 15–20% more likely to be female-authored, and that a woman's paper must rank about 20 percentiles higher in measured innovativeness to earn the same citations as a man's. The cleanest number comes from Ross and coauthors (2022): holding the actual lab contribution fixed, women are 13% less likely to be named as authors, and 58% less likely to be named on a patent, for work they demonstrably did. (Women are under-recognized further up the ladder too — less likely to be honored by the Econometric Society than their records warrant, and given less credit for co-authored work.) When recognition is this leaky, a blunt, pedigree-blind count can — imperfectly — let some of the overlooked be seen. (Among the very top scientists, the authors find some evidence — significant only at the 10% level — that the boost was larger for women and Jewish researchers; across all minority scientists on average, it washes out.)

But the same metric feeds the Matthew effect

Now the honest objection, and it's a serious one. The thing that makes a citation count useful for finding hidden talent — that it concentrates attention on the highly-cited — is the same thing that makes it dangerous. Visibility doesn't just sort people into better departments; it compounds.

Hager and coauthors find this directly. A 10-percentile-higher visible citation rank made a scientist about 5.8% more likely to be promoted to full professor, and 19% more likely to win an NSF grant — while invisible citations did neither. Being seen to be cited brought not just a better job but more money and more rank.

To feel the size of that, do the arithmetic. The average scientist here had about a 71% chance of making full professor over these years, and promotion probability rose roughly 4.1 points for every 10 percentiles of visible citations. So take two scientists with identical underlying records, one sitting at the median of visible citations and one at the 90th percentile — a 40-percentile gap. That gap is worth about 16 percentage points of promotion probability: call it 79% against 63%, on either side of the 71% average. Sixteen points of a career, bought not by doing better work but by having the same work counted.

The sociologist Robert Merton named this in 1968: the Matthew effect in science, after the gospel line "for unto every one that hath shall be given." Eminent scientists accrue disproportionate credit; the unknown get disproportionately little. And it is not just folklore. Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt (2018) compared early-career researchers who landed just above and just below a funding cutoff — all but identical in quality — and found that the winners went on to raise more than twice as much later funding (an extra €180,000 over eight years) and were 47% more likely to make full professor (28% versus 19%). A coin-flip of early visibility snowballs. A related worry runs through Azoulay, Fons-Rosen, and Graff Zivin's (2019) finding that fields visibly open up to outsiders only after a star dies: incumbency, once established, holds talent out.

So the same legibility cuts both ways. It widens the gate for a hidden star and it greases the cumulative advantage of whoever is already on top. The 1960s data show the first effect dominating at the introduction of metrics. There's no guarantee it still does.

Which is the deeper caveat, and the one the title's optimism has to survive. This is a story about the introduction of a metric into a world where information was scarce — where a citation count was the only legible quality signal a far-off committee could get. The marginal value of revealing that number was enormous precisely because nothing else revealed it. That tells us very little about the marginal value of piling more metric pressure onto today's saturated system, where every scientist's h-index is one click away and the incentive to game it is everywhere. DORA and CoARA are reacting to that second world, not the first. It is entirely coherent to believe both that the first citation index improved the allocation of talent and that the thousandth metric is making things worse.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That making citations visible causally improved the matching of scientists to departments is well identified — the visible-versus-invisible design, with its placebo and its geography cut, is a genuinely clean natural experiment, and I'd bet on the core result. That the mechanism is reduced information frictions — metrics revealing talent the market couldn't otherwise see — is convincingly shown and fits a wide pattern of evidence from other markets. That the biggest beneficiaries were hidden stars in lesser departments is solid; that they were disproportionately women is suggestive but not statistically nailed down, though it lines up with everything we know about how women's work is under-credited. And that the very same visibility fed Matthew effects in promotions and grants is also well identified — and is the honest price of admission.

So: counting citations helped science find its hidden stars. Measurement, at its birth, mostly fixed a misallocation and widened the gate — it let a refugee physician in Utah be discovered by Chicago, and may well have let a few overlooked women be seen. That is not a defense of metric saturation, gaming, or the tyranny the critics rightly decry. It's a reminder that the problem was never measuring science. It was forgetting what measurement was for.

What we don't know

  • Does the democratization survive more power? The women-among-hidden-stars result is the most important and the least certain in the paper, hostage to how few women were in 1960s science. Replications in larger or later settings — other countries, other decades, other metrics — would tell us whether legibility reliably helps the under-credited or just did so once.
  • Where does the curve turn? Hager et al. show metrics revealing information at their introduction; the backlash describes metrics distorting behavior at saturation. Nobody has cleanly located the point in between where a measure stops informing and starts being gamed. That, not "are metrics good or bad," is the policy-relevant question.
  • What's the net welfare sign today? Better allocation is a benefit; gaming, narrowing, and runaway Matthew effects are costs. We have causal estimates of the upside in one historical case and vivid descriptions of the downside now, but no ledger that nets them in a modern system.
  • What's the right kind of visibility? If the value of a metric is that it reveals overlooked quality, the design question is which signals reveal the most while inviting the least gaming. A citation count surfaced hidden stars in 1965; it's an open, and answerable, question what a 2026 version — built to be informative rather than targetable — would look like.

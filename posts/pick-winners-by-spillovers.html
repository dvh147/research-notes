"Governments can't pick winners." It is one of the most durable slogans in economic policy, and it travels with a gallery of cautionary tales: the supersonic Concorde that never turned a profit, the solar manufacturer Solyndra that burned through roughly half a billion dollars in U.S. federal loan guarantees before filing for bankruptcy in 2011. The lesson usually drawn is that public support for research should be neutral. Spread it thin. Offer the same R&D tax credit to everyone, treat every firm and every field alike, and let the market decide who deserves to win.

This post is about why that lesson is half right and half badly wrong — and about a growing body of work that tries to salvage the good half. The case rests on a single uncomfortable fact: a dollar of research at one firm, or in one technology, can be worth several times as much to everyone else as the same dollar somewhere else. If that is true, then "be neutral" is not a way of avoiding a choice. It is a choice — to fund the high-spillover dollar and the low-spillover dollar in exactly the same proportion, which is almost certainly not the proportion that does the most social good.

So here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence and an honest accounting of where it is strong and where it is still a hope: because the social returns to research are both large and very uneven, targeting innovation subsidies at the firms and technologies whose knowledge spills over the most should deliver materially higher social returns than spreading the same budget evenly. It is a case for picking winners — but by a measurable criterion, spillovers, rather than by a minister's hunch. Whether that case survives contact with the real world turns on two things, measurement and politics, and I will spend the second half of the post on exactly those objections.

The prize: a dollar of research, several dollars of benefit

Start with why anyone subsidizes research at all. When a firm invents something, it captures only part of the value — competitors learn from it, workers carry ideas to new employers, follow-on inventors build on it. Economists call the gap between what the inventor keeps (the private return) and what society gets (the social return) a knowledge spillover, and it is the textbook reason private R&D is undersupplied.

The gap is not small, and here the evidence is about as clear as empirical economics gets. The canonical estimate comes from Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013), who untangle the two forces that a firm's R&D exerts on its neighbors: a positive technology spillover and a negative business-stealing effect from product-market rivals. Using U.S. firm panel data — and, crucially, identifying the effect off changes in federal and state R&D tax credits rather than mere correlation — they find the technology spillover dominates, so that the gross social return to R&D is "at least twice" the private return. Their headline marginal figures are a social return around 55% against a private return around 21%. The socially optimal level of R&D, they conclude, is more than twice what firms actually do.

Other work pushes the multiple higher and confirms the lineage. The much-cited Jones and Williams (1998) derive, from a growth model, that optimal R&D spending is something like two to four times actual spending — and they bill that as a conservative lower bound. Two decades on, Lucking, Bloom, and Van Reenen (2019) revisit the question and put the ratio of social to private returns at roughly four to one, up from about three, with the gap widening rather than closing. And on the public-money side, Myers and Lanahan (2022) study U.S. Department of Energy grants and estimate that for every patent a grant recipient produces, about three more are produced by others who benefit from the spillover.

Pin down the order of magnitude, because the rest of the argument leans on it: research throws off social value far in excess of what inventors capture, and the economy does perhaps half to a quarter of the research it ideally should. That is the prize. The policy question is not whether to subsidize research — almost every economist says yes — but how to spend the subsidy budget so the prize is largest.

Not all research dollars are equal

The neutral approach implicitly assumes the spillover is roughly the same wherever the money lands. It isn't — and this is the empirical hinge of the whole argument.

Look first across firms. The same Bloom-Schankerman-Van Reenen study contains a result that should unsettle a lot of innovation policy: smaller firms generate lower social returns, because they tend to operate in technological "niches" where fewer others can build on what they do. The authors say so bluntly — that policymakers should reconsider their strong support for higher R&D tax-credit rates aimed at small firms. Much of the developed world does exactly the opposite, offering its most generous credits to small and young firms. On a spillover criterion, that reflex may be backwards. Relatedly, Knott, Posen, and Wu (2009) show that spillover "pools" are asymmetric and directional rather than a common reservoir every firm draws from equally, and Zacchia (2020) finds a firm's social return depends heavily on its position in the network of inventors. Who you are, and whom you are connected to, changes how much your research helps everyone else.

Now look across technologies, which is where the sharpest recent evidence lives. The seed for this post — Guillard, Martin, Mohnen, Thomas, and Verhoeven (2022) — builds a spillover measure they call P-Rank. The idea is Google's PageRank applied to the patent citation network: a patent is a web page, a citation is a hyperlink, and an invention's external value is the sum of the (private) values of everything that cites it, directly or indirectly, traced through the whole network. Run that on worldwide patent data and two things jump out. First, spillovers are concentrated in what the authors christen Hidden Giants — inventions that score far higher on P-Rank than on a plain forward-citation count, so their importance is obscured by the standard yardstick. Hidden Giants are responsible for 56% of all the external value created in their data, and outnumber the mirror-image "Illusory Giants" (over-rated by citation counts) by about 1.7 to 1. University patents are disproportionately Hidden Giants. Second, the marginal social return to subsidizing research differs enormously by field: above 40% in areas like wireless, clean energy, AI, and robotics, against roughly 15% in civil engineering, machine tools, and mechanical elements.

Sit with that range. If a field-level marginal social return can be 40% in one place and 15% in another, then the field you put the next subsidy dollar in matters more than almost any other design choice. The best evidence — firm-level and technology-level, from independent measures — says spillovers are not a uniform fog hanging over the economy. They are lumpy. And if they are lumpy, neutrality is leaving the lumps unexploited.

Spreading it evenly leaves money on the table — and sometimes burns it

You might think a uniform subsidy is at worst harmlessly blunt: you don't capture the extra value from the high-spillover dollars, but no harm done. The theory says it can be worse than that.

The sharpest warning comes from Acemoglu, Akcigit, Alp, Bloom, and Kerr (2018). In their estimated model of U.S. firm dynamics, broad subsidies to incumbent firms' R&D are actively counterproductive, because they prop up low-productivity firms that should be exiting and hogging scarce skilled researchers. Taxing the continued operation of incumbents, by contrast, raises welfare by around 1.4% — by pushing low-types out and freeing talent for high-type firms to use. The instrument most politicians reach for (subsidize the incumbents) is, in this model, the wrong sign. König, Storesletten, Song, and Zilibotti (2022) find a related non-monotonicity in Chinese data: a moderate across-the-board R&D subsidy lifts productivity growth, but a very large one slows it, because it pulls the wrong firms into innovating. And Akcigit, Hanley, and Serrano-Velarde (2021) show that uniform research subsidies tend to over-subsidize applied research at the expense of basic research, where the spillovers are larger — so the untargeted instrument quietly tilts the economy toward the lower-spillover activity.

Even when uniform subsidies aren't harmful, they may be feeble. Atkeson and Burstein (2019) find only modest scope to raise aggregate productivity over a 20-year horizon with reasonably sized uniform innovation subsidies (though they note the welfare gains can still be meaningful). The recurring theme across these very different models: the composition of who does the extra research is what matters, and a uniform subsidy is precisely the instrument that leaves composition alone.

A fair caveat before we go on: this section is mostly structural models — estimated and disciplined by micro-data, but models. Read it as "the best current theory, calibrated to real firms" rather than a clean natural experiment: it says untargeted support underperforms in theory, not that targeting works in practice. For that, we need the affirmative case — and then the objections.

What targeting actually looks like

If neutrality is the wrong default, what is the right one? The theory of who to target is surprisingly well developed.

For firms, Akcigit, Hanley, and Stantcheva (2022) solve for the optimal R&D subsidy when firms differ in research productivity, that productivity is private information, and their research spills over. High-research-productivity firms earn disproportionately higher returns to R&D, so the optimum leans toward them — and, neatly, a simple nonlinear schedule that offers a lower marginal subsidy at higher R&D levels does almost as well as the unrestricted optimum. For networks, there is the "key player" tradition: Ballester, Calvó-Armengol, and Zenou (2006) show that the agent worth targeting is the one with the highest intercentrality — the one whose own activity and whose knock-on effect on others are jointly largest — which is generally not the biggest player. Galeotti, Golub, and Goyal (2020) generalize this: when efforts reinforce each other, the optimal intervention loads on the most globally connected parts of the network, and for a large budget it collapses to essentially a single dimension. The common message is that "target the obvious big incumbent" is rarely the answer; target the unit with the largest system-wide footprint.

How much is at stake from getting this right? Here the Hidden Giants paper supplies a number. Feeding its field-level spillover estimates into a model of how subsidies induce marginal research, the authors compute that targeting a fixed budget at the high-return technology areas yields social returns at least 40% higher than spreading the same money uniformly within a country — and that coordinating field-level subsidies across OECD countries could add another 30% on top.

A toy version makes the mechanism legible. Imagine a government with two units of subsidy and two fields: wireless, where a marginal subsidy dollar throws off about 40 cents of social value, and civil engineering, where it throws off about 15. Split the money evenly — the neutral policy — and two dollars buy you 55 cents of social value. Now tilt toward wireless. Because the fields start so far apart, even a partial reallocation buys a lot: the optimum keeps shifting money toward wireless until the marginal returns equalize (they fall as you crowd more research into a field), and that equalizing point sits well to the wireless side of 50-50. The gap between "55 cents from splitting evenly" and "what you get from tilting" is the prize from targeting — and with real fields ranging from 15% to 40%-plus, the paper's estimate that the prize is north of 40% is not surprising. The key is the dispersion in marginal returns; the wider it is, the more neutrality costs you.

That is the affirmative case: large prize, uneven spillovers, a known recipe for whom to target, and a model-based estimate that the recipe beats neutrality by a lot. Now the two reasons to be careful.

Objection! You can't measure spillovers well enough

This is the serious objection, and I want to give it its due rather than wave it away. Targeting is only as good as the spillover measure, and spillovers are slippery.

They go to unexpected places. The Myers and Lanahan study of energy grants found that many of the spillover patents landed in technological areas substantially different from the ones the grants targeted, and that only about 60% of the spillovers stayed inside the United States — so even a well-aimed subsidy leaks across both technological and national borders in ways the targeter didn't plan. The measurement tools are imperfect too. P-Rank rests on patent citations (much valuable knowledge is never patented) and weights them by stock-market-based private values (which can reflect monopoly power as much as productivity). The Hidden Giants authors are admirably explicit about all of this, listing unmeasured knowledge, monopoly rents in their value weights, and the instability of patent-system institutions among their caveats, and calling their evidence on whether spillover rankings stay stable over time merely "preliminary."

So: should we conclude that measurement defeats targeting? I don't think so, for two reasons. First, the bar is not perfection but improvement over the status quo, and the status quo is either no targeting at all or targeting on cruder signals. The whole point of the Hidden Giants result is that the standard metric — citation counts — systematically understates the best targets; a measure that corrects even part of that error points the money in a better direction than no measure does. Second, the recommendation is to tilt toward broad fields and classes of firm, not to bet the budget on individual projects. Targeting a portfolio of high-spillover technologies is far more forgiving of measurement noise than picking single winners. Still, I'll put this on the honest side of the ledger: the measurement problem is the binding constraint on how far you should push targeting, and anyone who tells you spillovers can be measured cleanly is overselling.

Objection! This is just "picking winners" by another name

The other objection is political, and it is the one that gave us the slogan we started with. Hand a government discretion to steer research money toward "high-spillover" firms and you have handed it discretion to steer money toward well-connected firms. The history of industrial policy is littered with the results.

The risk is real and recently quantified. Akcigit, Baslandze, and Lotti (2023) show that market leaders are more likely to be politically connected but less likely to innovate; connections buy survival and growth but not productivity, and at the aggregate level the gains from connections do not offset the losses from the reduced churn they cause. Discretion captured by incumbents is exactly the failure mode the skeptics warn about.

But — and this is the crux — the answer to "discretion gets captured" is to target by a rule rather than by discretion, which is the entire spirit of a measurable spillover criterion. The cleanest evidence here comes from Cingano, Palomba, Pinotti, and Rettore (2025), who study a subsidy program that ranked applicants partly on objective rules and partly on local politicians' discretion, and exploit the funding cutoff as a regression discontinuity. Their result is almost too on-the-nose: relying only on the objective criteria would cut the cost per job created by about 11%, while relying only on political discretion would raise it by about 42%. Rules beat hunches, and by a wide margin. (Two honest hedges: this program subsidized investment and jobs, not R&D spillovers specifically, so it is the closest analogue rather than a direct test; and "objective rule" there is not the same rule as "spillover potential.")

The supporting evidence rhymes. Criscuolo, Martin, Overman, and Van Reenen (2019), exploiting rule changes in a European subsidy map as an instrument, find that the targeting margin is decisive: a 10-percentage-point rise in the maximum investment subsidy raised manufacturing employment by about 10% — but the effect came entirely from small firms, while large firms pocketed the money without increasing activity. Santoleri, Mina, Di Minin, and Martelli (2024), using a regression-discontinuity design on the EU's SME Instrument, find that what works is the money (relaxing financial constraints), not a certifying "quality stamp" without funding. And the broader reassessment of industrial policy has turned cautiously favorable: Juhász, Lane, and Rodrik (2024) survey the new empirical wave and conclude it offers "a more positive take" than the old "governments can't pick winners" consensus, while Lane (2025) shows South Korea's targeted heavy-industry drive expanded the targeted sectors, spilled downstream through the input-output network, and persisted after the policy ended. None of this says discretion is safe. It says a disciplined, rule-based, spillover-weighted target is a different animal from a minister picking a national champion — and the evidence we have favors the rule.

For what it's worth — and now I am extrapolating past the cleanest evidence, so treat this as my read rather than a theorem — I think the "can't pick winners" slogan has done real damage by collapsing two different things: picking winners by politics, which tends to fail, and picking winners by a transparent, auditable criterion, which we have barely tried. The spillover criterion is attractive precisely because it is the kind of rule you can publish, contest, and check.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. The premises are strong: that research throws off social returns far above private ones is about as well established as anything in the field (multiple designs, replications, identification off real tax changes); that those spillovers are lumpy across firms and technologies is fairly convincing, resting on several independent measures that agree; and that uniform subsidies underperform a targeted alternative is solid in theory and supported by a clutch of estimated models. On those three, I am comfortable saying the reflexive "be neutral" stance is too strong: the economics of spillovers implies that good innovation policy should weight spillovers, and that a disciplined criterion beats both even spreading and political favoritism.

Now the bound. The headline that targeting beats uniform by 40% or more is a model-based counterfactual, not a measured outcome — nobody has yet run the experiment of subsidizing firms by their spillover potential and watching the realized social return roll in. The cleanest causal evidence we have is on the premises (the size and unevenness of spillovers; rules beating discretion), not on the full targeted-versus-uniform comparison. And the practical payoff is capped by the two objections: it is worth only as much as our spillover measures are trustworthy and our institutions are capture-resistant. So my confidence ladder runs high on "spillovers are big and uneven," moderate on "targeting beats neutral," and deliberately humble on "a real government could capture most of the modeled gain." Pick winners by their spillovers — but with a measuring tape in one hand and a rulebook in the other.

What we don't know

  • The missing experiment. No field trial or quasi-experiment has targeted firms by spillover potential and measured the realized social return against a uniform control. That is the study that would move this from "promising theory" to "established."
  • Do the rankings hold still? Targeting on past spillover patterns only works if the high-spillover fields stay high-spillover. The evidence that they do is, by the seed authors' own admission, preliminary.
  • Does the gain survive general equilibrium? Concentrating subsidies on high-spillover research could bid up the wages of scarce researchers and crowd out research elsewhere. Some of the modeled gains may be partial-equilibrium illusions — and Acemoglu and coauthors' result is a reminder that the margin you target can flip the sign.
  • Is private value the right weight? P-Rank weights spillovers by market value, which says nothing about environmental or distributional externalities and may reward monopoly. The "best" technologies to subsidize on a broader welfare measure could differ.
  • Can a government actually commit to the rule? The whole case depends on targeting by criterion rather than connection. Whether a real political system can bind itself to the rule — or whether "spillover potential" simply becomes the next thing to lobby over — is an open, and decidedly non-technical, question.

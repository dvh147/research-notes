In 2001 the U.S. Patent Office granted patent 6,329,919 to IBM. The invention was a "system and method for providing reservations for restroom use" — software to let airline passengers reserve a spot in the queue for the lavatory. It is a real patent. It was also, by the only test that ultimately matters, close to worthless: IBM eventually stopped paying the modest annual fee required to keep it alive, and the patent lapsed. Nobody was willing to spend even renewal money to hold onto it.

That same year, the Patent Office granted patent 6,317,722 to Amazon, covering the use of electronic shopping carts to generate personalized recommendations — the now-ubiquitous "customers who bought this also bought." When the patent issued, Amazon's stock jumped 34% over two days, adding roughly $900 million to the company's market value. One patent. Nine hundred million dollars.

Both of these are in the same dataset, counted the same way, each "one patent." If you summarize American invention by counting patents — or by reporting the value of the average patent — you are averaging the airplane toilet and the recommendation engine and reporting the result as though it described a typical invention. It describes nothing. The central fact about the private value of patents is that it lives almost entirely in the right tail: most patents are worth almost nothing, and a tiny fraction are worth a fortune. This isn't a quirk of one database or one clever measure. It is one of the most robust regularities in the economics of innovation, and it should change how you read every patent count you ever see again.

(Those two examples come straight from Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman's 2017 study in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, which I'll lean on heavily below.)

Four ways to price a patent, and they all agree

The reason to believe the skew is real — and not an artifact of how someone happened to measure value — is that economists have priced patents four genuinely different ways, with four different kinds of measurement error, and gotten the same shape every time.

Renewals. Patents must be renewed periodically, for a fee that rises over the patent's life. A rational owner pays the fee only if the patent is still worth more than the fee. Watching when owners let patents lapse therefore reveals a lower bound on what each patent is worth — the logic of treating a patent as a financial option, developed by Ariel Pakes and Mark Schankerman in the 1980s. Schankerman's 1998 study of French renewals in the RAND Journal of Economics found the value distribution "highly skewed," and concluded that patent protection is "a significant but not the major source of private returns to R&D."

Surveys. You can simply ask the people who own or invented a patent what it is worth. Harhoff, Scherer, and Vopel (2003) did this for a sample of valuable German patents, and Giuri and 18 coauthors (2007) did it at continental scale, surveying the inventors behind thousands of European patents about the minimum price at which they would have sold the rights.

Stock-market reactions. Kogan and coauthors read each patent's value off the stock market: when a public firm is granted a patent, the bump in its share price over the next three days — filtered for noise — is the market's estimate of that patent's private value. They did this for 1.8 million U.S. patents granted between 1926 and 2010.

Auction prices. Most directly of all, you can look at what patents actually sell for. Fischer and Leidinger (2014) used real transaction prices from Ocean Tomo's patent auctions.

Renewals, surveys, asset prices, auction prices. These methods share no common machinery and fail in different ways. And every one of them finds the same thing: a left wall of near-worthless patents, a long thin tail of enormously valuable ones, and almost nothing that looks "typical." When four independent instruments give the same reading, you stop worrying that the reading is an instrument error.

How skewed is "skewed"?

Skew is one of those words that has lost its force from overuse, so let me put numbers on it.

The single most useful summary comes from Scherer and Harhoff (2000), who assembled eight separate datasets of returns to inventions and innovations — German patents, U.S. university licenses, startup-company outcomes, and more. Across all eight, the top 10% of projects captured between 48% and 93% of the total value. Not 60/40, not even the folkloric 80/20 — in several of their samples it was closer to 90/10. A tenth of the inventions were essentially the whole game.

Now look at the gap between the average and the typical. In a symmetric distribution the mean and the median sit on top of each other. In the patent-value distribution they are wildly far apart, because the mean is dragged upward by the tail:

  • Bessen's (2008) renewal-based estimates put the mean value of a U.S. patent (1991 cohort) at roughly $78,000 but the median at only about $7,000 — the average patent is worth about eleven times the typical one.
  • Kogan and coauthors, pricing patents off the stock market, call their distribution "fat-tailed" in so many words and report a median patent value of about $3.2 million (in 1982 dollars), with the mean far above it.
  • For European patents, Gambardella, Harhoff, and Verspagen (2008) estimate a mean above €3 million, a median about a tenth of that (~€300,000), and a mode — the single most common value — of just a few thousand euros.

And the left wall is crowded. In the European inventor survey, about 68% of all patents were worth less than €1 million to their owners — many far less. A large share of patents, in the inventor-survey work of Torrisi and coauthors (2016), are never used internally or licensed out at all: "sleeping" patents filed to block rivals or just in case, that do nothing and earn nothing. The median patent isn't a modest success. It's mostly a near-miss.

Here is a toy version of the whole distribution, deliberately crude. Imagine a firm files 100 patents. Ninety of them turn out to be worth $10,000 each. Nine are worth $1 million each. One is worth $100 million. The total value is $0.9M + $9M + $100M ≈ $110 million. The mean patent is "worth" $1.1 million — a number that describes not one single patent in the portfolio. The median is $10,000. And the top 1% of patents — that one blockbuster — holds 91% of the value. That stylized split is almost exactly what Scherer and Harhoff found in real data. The average is a centaur: an arithmetic creature that corresponds to nothing you can point at.

Why counting patents misleads

Once you internalize the shape, a lot of standard innovation talk starts to look broken.

"Company A filed 500 patents last year, Company B filed 300, so A is more innovative." Maybe. But if B's 300 includes one molecule that anchors a $5 billion drug franchise and A's 500 are defensive filings around an aging product line, the count has the sign exactly backwards. Counting patents treats a lottery ticket and a winning jackpot as the same unit — because at the moment of filing, before the market votes, they are the same unit. The patent system is designed to be cheap to enter and expensive to keep, precisely so that the filer doesn't have to know in advance which is which.

The averages mislead in a subtler way. Because the mean is governed by the tail, any average value of innovation is really a statement about a handful of blockbusters wearing a disguise. Report "the average patent generates $X," and a reader hears "patents typically generate around $X." But there is no "typically" here. The distribution has no meaningful center of mass. A back-of-the-envelope from the numbers above: if the top 10% holds ~85% of value, then removing the single most valuable patent from a large portfolio can cut the portfolio's total value by more than dropping the entire bottom half. Means assume that observations are interchangeable and that errors wash out across many of them. In a fat-tailed world, they don't wash out — they accumulate in the tail, where a single observation can outweigh thousands.

This is why "how many patents did we produce" is very nearly the wrong question. The right question is "did we catch anything in the tail," and the count barely speaks to it.

The citation trap

If raw counts are bad, the field's favorite fix is to weight patents by how often they are later cited, on the logic that important patents get cited more. Citation-weighting is a real improvement, and citations are correlated with value: Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg (2005) found that an extra citation on a firm's patents is associated with roughly a 3% bump in market value, and Kogan and coauthors put the value of one extra citation, near the median, at somewhere between 0.1% and 3.2%. Harhoff, Narin, Scherer, and Vopel (1999) estimated that a single citation around the median was worth, on average, more than $1 million.

But — and this is the catch — citations explain only a small slice of the variation in value. Bessen found that citations "explain little variance" in patent value. Fischer and Leidinger, testing indicators against real auction prices, found that forward citations and patent-family size predicted value but accounted for only a small share of it. The IBM airplane-toilet patent is the cautionary tale: it racked up enough forward citations to sit in the top 20% of its cohort scientifically, even as its owner judged it not worth a renewal fee and let it die. Citations measure something — roughly, scientific or technical influence — but that something is not the same as private value, and the two can point in opposite directions for any individual patent.

So citation-weighting blunts exactly the skew it is meant to capture. It pulls the worthless-but-cited patents up and leaves some valuable-but-uncited ones down, and it still leaves most of the value-variation unexplained. The honest summary is that we can see the tail exists far more confidently than we can predict which patents will be in it.

Objection: isn't everything skewed?

A fair skeptic will say: incomes are skewed, firm sizes are skewed, city sizes are skewed — skew is the default for economic variables, so why is this news?

Two answers. First, the degree is the news. "Skewed" can mean the top 10% earns 30%, as with income, or it can mean the top 10% holds 90%, as with patents. The patent case is at the extreme end, extreme enough that the ordinary statistical reflexes — report a mean, run a regression on the level — actively mislead rather than merely simplify.

Second, the shape of the tail itself is contested, and the contest matters. Harhoff and coauthors found their data fit a lognormal distribution reasonably well. But Silverberg and Verspagen (2007), applying extreme-value statistics to the very top of the distribution, argued that the upper tail is better described by a Pareto (power-law) curve — which is heavier than lognormal, meaning even more of the action is concentrated in even fewer patents than a lognormal would predict. This is not hairsplitting. A sufficiently heavy Pareto tail can have an unstable or even ill-defined mean, in which case computing an "average patent value" isn't just misleading, it's close to meaningless — your estimate of the average keeps climbing as your sample gets bigger, because you keep catching new monsters in the tail. Whether patent value is "merely" lognormal or genuinely power-law is, to me, the most important open question in this whole literature.

And note which way the remaining measurement errors push. Renewal models can't see the top of the tail, because the renewal fee is trivial next to a blockbuster's value, so a $10,000 patent and a $100-million patent both just "get renewed" and look alike at the top. Kogan's measure covers only public firms. Surveys depend on what inventors are willing to say. Each of these errors truncates or compresses the tail — which means the true skew is, if anything, greater than what any single study measures. The evidence we have is a lower bound on how extreme the reality is.

What this changes

Let me flag that the following is more a reading of the evidence than a finding in it.

If value lives in the tail, then the most consequential mistake an innovation policymaker or an R&D manager can make is to judge a program by its hit rate. Scherer and Harhoff drew exactly this moral a quarter-century ago: programs that produce "many economic failures" should not be judged failures, because in a skew world the failures are the price of admission to the tail. You buy a lot of lottery tickets to hold one jackpot, and the discarded tickets are not waste — they are the cost of the jackpot. Their more unsettling point: the skew is so severe that you cannot diversify the residual risk away, even across a large portfolio of projects. With ordinary risks, a hundred independent bets average out to near-certainty. With these risks, a hundred bets still leaves you hostage to whether any one of them hits.

That reframes a number of debates. It is part of why "picking winners" is so hard and why funders who can tolerate a long string of failures — and resist the temptation to cut a program after a barren stretch — may systematically outperform those who can't. It cautions against management dashboards that reward patent counts, and against evaluations that penalize a research unit for a few quiet years. And it means that the social and private payoff to innovation policy will, like the underlying inventions, be dominated by a few cases — so the right evaluation horizon is long, and the right denominator is "did we enable any tail events," not "what was our average return per dollar."

Bottom line

The private value of patents is about as right-skewed as economic quantities get. A small fraction of patents — on the order of the top 10% — accounts for the large majority of total value, often 80% to more than 90% of it; the median patent is worth a few thousand to a few million dollars while the top is worth hundreds of times more; and the most common patent is worth almost nothing. I hold this with high confidence, because four methods that fail in different ways all agree, and because their errors mostly run toward understating the skew. The practical corollary — that patent counts and average values are poor descriptions of inventive output, and worse targets for policy or strategy — follows directly and I'd defend it nearly as strongly.

What I would not claim is a precise number for "the value of the average patent." That number is real only to the extent the tail is well-behaved, and the tail may not be. The level is uncertain; the shape is not.

What we don't know

  • How heavy is the tail, really? Lognormal or Pareto? The answer decides whether an "average patent value" is a merely-fragile statistic or a meaningless one, and the literature hasn't settled it.
  • Can we spot the tail in advance? Citations correlate with value but explain little of it, and may relate to value non-monotonically. We can confidently say the tail exists; we are much worse at saying which patents are in it before the market votes. (That is its own question, and a humbling one for anyone promising to "fund the best.")
  • Does the skew of the private value match the skew of the social value? A patent's value to its owner and its value to society are not the same thing, and a blockbuster by one measure can be modest by the other. Whether the tails line up is largely open.
  • Is the skew getting more or less extreme over time? With value data now spanning nearly a century, this is answerable, and worth answering.

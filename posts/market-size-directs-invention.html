Between 1975 and 1999, the world's pharmaceutical industry brought 1,393 new drugs to market. Sixteen of them -- about one in ninety -- were for tropical diseases and tuberculosis, even though those diseases were then, and are now, among the largest killers on the planet (Trouiller et al. 2002). A later count covering 2000 to 2011 found the share had crept up to four percent, and that of 336 genuinely new molecules, exactly four were for neglected diseases (Pedrique et al. 2013).

It is tempting to read those numbers as a moral failure -- callous companies ignoring the sick poor. I think that reading is mostly wrong, and the right one is more unsettling. The numbers are what you get when invention obeys a simple economic law: it flows toward markets that can pay. The sick poor are not a small market because they are few. They are a small market because they are poor.

This post is about that law and its evidence. The claim is narrow: the size and profitability of a market causally pulls the direction of invention -- when expected demand for a class of products rises, R&D and new products follow -- and the cleanest demonstrations come from medicine, where you can watch the drugs chase the dollars. I will argue the claim is well established for the direction of innovation, more modest for its overall rate, and that its real sting is the corollary: because demand means purchasing power, not need, the market quietly decides which human problems get solved. That makes "who can pay" one of the most important and least discussed levers in all of innovation policy. If demand directs invention, then policy that shapes demand -- insurance, procurement, guaranteed markets -- is innovation policy. And the things no one can pay for go uninvented, predictably.

Does demand really pull invention?

The idea that demand drives invention is old, and for a long time it was suspect. In 1966 the economist Jacob Schmookler assembled long time series -- railroads, petroleum refining, building, even horseshoes -- and argued that the amount and direction of inventive activity in a sector tracked the volume of sales and investment there, not some autonomous march of science. Invention, on this "demand-pull" view, was something the market called forth.

The trouble was identification. In 1979 David Mowery and Nathan Rosenberg published a famous review (Mowery and Rosenberg 1979) arguing that the demand-pull studies had fooled themselves. They had confused need with demand -- needs are infinite and shapeless, so they cannot explain why any particular thing gets invented -- and their statistics were close to tautological. Of course you see invention where there is a lot of economic activity; that does not prove the activity caused the invention rather than the other way around. Scientific opportunity, the critics insisted, was doing at least as much of the work.

Settling that argument takes a demand shock that has nothing to do with the science. Daron Acemoglu and Joshua Linn found one in demographics (Acemoglu and Linn 2004). Different age groups buy different drugs; as the American population aged through the late twentieth century, the potential market for some drug categories swelled and for others shrank, for reasons -- the size of birth cohorts decades earlier -- that had nothing to do with any laboratory. Lining those predictable demographic shifts up against the arrival of new drugs, they found a clean effect: a one percent increase in a category's potential market size was followed by a four to six percent increase in the number of new drugs entering it. The drugs followed the customers, and because the customers arrived for reasons outside the lab, this is about as close to a controlled experiment as the economics of innovation gets. It is also the answer to Mowery and Rosenberg: exogenous demographic demand is demand, not need, and it is not a stand-in for scientific opportunity.

The same logic works across borders. Arnaud Costinot and coauthors (Costinot et al. 2019) show that countries become net exporters of precisely the drugs their own demographics make them most prone to need. Demand pulls not just the quantity of invention but its direction -- toward whoever is doing the demanding. (For the broader economics of the industry, Lakdawalla 2018 is a good survey.)

Turning demand on like a tap

Demographics move slowly. The sharper test is whether you can reach over, turn demand on, and watch invention respond. Health policy does this all the time, which makes it a natural laboratory.

Amy Finkelstein studied three American policies that, between 1986 and 1993, abruptly raised the return to particular vaccines: a 1986 law that indemnified makers against injury suits, a 1991 recommendation that all infants be vaccinated against hepatitis B, and 1993 Medicare coverage of flu shots (Finkelstein 2004). Each made a specific vaccine more profitable without making it any more scientifically tractable. The response was large: roughly a 2.5-fold increase in clinical trials for the affected vaccines, with the induced spending accounting for about 70 percent of all subsequent investment in those diseases. Finkelstein even priced the relationship: a one-dollar increase in a vaccine's annual market revenue pulled in about five to six cents of additional investment.

The pattern repeats whenever a government enlarges who can pay. When the United States added prescription-drug coverage for seniors through Medicare Part D in 2006, Margaret Blume-Kohout and Neeraj Sood found that the drug classes most used by the elderly -- the ones whose market Part D had just inflated -- saw a 31 to 33 percent increase in compounds entering preclinical testing and an 18 percent rise in drugs entering clinical trials, climbing toward 38 percent in the following years, relative to classes serving younger patients (Blume-Kohout and Sood 2013).

Most telling, because it speaks to the global stakes, is China. When China rolled out the New Cooperative Medical Scheme -- public health insurance for hundreds of millions of rural residents -- Xuan Zhang and Huihua Nie watched domestic pharmaceutical patents tilt toward exactly the diseases common among the newly insured. Diseases with a ten-percentage-point higher rural-patient share got a 12.4 percent increase in relevant patent applications (Zhang and Nie 2021). Their framing is the thesis of this whole post in a sentence: poor countries innovate little not because they have few people, but because their people have "low levels of income and lack of health insurance coverage." Give the poor purchasing power, and invention turns toward them.

It's the revenue, not the patients

If you have been nodding along, here is the result that sharpens the claim into something less comfortable than "help people get care and you get more cures."

China also ran the experiment in reverse. In 2008 and 2009 it expanded a public vaccination program -- more people, more doses -- but simultaneously imposed price controls on the affected vaccines. Volume up, price down. Hao Geng and Ce Matthew Shi found that new vaccine clinical trials for the policy-affected diseases fell by 83 percent (Geng and Shi 2024). More patients getting vaccinated, far less innovation -- because the thing that fell was revenue.

To see why that is the heart of the matter, strip it to a toy. Imagine two diseases, each afflicting a million people. The sufferers of the first can pay $1,000 a year for a treatment; the sufferers of the second, $5. A company deciding where to point its scientists is choosing between a $1-billion-a-year market and a $5-million one, and it will choose the first essentially every time -- even though the two diseases are equally common and equally worth curing in human terms. "Market size" is patients multiplied by ability to pay. A disease of the poor is a small market no matter how many it kills.

The natural experiments line up like a dose-response curve -- raise the money behind a need and invention appears; cut it and invention vanishes:

Demand shock Setting Effect on innovation
Aging population US drug categories +1% market size -> +4-6% new drugs
Vaccine policies, 1986-93 US vaccines ~2.5x clinical trials; $1 revenue -> 5-6c R&D
Medicare Part D, 2006 US drug classes used by seniors +31-33% preclinical; +18-38% clinical entries
Rural health insurance China, newly covered diseases +12.4% patents
More volume, lower price China vaccines -83% clinical trials

(Geng and Shi are careful, and so should we be: in their case, curbing the wasteful pursuit of me-too vaccines was, for most diseases, fine or even good -- though for one disease with real long-run promise the lost innovation was a genuine cost. More demand-pulled R&D is not automatically more valuable R&D, a caveat I return to below.)

Not just drugs

Pharmaceuticals are the cleanest case because drugs map neatly onto diseases and diseases onto demographics. But if the law is real it should show up wherever the market for an input or an output moves -- and it does.

Run the clock back to the 1860s. When the American Civil War cut off the South's cotton exports, British textile mills were forced onto the lower-quality Indian cotton they had long disdained. Walker Hanlon showed that this input shock redirected invention itself: British engineers developed new machinery specifically to spin the inferior Indian fiber, and -- the striking part -- once those technologies existed, the relative price of Indian cotton climbed back to where it had started, as if the innovation had erased the quality gap (Hanlon 2015). Necessity, here a sudden change in what the market had to work with, was the mother of a very particular kind of invention.

Jump to modern agriculture. Jacob Moscona and Karthik Sastry tracked which crops American researchers improved over the past half-century and found that innovation -- new seed varieties, new patents -- has steadily redirected toward crops increasingly battered by extreme heat. That induced innovation is real insurance: they estimate it has offset about 20 percent of the climate-driven losses in US farmland value since 1960, and might offset 13 percent of projected damage by 2100 (Moscona and Sastry 2023). (Hold on to one line from that paper: their innovation "did not respond to non-US climatic distress." The labs answered the American market and ignored everyone else's weather.) The pattern even held in a centrally planned economy: Justin Yifu Lin found that across Chinese provinces, the ones with more rice acreage -- a bigger internal market for rice technology -- got more rice research and faster adoption of hybrid varieties (Lin 1992).

Textiles in the 1860s, drugs in the 1990s, seeds today, in capitalist and planned economies alike: invention turns toward the market that is growing.

The dark side: invention follows money, not need

Now the corollary, stated without flinching. If invention follows demand, and demand is purchasing power, then the problems of people who cannot pay are, in a precise economic sense, uninteresting to the innovation system -- however severe.

Start with the natural experiment that should have helped and didn't. Through the 1990s and 2000s, the WTO's TRIPS agreement pushed dozens of developing countries to adopt pharmaceutical patents, on the theory that stronger property rights would spur the invention of drugs those countries needed. Margaret Kyle and Anita McGahan checked (Kyle and McGahan 2012). In wealthy countries, more patent protection did go with more drug-development effort. But "the introduction of patents in developing countries has not been followed by greater R&D investment in the diseases that are most prevalent there." A patent is a key to a market; it is worthless where the market itself is too poor to be worth entering. The binding constraint was never the strength of the property right. It was the money behind the demand. Consistent with that, when India was forced to grant drug patents in 2005, Mark Duggan and coauthors found that prices rose only three to six percent and quantities barely moved (Duggan et al. 2016) -- the patent was close to a non-event where the market was poor.

Michael Kremer had laid out the logic a decade earlier: the reason there was (and largely still is) no malaria vaccine, despite malaria killing hundreds of thousands a year, is "simply that the countries affected by these diseases are poor, and cannot afford to pay much" (Kremer 2002). The pipeline numbers from the start of this post are the fingerprints of exactly that: sixteen of 1,393 new drugs for tropical diseases and TB from 1975 to 1999, four percent of products and one percent of new molecules from 2000 to 2011. The most recent industry-wide tally, from the G-FINDER survey, puts total global R&D for all neglected diseases at about $4.2 billion in 2023 -- a small fraction of total pharmaceutical R&D -- and that figure fell, rather than rose, for HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria from the year before (WHO Global Observatory / G-FINDER).

(The familiar shorthand for this is the "10/90 gap" -- the claim that under a tenth of health research addresses the problems causing nine-tenths of the disease burden. It comes from a 1990 assessment, and the exact ratio is now dated and contested; treat it as a vivid historical statistic, not a current measurement. The direction it points, though, is corroborated by everything else here.)

And the penalty is not only that the drugs go uninvented; even when they exist, they may not arrive. Pinelopi Goldberg argues that in poor countries the binding effect of drug patents falls less on prices than on access -- multinationals often decline to enter small, poor markets at all, or enter late and thinly (Goldberg 2010). The poor are skipped twice: first by the inventors choosing what to make, then by the sellers choosing where to go.

It would be comforting to file all this under "a pharmaceutical problem." Moscona and Sastry's agriculture result says otherwise. Their adaptive crop innovation, recall, answered American heat and not anyone else's; they warn plainly that "directed innovation concentrated in only a few places could deepen global disparities." The rich world will invent its way partway out of climate damage and sell the poor world whatever falls out, designed for someone else's fields. The logic even scales to whole economies: Pinelopi Goldberg and Tristan Reed argue that many poor countries are simply too small a market -- given how income is distributed within them -- to support the increasing-returns technologies that drive development, unless trade enlarges the market for them (Goldberg and Reed 2023). The same law that hands Medicare's seniors a wave of new drugs leaves the global poor waiting on inventions no one has a reason to make.

But how much invention, really?

I have been describing direction -- which problems get attacked. It is worth being honest about the other question: does a bigger market call forth genuinely more invention overall, or just shuffle a fixed pool of R&D toward the paying customers?

Here the strong version of the claim should be reined in. Acemoglu and Linn's four-to-six-percent figure is a within-category number: it tells you how invention reallocates across drug classes, not how much net-new science a larger total market produces. When Pierre Dubois and coauthors went after the aggregate elasticity with better data and methods, they found something much more modest -- an elasticity of innovation to market size of about 0.23, implying that it takes roughly $2.5 billion in extra annual revenue to call forth a single additional new chemical entity (Dubois et al. 2015). So the market-size lever moves direction powerfully and the overall rate only gently. Doubling a disease's market does not double its drugs; it nudges the count up by something like a fifth.

Two more honest caveats. First, demand-pulled R&D is not automatically valuable R&D: Finkelstein flagged a sliver of socially wasteful induced investment, and the China vaccine case showed that cutting some induced innovation cost society little. The market pulls invention toward revenue, and revenue is an imperfect proxy for human value -- which is, after all, the whole reason the neglected-disease story is a tragedy and not merely an accounting identity. Second, some of what looks like "more" invention for a rich market is invention redirected from somewhere else: a lab pointed at the elderly is a lab not pointed at something else, and counting the true net gain is genuinely hard.

None of this dents the central claim. That demand pulls the direction of invention is about as well established as anything in this literature -- multiple clean designs, across centuries and sectors, agree. It is the magnitude of the rate effect, and the social value of what gets pulled, where the honest answer is "less than the enthusiasts hope."

Bottom line

Invention is not a force of nature that rains equally on all human problems. It is an economic activity, and like other economic activities it goes where the money is. The evidence that the direction of invention follows market size -- bigger, richer markets get more of it -- is strong and broad: you can see it in American drug approvals tracking the aging population, in clinical trials jumping when an insurance program or a vaccine recommendation enlarges who can pay, in British engineers retooling for Indian cotton, in American seed labs chasing the crops that heat is threatening. What is more contestable is the overall rate: at the level of a whole industry the elasticity is modest, and not every induced invention is worth having.

The lesson I take is the uncomfortable one. Because the demand that pulls invention is demand backed by money, the innovation system is, by construction, a machine for solving the problems of the well-off faster than the problems of the poor -- not out of malice, but out of arithmetic. That reframes a great deal of innovation policy. If you want inventions for problems the market underserves -- a malaria vaccine, a drought-tolerant cassava, an antibiotic no one can profit from -- the fix is not mainly to exhort scientists or to strengthen patents. It is to manufacture the missing demand: insurance, procurement, prize funds, guaranteed markets that turn a need that cannot pay into a market that can. How well those "pull" instruments actually work is its own question, and a real one.

The market is an extraordinary engine for directing human ingenuity. It just points wherever the money is -- which is both why it works and why, left to itself, it will keep leaving the people who most need new ideas waiting the longest.

What we don't know

  • Direction versus rate, precisely. We have a strong qualitative result on direction and essentially one careful aggregate elasticity (about 0.23) on rate. How much net-new global invention a given increase in world purchasing power buys -- as opposed to reshuffling existing effort -- is still poorly pinned down.
  • Does the value of induced invention track its quantity? Demand pulls R&D toward revenue; how often that revenue lines up with social value, rather than me-too products, is measured in only a handful of settings.
  • Do the "pull" fixes scale to early-stage science? Engineering demand clearly accelerates late-stage development -- Finkelstein's whole effect was in clinical trials. Whether a guaranteed market can call a genuinely new technology into being from scratch, a vaccine that does not yet exist in any lab, is much less tested.
  • How far does the corollary reach beyond health and agriculture? The cleanest evidence comes from fields where need and market diverge most starkly. We know less about how strongly purchasing power skews invention in energy, software, or consumer goods.

2026-06-09 · 13 min read · economics of innovation user innovation

Around 2013, a group of parents of children with type 1 diabetes did something their device makers wouldn't. The continuous glucose monitors on the market couldn't quietly send a sleeping child's blood-sugar readings to a parent's phone in the next room, let alone across town. So the parents reverse-engineered the hardware, wrote the missing software themselves, and posted the whole thing online for anyone to use, free, under the slogan #WeAreNotWaiting. The result — a system called Nightscout — was built by the people who needed it, not by a company, and then given away. Eric von Hippel opens his book Free Innovation (2017) with exactly this kind of story.

It is tempting to file that under "heartwarming exception." It mostly isn't. When von Hippel went looking — back in a 1976 study of 111 scientific-instrument innovations — he found that roughly 80% of the ones that gave users a real jump in capability had been invented, prototyped, and first field-tested by the users themselves (the scientists), not by the instrument companies that later sold them. The first commercial versions of major instrument types were, in effect, copies of devices that users had already built for themselves.

That is the claim of this post, and it has two halves that pull in opposite directions. The first half is upbeat: a large and systematically undercounted share of innovation — especially of genuinely new functionality — comes from users and customers rather than from producers' R&D labs. The second half is the catch, and it is why the title hedges: most of what users invent never spreads. They build it for themselves, they rarely patent it, they often give it away — and then, more often than not, nobody else ever adopts it. Both halves matter. Together they say our standard mental model of innovation, the one with a corporate or university lab at the center, is not so much wrong as badly incomplete — and that the official statistics are missing a real chunk of the activity.

The first people to build a new tool are often the people who need it

Start with the producers'-eye view that the 1976 instruments result upends. The intuitive model is a division of labor: firms invent, users buy. But users have something firms lack — direct, detailed, often hard-to-articulate knowledge of their own needs, what von Hippel called "sticky" information. A scientist at the frontier hits a wall today that the instrument company won't hear about for years. If the need is sharp enough and the user capable enough, the user just solves it.

Von Hippel turned this into a usable idea: lead users, people at the leading edge of a market who feel tomorrow's needs now and expect high benefit from a solution. The most cited test of whether lead users are worth seeking out is a natural experiment inside 3M, reported by Lilien, Morrison, Searls, Sonnack & von Hippel (2002). 3M ran "lead-user" idea-generation projects alongside its conventional ones in the same divisions and compared the results. The lead-user projects' ideas were conservatively projected to generate $146 million in annual sales after five years — more than eight times the forecast for the average traditional project. Funding divisions projected their highest rate of major new product lines in half a century. (Hold onto a caveat: those are sales forecasts, not realized revenue, from one firm. I'll come back to it.)

This is not just a consumer story, and it is not just an American one. Survey Dutch high-tech SMEs and you find it again: in de Jong & von Hippel's (2009) sample of 498 firms, 54% had developed or modified process equipment or software for their own use, at real private expense — and a quarter of those user innovations were later transferred to the equipment producers, many with no payment at all. Adams, Fontana & Malerba (2013) put numbers on it in semiconductors and found that the magnitude of innovation by user firms, measured in patents, is large both absolutely and relative to the chip makers themselves.

And it is not a rich-world luxury. In one of my favorite examples, van der Boor, Oliveira & Veloso (2014) traced the development history of the world's mobile financial services — phone-based banking and payments. Eighty-five percent of the innovations originated in developing countries, and at least half were pioneered by users rather than by producers (about 45% by producers; the rest jointly). The traditional picture of technology trickling from rich-country labs to poor-country adopters had the arrows backwards.

Across scientific instruments, factory equipment, chips, and mobile money, the same pattern keeps surfacing: when functionally new capability appears, the user is unusually often the one who got there first.

Now count the garages

The cases above are vivid but selected. The harder question is how common user innovation is in the general population. You cannot answer that from case studies — you have to go count. Starting in the late 2000s, von Hippel and collaborators did, running representative national surveys that simply asked people whether they had built or modified a product to do something it couldn't do before.

The flagship is von Hippel, de Jong & Flowers (2012), a representative survey of 1,173 UK consumers. 6.1% of UK adults — about 2.9 million people — had developed or modified a consumer product for their own use in the previous three years. Companion surveys put the general-population rate at roughly 4–6% across six countries — the UK, the US (5.2%), Japan (3.7%), Finland, Canada, and South Korea (Ogawa & Pongtanalert 2011 for the US and Japan figures). A few percent of all adults sounds small until you multiply by a national population and a few years.

Two numbers from that literature reset the prior. First, scale: the UK consumers in aggregate spent more developing products for themselves than every UK firm combined spent on consumer-product R&D — about 1.4 times as much. Pooling the six surveyed countries, von Hippel estimates that tens of millions of people collectively spend tens of billions of dollars a year, in time and materials, building things for their own use. Second, the accounting blind spot: Sichel & von Hippel (2019), working in national-accounts terms, estimate US household product-R&D investment at about $41 billion a year (2012 dollars), a capital stock around $233 billion — "about half of what producers spend in R&D to develop new products for consumers" — and note that essentially none of it is counted as investment in GDP or the official R&D statistics.

Why does the spending hide? Because users overwhelmingly don't sell what they make and don't lock it down. In the surveys, consumers almost never use intellectual property, and across the six-country data more than 90% of these innovations meet von Hippel's definition of "free innovation": developed on unpaid discretionary time, and left unprotected, free for anyone to copy. An activity that generates no invoices and no patents is nearly invisible to the instruments economists use to measure innovation. That is the core of the "undercounted" half of the claim: it isn't that the spreadsheets are wrong about producer R&D, it's that there's a second, unpriced innovation economy sitting next to it that the spreadsheets were never designed to see.

How confident should we be in all this? On the qualitative point — that ordinary users are a real and quantitatively non-trivial source of innovation, mostly unpriced — the evidence is convergent across domains, methods, and decades, and I'd call it well established. On the exact magnitudes, less so, and I'll get to why.

But is any of it actually good?

A fair skeptic says: sure, lots of people fiddle with their bikes and their kitchen gadgets, but that's hobby tinkering, not the stuff that moves an economy. Most of those 2.9 million UK "innovations" are surely trivial.

Many are. But the evidence says users are over-represented precisely where it counts — in functionally novel products, the ones that open a new category rather than tweak an old one. von Hippel & Kaulartz (2021) put the regularity plainly: users, rather than producers, tend to pioneer products for which both the need and the solution are new, while producers tend to dominate later, when the job is to improve and scale a known design. The cleanest recent test comes from Preissner, Raasch & Schweisfurth (2024), who took 60 innovations that the disruption literature had independently flagged as disruptive and coded who originally developed each. Forty-three percent of these disruptive innovations were originally developed by users. Users were the likely source when customer preferences were turbulent, when the novelty was functional rather than deep-technical, and when the invention was hard to appropriate; producers led when the novelty was technological. Mountain-biking and windsurfing gear, much of open-source software, and mobile money are all user-origin stories, and several of them spawned industries.

So the honest reading is two-tiered. A great deal of user innovation is small and local. But the upper tail of user innovation is disproportionately category-defining, and the 3M result suggests that a firm that goes looking for it can find ideas worth many times its in-house average. I'd rate this fairly convincing rather than airtight, because the showcase numbers — the 3M 8×, the 43% — come from forecasts and curated samples, not from a clean estimate over all innovation.

Objection: but most of it never spreads

Here is the catch that earns the second half of the title. Go back to the UK survey: of all those consumer innovations, only about 17% were ever shared with anyone else. Flip that around and it's a striking image — for every user-built product that reaches a second person, roughly five more sit unused on the workbench that built them.

This is not an accident; it's a market failure, and a well-documented one. A consumer who solves her own problem has little reason to take on the cost and hassle of telling the world. de Jong, Gillert & Stock (2018) show the bite of it: in their data, whether a consumer innovation gets adopted by others is unrelated to how generally useful it is — unless the innovator happens to be motivated to commercialize it. Usefulness alone doesn't make a thing spread; somebody has to want to spread it. Later work finds the same diffusion gap and pokes at what closes it — community involvement and certain innovator identities help (Yu & de Jong 2024), and makerspaces roughly double diffusion rates relative to lone tinkerers (Halbinger 2018) — but the baseline is low.

The exception proves the rule. Remember the mobile-money case, where user innovations diffused faster than producer ones — at more than twice the rate. What was different? In that setting there were operators, agents, and competitors with a direct commercial incentive to copy and scale a working idea. Where a diffusion channel exists, user innovation travels; where it doesn't — the default for a lone consumer — it stalls. So the diffusion question doesn't undo the source claim, but it sharply qualifies what it's worth. A lot of valuable functionality is being invented and then stranded. The evidence here is mixed only in the sense of being setting-dependent; the underlying pattern — low diffusion by default, higher where someone has a reason to spread it — is consistent.

Objection: are we just counting hobbies with a generous ruler?

The magnitudes deserve a harder look too, because they do a lot of rhetorical work. Three honest dents:

First, the surveys are self-reports, and what counts as developing a product "with a novel function" is a judgment call the respondent makes. Cirera & Muzi (2020) show that measured innovation rates can swing substantially with how the survey question is framed. Push the wording one way and you'd get fewer than 6%; push it another and more. The order of magnitude is robust across many surveys; the second decimal place is not.

Second, the headline spending comparisons value unpaid time, so they aren't a like-for-like with firms' accounting R&D. That's a defensible choice — an hour a hobbyist spends is a real economic resource — but it's why the same activity can look like "1.4 times all firms' consumer R&D" in the UK framing and "about half" in the US national-accounts framing. Both are honest; the gap is method, not contradiction. The truthful summary is a range: household product-development effort runs somewhere from roughly half to more than all of what firms spend developing products for consumers, depending on country and on how you price a Saturday afternoon. Tellingly, when Chen, Su, de Jong & von Hippel (2020) added non-need motivations (learning, fun, helping others) to a Chinese survey, the measured innovation rate rose by about 1.4× — a reminder that these counts move with definitions.

Third, the showcase efficacy numbers carry selection and forecast risk, as flagged: the 3M figure is a projection, and the "43% of disruptive innovations" comes from a small, hand-picked set.

None of this overturns the claim. It bounds it. The sign and the order of magnitude survive; the precise share does not.

What this means for firms and policy

If users are a real source — and especially the source of the functionally new — then a firm's R&D strategy has a blind spot. The theory was worked out by Gambardella, Raasch & von Hippel (2017), who built a microeconomic model of a market with both user and producer innovation. Two results stand out. Social welfare rises when firms support and harvest users' innovations, because users and producers end up doing complementary parts of the job. But profit-maximizing firms switch "too late" from doing all the inventing in-house to supporting and harvesting users — because some of the surplus from user innovation is an externality the firm can't capture. (This is a model result, a speculation flag relative to the empirical sections above — it tells us what to expect, not what's been measured.)

The practical move is to stop treating users as an audience and start treating them as a development lab you don't have to fully pay for. Some firms already do: they run lead-user searches (the 3M playbook), host user communities and watch what gets built (Jeppesen & Frederiksen 2006), hire embedded lead users (Schweisfurth & Raasch 2015), and — in open source — accept freely revealed contributions as a serious input to commercial products (von Hippel & von Krogh 2003). The reason this can work without the firm getting fleeced is that free revealing is often privately rational for the user: Harhoff, Henkel & von Hippel (2003) show, with a simple model, that under realistic conditions a user does better by revealing an innovation — and getting it improved, supported, and turned into a standard — than by hoarding it.

For policy, the diffusion failure is the lever. If the problem isn't that users fail to invent but that what they invent fails to spread, then the highest-value interventions are the ones that build diffusion channels — makerspaces, repositories, design platforms, light-touch support for community-hosted innovation — rather than another producer subsidy. And at minimum, the measurement community has a to-do item: if household R&D is roughly half of consumer-product business R&D and missing from the accounts, our productivity and innovation statistics are mismeasuring where ideas come from.

Bottom line

Users and customers are a genuine, repeated, and quantitatively serious source of innovation — most clearly for products whose function is new — and almost all of it is unpriced, unprotected, and uncounted. On that, after fifty years and a stack of convergent studies across instruments, factories, chips, phones, and living rooms, I'm fairly confident. The producer-centric model of "where ideas come from" should be downgraded from the story to a story.

But temper it with the catch. By default, most user innovation never reaches a second person; it spreads only where someone — a community, a competitor, a commercializing user — has a reason to carry it. So the right summary isn't "users out-innovate firms." It's that there is a large, mostly invisible reservoir of user-built functionality, of which we currently capture only a trickle, and the binding constraint is diffusion, not invention. The size of that reservoir is real; its exact volume depends on how you measure, and how much of it would be valuable to others if it could escape the garage is genuinely unknown.

What we don't know

  • The true aggregate share. Because the numbers rest on self-reports and imputed time, the honest estimate is a range, not a point. A measurement breakthrough — administrative traces of user innovation, or a consensus survey instrument — would tighten it.
  • The value of the undiffused part. We know ~17% spreads. We don't know whether the other ~83% is mostly idiosyncratic junk or full of stranded gems. That single unknown drives how much the diffusion failure actually costs.
  • Whether "harvest your users" generalizes. The strategy works at 3M, LEGO, and in open source. We have little clean causal evidence on how far it travels to ordinary firms in ordinary industries.
  • Whether policy can fix diffusion cheaply. Makerspaces and platforms correlate with more diffusion, but the causal, welfare-relevant effect of subsidizing them is untested.

2026-06-02 · 15 min read · economics-of-innovation misallocation

The most quoted worry in the economics of innovation right now is that we are running out of ideas. The flagship evidence is a 2020 paper by Nicholas Bloom, Charles Jones, John Van Reenen, and Michael Webb, bluntly titled "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?" Their answer is yes: across chips, crops, and corporate R&D, it takes ever more researchers to sustain the same rate of progress. Keeping Moore's Law on track, they calculate, now needs more than 18 times the research effort it did in the early 1970s. The natural reading is fatalistic — the low-hanging fruit is gone, and there is not much to do about it.

I want to argue for a more hopeful, and I think under-appreciated, second story that runs alongside the first. A good chunk of the invention we don't see is not missing because the ideas got harder or because the talent isn't there. It is missing because the talent is in the wrong place — or in no place at all. Economists have a dry word for this: misallocation. The right people are not doing the inventing, and the people who are inventing are too often stuck where they invent least.

That distinction matters because the two stories have opposite policy implications. If invention is scarce because ideas are intrinsically hard to find, there is little to do but spend more. If a meaningful slice of the shortfall is misallocation, then it is recoverable — and the levers are surprisingly concrete: who gets exposed to invention as a child, whether an engineer can quit and join a rival, whether a patent can be sold to the firm that would actually use it, whether a talented but poor teenager can afford the PhD.

Here is the claim, stated plainly: a large share of the gap between potential and realized invention is misallocation, not a shortage of talent or an exhaustion of ideas — so reducing the frictions that keep inventive talent from its best use could raise idea production substantially. I'll be honest up front about the size of the prize, because the strongest numbers come from calibrated models rather than clean experiments. But the direction is well established, and it points somewhere encouraging.

What "misallocation" means, and why it's first-order

Start with the lens itself, because it has a serious pedigree. The idea that an economy can have all the right inputs and still under-produce — because those inputs are attached to the wrong activities — is the heart of Hsieh and Klenow's 2009 study of manufacturing in China and India. They found that simply reallocating labor and capital across existing plants, so that marginal products lined up the way they do in the United States, would raise manufacturing productivity by 30–60%. Nothing new is invented — the same factories, workers, and machines, simply rearranged to produce more.

Now apply the lens to talent instead of capital. The seminal move is Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny (1991): where a society's ablest people go — building companies versus rent-seeking through law or finance — is a first-order determinant of growth. Their cross-country evidence: more engineering majors, faster growth; more law concentrators, slower.

The modern, carefully-measured version is Hsieh, Hurst, Jones, and Klenow's 2019 paper in Econometrica, and it is the single most important number in this post. In 1960, 94% of US doctors and lawyers were white men; by 2010 that share had fallen to 62%. Innate talent for medicine or law surely did not shift across groups over those 50 years — so in 1960 an enormous pool of capable women and Black men were simply not doing the work they were best suited for. Feeding the historical convergence through a model of occupational choice, the authors estimate that between 20% and 40% of all growth in US output per person from 1960 to 2010 can be attributed to the improved allocation of talent. A fifth to two-fifths of half a century of growth — from letting the right people do the right jobs.

That is the frame. Misallocation is not a rounding error; it has already been one of the largest growth stories of the postwar era. The question is how much of it is specifically about invention — and there, the evidence splits cleanly into two margins.

Think of realized invention as the product of three things: talent × match quality × deployment. A society loses inventions when talented people never deploy at all (they never become inventors), and when the inventors it does have work below their best-match productivity (stuck in the wrong field or firm). Call these the entry margin and the matching margin. Both turn out to be large.

Margin one: the inventors we never get

The cleanest evidence that we are wasting inventive talent before it ever starts comes from Bell, Chetty, Jaravel, Petkova, and Van Reenen's 2019 study in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, which linked 1.2 million inventors on US patents to their childhood tax records. The headline gradient is stark: children born into the top 1% of the income distribution are about ten times as likely to become inventors as children from below-median-income families.

The obvious objection is that richer kids are simply more able. The authors test this directly and it fails. The invention gap persists even among children who scored identically on third-grade math tests — and early math scores are otherwise highly predictive of who invents. A low-income child in the top 5% of third-grade math is no more likely to become an inventor than a below-average-math child from an affluent family. Ability is not the binding constraint; environment is. They pin the mechanism down causally: children whose families move to a high-innovation area when young become more likely to invent, and the effect is eerily specific — exposure to a particular technology class, even a particular inventor gender, predicts inventing in exactly that class. Girls become more likely to patent in a field only when they grew up around female inventors in it.

The punchline is the one the authors made famous as the "lost Einsteins." If women, minorities, and children from low-income families invented at the same rate as high-income white men, they calculate, the innovation rate in America would roughly quadruple. (That is a counterfactual accounting figure, not an estimated treatment effect — a ceiling on the entry margin, not a forecast.)

Why don't financial rewards already fix this? Because, as the same team showed in a companion JEEA paper, "Do Tax Cuts Produce More Einsteins?", the people who matter most for invention barely respond to money. Inventor income is wildly skewed — the top 1% of inventors collect more than 22% of it — so cutting top tax rates mostly showers cash on people who would have invented anyway, doing nothing for the talented child who was never exposed. Their verdict: financial incentives have "limited potential" to raise aggregate innovation; exposure and mentorship could do far more.

A complementary study pushes the same conclusion through a different door. Murat Alp Çelik, in "Does the Cream Always Rise to the Top?" (Journal of Monetary Economics, 2023), links a century of US patents to 1930 census records by surname: richer family backgrounds predict becoming an inventor, while more-educated backgrounds predict inventor productivity. The two don't coincide, so rich-but-mediocre candidates crowd talented-but-poor ones out. In the working-paper version of his calibrated model, a genuinely meritocratic allocation raises the rate of innovation by about 10%. (A model figure, so hold it loosely; the qualitative point — that the cream does not reliably rise — rests on the cleaner descriptive data.)

So the entry margin is real and large, and the evidence that it is environment, not ability is about as good as social science gets. This is the strongest leg of the argument.

Margin two: inventors stuck in the wrong place

The second margin is subtler, and to me more surprising. Even among people who do invent, a lot of potential is lost because they can't — or don't — move to where they'd invent most.

The cleanest evidence comes from an accident of legal history. In 1985, Michigan inadvertently reversed its policy and began enforcing employee non-compete agreements — contracts that bar a worker from joining or starting a rival firm. Because the change was a legislative side-effect rather than a response to local economic conditions, it makes a credible natural experiment, which Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming exploited in a 2009 Management Science paper. Enforcing non-competes cut inventor mobility in Michigan by about 8% — and, tellingly, the lock-in fell hardest on exactly the inventors whose movement matters most: those with firm-specific skills (a further 15% drop) and those in narrow technical specialties (a further 16%). These are the people for whom finding a good match is hardest and most valuable, and they were the most stuck.

The same shock produced a regional brain drain: Marx, Singh, and Fleming showed knowledge workers fled Michigan for states where non-competes were unenforceable, and the outflow was concentrated among the highest-impact and most collaborative inventors. And Starr, Frake, and Agarwal (2019) document that the damage spills beyond those who sign: where non-competes are common and enforceable, even workers who never signed one get fewer offers, move less, and earn less, as the whole local market for talent thickens with friction.

This is not a niche contract. Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021), in a nationally representative survey, find that roughly 18% of US workers are currently bound by a non-compete and about 38% have signed one at some point — figures broadly in line with the Federal Trade Commission's 2024 estimate that around one in five American workers is covered. We have built a lot of machinery for keeping people where they are.

Does the friction actually cost us inventions? Here I have to be careful, because this is where the honest counter-evidence lives. The Michigan studies found that enforcing non-competes reduced mobility sharply but did not reduce the state's overall patenting rate. Patents and non-competes can be complements — firms that lock workers in also invest in patentable R&D. So the damage is better described as allocative than as a fall in raw counts: the same patents, but produced by people who can't move to their best match, and captured by incumbents rather than the upstarts mobility would seed. Which brings up the most provocative recent finding on this margin.

In "Where Have All the 'Creative Talents' Gone?" (NBER, 2023), Ufuk Akcigit and Nathan Goldschlag track US inventors and find them increasingly absorbed by large, established incumbents — the share of inventors working at big incumbent firms rose from about 49% in 2000 to 57% by 2016. And in a matched event study comparing similar inventors hired by incumbents versus young firms, those who land at incumbents earn about 13% more but produce 6–11% fewer (and lower-impact) patents. The labor market, in other words, is sorting inventors toward where they get paid most, which is not the same as where they invent most. That is misallocation in its purest form: the price signal and the social return have come apart.

Which is the precise gap that the paper seeding this post is built to measure. In "Intellectual Mobility Frictions," Jordan Bisset and Dennis Verhoeven model the cost a firm must pay to move an inventor into a technological field they haven't worked in before — the friction of redeploying talent across the idea space, not just across employers. Calibrating the model, they estimate that even moderate reductions in these allocation frictions could raise idea generation by about 50%. Like the other counterfactuals here, that is a model-based number and I'd treat it as illustrative rather than precise — but it is in the same surprising territory as everything else on this margin: the losses from talent being stuck are big.

What moving the talent buys

If misallocation is the disease, reallocation is the cure — and we can watch it work. The most direct evidence comes from inventors who move across borders. In "The Global Race for Talent" (QJE, 2024), Marta Prato tracks inventors migrating along the US–EU corridor and finds that, after moving, a migrant inventor's own patenting rises by about 33% a year. And this isn't zero-sum: the collaborators a migrant leaves behind also patent about 16% more per year, as knowledge flows back through the old network. When she uses her calibrated model to double the US H-1B visa program, long-run growth rises about 4% in both regions — and the reason, in her words, is that the policy "sorts inventors to where they produce more innovations and knowledge spillovers." That phrase is the whole argument in miniature.

You can even put a market price on the misallocation of ideas themselves. Akcigit, Çelik, and Greenwood (2016) study the US market for patents and start from a simple observation: a given invention is worth more in the hands of a firm whose business is close to it. Sometimes an idea is born in the wrong firm. Their data show that roughly 16% of patents get sold at least once, and — exactly as the theory predicts — patents that are a poor fit for the firm that owns them are about 13% more likely to be sold. The market is visibly shuffling ideas toward better matches. In their model, speeding that reshuffling lifts the growth rate from a benchmark of about 2.1% toward 3.4% in the frictionless ideal — a striking gap that comes from nothing but better matching of existing ideas to firms. Finally, reallocation beats brute spending even in policy design. Akcigit, Pearce, and Prato (2024, Review of Economic Studies) use Danish data to compare equal-cost policies and find an education subsidy raises innovation about four times as much as an R&D subsidy — roughly +20% versus +5% for the same money. The reason is pure allocation: R&D subsidies mostly reward inventors already in the game, while education subsidies pull credit-constrained, talented-but-poor youth into careers they'd otherwise never reach. It's the lost-Einsteins logic with a price tag.

How big is the prize? A back-of-the-envelope

It is tempting to add these numbers up. Don't — that would be the central mistake. The "innovation rate could quadruple" figure (entry margin) and the "+50% idea generation" figure (matching margin) are measuring overlapping potential through different models, and stacking them would double-count wildly.

The disciplined way to see it is the talent × match × deployment identity. Imagine 100 people with real inventive potential. Suppose the entry friction lets only 30 of them become inventors, and each of those 30 works at 70% of their best-match productivity, stuck in a suboptimal field or firm. Realized invention is 30 × 0.7 = 21 units against a potential of 100 — about a fifth. Most of that loss is the entry margin (hence the high lost-Einsteins ceiling); but even with the inventor pool fixed, lifting match quality from 0.7 to 1.0 raises output more than 40% (hence mobility estimates near "+50%"). Both margins can be large at once because they multiply, not add.

So what's the honest summary number? I won't give a point estimate, because the literature doesn't support one. But three independent bodies of evidence — historical talent reallocation that already delivered a fifth to two-fifths of US growth, an entry-margin counterfactual measured in multiples, and a matching-margin counterfactual in tens of percent — all point the same way. The recoverable share of "missing" invention looks like tens of percent, not a rounding error. That is the claim I'm willing to defend.

Objection: isn't this just efficient sorting?

The sharpest counterargument is that what I'm calling "misallocation" is actually people ending up exactly where they belong. Maybe the inventor at the big incumbent really is most valuable there; maybe the worker who didn't move had nowhere better to go. If inventors are already optimally sorted, there is no free lunch.

This is exactly why the natural experiments matter, and why I've leaned on them. When non-compete enforceability flips for reasons unrelated to local fundamentals — a Michigan drafting accident — and inventor mobility and the geography of talent visibly move in response, that is misallocation being revealed. Efficient sorting doesn't lurch when a contract rule changes by mistake. The migration and patent-market results tell the same story from the other side: output rises when a friction is relaxed. You don't get a 33% patenting jump from a move if the inventor was already optimally placed.

The second objection is the one I opened with: aren't ideas just getting harder to find? Yes — and I fully concede Bloom and coauthors' result; it's robust and important. But it describes a different margin. Research productivity can be falling for deep technological reasons and a recoverable slice of invention can be lost to misallocation at once; the two are additive worries, not rivals, and misallocation is the part we have policy handles on. Note too the honest dent from the Michigan result: the evidence that the matching friction lowers raw patent counts is weak. What it robustly supports is that friction degrades who invents what, and where the value lands — invention quality and dynamism more than headline quantity.

Finally, the magnitudes. Be skeptical of them, including mine. The 20–40%, the quadrupling, the +50%, the growth ladders — these are calibrated-model and accounting counterfactuals, and they inherit every assumption baked into those models. The estimates I'd actually bet on are the directly measured ones: the 10× inventor gradient that survives controlling for childhood test scores, the 8% mobility drop from non-compete enforcement, the 33% patenting jump after migration, the 6–11% output penalty at incumbents. Those are the spine. The big counterfactuals are the flesh, and flesh is softer.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That talent is badly allocated across the economy, and that fixing it is worth a lot, is well established — one of the great growth stories of the last half-century. That invention suffers from a large entry friction — talented people who never invent because of where and to whom they were born — is also on firm ground, with a clean separation of environment from ability. That a matching friction strands working inventors in low-productivity placements is well supported in direction by credible natural experiments, though the precise tax it levies on output is genuinely uncertain, and may fall more on the composition and dynamism of invention than on raw counts. That the total recoverable prize is "tens of percent" is my calibrated guess — convergent but model-dependent numbers I wouldn't defend to the decimal.

So: much "missing" invention is misallocated talent, not missing talent. I'm confident in the adjective "much" and deliberately did not write "most." The hopeful implication survives the hedging, though. Unlike the exhaustion of ideas, misallocation is something we know how to push on — through childhood exposure and mentorship, through education subsidies that reach poor talent, through lighter non-compete enforcement, through openness to immigrant inventors, through thicker markets for ideas. None of these requires inventing anything new. They just stop wasting the inventors we already have.

What we don't know

Several things would change my mind or sharpen the estimate, and they mark the research frontier:

  • The true size of the matching loss. We have clean estimates that non-competes cut mobility and that incumbents under-use inventors, but no consensus number for how much aggregate invention better matching would buy. The Michigan "patenting didn't fall" result warns the answer might be smaller — or more about quality than quantity — than the model counterfactuals imply.
  • General-equilibrium offsets. If we unstuck every inventor, would they crowd the same hot fields and compete away each other's returns? Reallocation gains in partial-equilibrium studies can shrink once everyone moves at once. Nobody has cleanly measured the congestion.
  • Whether the counterfactuals hold out of sample. "Equalize inventor rates and invention quadruples" assumes the newly-drawn inventors are as productive as the incumbents. The exposure evidence is encouraging on this, but it's an extrapolation past anything we've actually observed.
  • The policy frontier. We know exposure matters; we know much less about which interventions scale. A mentorship program that works in a trial of hundreds may not survive rollout to millions. The cheapest way to test the thesis is to run those experiments and watch the inventor pipeline.

The deepest reason to care is that this is the optimistic corner of a pessimistic literature. If ideas merely got harder to find, the future would be a slow grind against diminishing returns. But if a real share of the shortfall is talent we're wasting, then some of the next century's inventions are already here — in people never given the chance, or the mobility, to make them.

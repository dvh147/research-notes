In January 2023, Nature published a paper with a title that reads like an obituary: "Papers and patents are becoming less disruptive over time." Michael Park, Erin Leahey, and Russell Funk had run a single number — the "disruption index" — across 25 million papers going back to 1945 and nearly 4 million patents going back to 1980, and the number kept falling. By their reckoning the average paper had become 91.9% to 100% less disruptive since the war, depending on the field; patents had fallen 78.7% to 91.5% since 1980. The press did the rest. Science is running out of breakthroughs. The age of disruption is over. We are standing on the shoulders of giants and finding nothing new.

It is a genuinely alarming claim, and it would matter enormously if it were true at face value. If the engine of long-run growth — path-breaking science and technology — really had lost nine-tenths of its disruptive power, that would reframe everything from how we fund research to how worried we should be about stagnation. So the finding deserves not a headline but a trial. And over the past three years it has had one: a wave of methodological critiques argues that the decline is mostly an illusion baked into the ruler.

Here is the verdict I will defend, with calibrated confidence: the decline is real, but the famous numbers are not. The dramatic magnitudes are mostly an artifact of the way bibliographies have fattened over time; the patent slump may not exist at all; but a real, much milder shift toward consolidation does survive the critiques — not because the disruption index is trustworthy (it isn't, in its decimals), but because several independent signals that owe nothing to that index point the same way. Most importantly, the right thing to worry about is not what the headlines said. Science is not producing fewer breakthroughs. It is producing them at a lower rate, as a shrinking slice of a vastly larger enterprise. Those are different claims, and the difference is the whole story.

I'll go in five steps: the ruler and what it measures; what Park and coauthors actually found (which is more than the headline); the two objections that genuinely wound the finding; why I still don't think it's all a mirage; and the number that actually matters.

The ruler

Everything rides on one metric, so it is worth a minute. The "disruption index" — also called the CD index, for consolidating–disrupting — was introduced by Funk and Owen-Smith (2017) and first applied at scale by Wu, Wang, and Evans (2019). It tries to capture something citation counts can't: not how much a paper is used, but whether it redirects the conversation.

The intuition is a small one. Take a paper P that builds on three earlier works — it cites references A, B, and C. Now watch who cites P afterward. If those later papers cite P and also keep citing A, B, and C, then P sits comfortably inside an ongoing tradition: it consolidates. But if later papers cite P instead of A, B, and C — if P comes to eclipse the very work it was built on — then P disrupts: it becomes the new foundation, and its intellectual parents fade from the footnotes. The index runs from −1 (purely consolidating) to +1 (purely disruptive). Watson and Crick's 1953 structure of DNA scores high; a careful paper that merely refines an existing technique scores low, no matter how often it is cited.

It is a clever idea, and it is doing something real. But notice the load-bearing assumption: the score depends entirely on whether later citers also cite your references. Hold that thought, because it is exactly where the trouble lives.

What they actually found

Run that index across six decades and the picture is stark and remarkably consistent. In Park, Leahey, and Funk's data the average paper's disruption fell by roughly 90% or more across every broad field; the decline was steepest early and flattened toward the end of the series. That is the number everyone quoted.

But the paper is more careful than its reception, and two of its lesser-known results matter for the trial to come.

First, the authors clearly anticipated the obvious objection — isn't this just your weird index? — and threw a lot at it. They built two versions of the CD index that explicitly subtract out the effect of reference-list length; the decline survived. They controlled for field, year, team size, and citation counts in regressions; it survived. They simulated random "rewired" citation networks and showed the real decline was larger than chance would produce, and growing. They recomputed it on four entirely separate databases — JSTOR, the American Physical Society, Microsoft Academic Graph, PubMed — and on alternative disruption measures proposed by other authors; the decline showed up everywhere. They even restricted the sample to papers in Nature, PNAS, and Science, and to Nobel-winning work, and still saw it fall. So the lazy version of the critique — "they ignored citation inflation" — is simply wrong. They addressed a version of it. The live question is whether they addressed enough of it.

Second, and more interesting, they buttressed the index with evidence that has nothing to do with citations at all. They measured the language of science. The diversity of words in paper titles fell by 76.5% to 88% over the period; the novelty of word combinations fell too. And the verbs scientists use shifted in a telling way: in the 1950s, the common verbs evoked creation and discovery — produce, form, determine, report; by 2010 they had been largely displaced by verbs of incremental improvement — improve, enhance, increase, associate. Patents tell the same story. Whatever you think of the disruption index, longer reference lists cannot explain why scientists started writing "enhance" instead of "discover."

So the original paper is not a one-trick result. It is one contested metric braced by several less-contested ones. Keep that architecture in mind.

Objection! The ruler stretches

Now the critique that genuinely lands. The disruption index asks whether later citers also cite your references — and the average reference list has been growing for the better part of a century. Pan and coauthors (2018), in a study of the "memory of science," found references per paper growing about 1.8% a year, enough to double the total supply of citations roughly every twelve years; Nicolaisen and Frandsen (2021) document the same swelling across every field they checked. They call it citation inflation, and it is poison for a metric built on reference overlap.

Here is why, concretely. Suppose in 1960 a typical paper cited fifteen works, and by 2010 it cited fifty. The disruption index scores your paper as consolidating whenever later papers cite both you and your references. But if everyone in 2010 is citing fifty things instead of fifteen, then any later paper is mechanically more likely to sweep up your references too — not because your work failed to redirect the field, but because bibliographies got fatter. The denser the citation network, the more the index is dragged toward zero. A paper can look more "consolidating" in 2010 than an identical paper would have looked in 1960, purely because of the ambient bookkeeping.

Petersen, Arroyave, and Pammolli (2024, 2025) turn this from a worry into an indictment. Across a deductive argument, computational simulations, and real data, they show the CD index systematically falls over time from citation inflation alone, and argue that Park and coauthors' corrections don't go far enough to remove it. Their cleanest move is a small natural experiment: they compare ordinary PNAS articles with "PNAS Plus" articles, which differ mainly in length — and the longer ones score as less disruptive, just as the bias predicts. And when they recompute disruptiveness in a large modern dataset, they find it rising from 2005 to 2015, not falling. If the trend can reverse in the most recent decade once you handle inflation, the 90% headline cannot be taken literally.

I find this convincing on the narrow point it makes. The magnitude is mostly an artifact. A decline of "90 to 100 percent" is not a measurement of the world; it is, in large part, a measurement of how reference lists grew. On the size of the effect, the critics win.

Objection! The patents never slumped

The patent half of the finding fares even worse. Macher, Rutzer, and Weder (2024), writing in Research Policy, go after it directly and, to my reading, mostly demolish it.

Their key point is mundane and devastating: the patent database Park and coauthors used only records backward citations starting in 1976. So a patent from the early 1980s has most of its intellectual ancestors amputated — its references to pre-1976 work simply aren't in the data. With its parents missing, an old patent looks artificially disruptive (nobody can "also cite" references that were never recorded), which manufactures a high starting point and therefore a steep apparent decline. Macher and coauthors calculate that 88% of the entire 1980–2010 patent decline is explained by this truncation alone. Add a US patent-law change that altered how applications are cited, correct for both, and extend the data to 2016, and the slump doesn't just shrink — it reverses. The number of highly disruptive patents has been rising since 1980, and especially since 2008.

I take this as close to dispositive for patents. The honest position is that the patent decline is probably an artifact, and we should stop citing it. On that leg of the finding, treat the original claim as withdrawn.

So far the score is lopsided: the magnitudes are inflated, and the patents are a mirage. Why don't I just call the whole thing debunked?

Why I don't think it's all a mirage

Because citation inflation is a story about a citation index, and it cannot explain the evidence that isn't about citations.

Recall the linguistic results. Longer reference lists do not shrink the vocabulary of titles, and they do not push scientists from "discover" to "enhance." Those shifts were measured on the text of papers, not the structure of their footnotes, and they move in lockstep with the disruption decline. You would have to tell a separate and rather strained story to explain them away.

And the disruption index is not the only instrument pointing at consolidation. Consider three independent lines of evidence:

  • Chu and Evans (2021), studying 1.8 billion citations across 241 fields, show that as a field publishes more each year, its canon ossifies: attention concentrates on already-famous papers and newcomers struggle to break in. The rank-order of a field's fifty most-cited papers from one year to the next firms up from a loose correlation of 0.25 when a field publishes a thousand papers a year to a rigid 0.74 at a hundred thousand. That is a mechanism for declining disruption — sheer field size entrenching the incumbents — that never touches the CD index.

  • The macro picture agrees. Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb (2020) document that research productivity is falling sharply across industries: it takes about eighteen times as many researchers to sustain Moore's Law today as in the early 1970s. Ideas, they conclude, are getting harder to find. Jones (2009) gives the mechanism — a "burden of knowledge" that forces innovators to specialize and team up as the frontier recedes — and Wang and Lobo (2024) find the matching fingerprint in patents: output per inventor has fallen steadily even as the number of inventors exploded.

  • Even outside the world of papers, He and Lee (2025) build a "product disruption index" for automobiles and find disruptiveness declining there too, in step with the patent and publication trends.

This is the heart of the matter. The disruption index is a flawed ruler — young, contested, and only modestly validated against expert judgments of what counts as a milestone (Bornmann and Tekles, 2021; Leibel and Bornmann, 2024, whose review catalogs its many biases and concludes the original version is not even the best variant). I would not stake a dollar on any single disruption number. But when a flawed index, an analysis of scientific language, a model of field-size ossification, and the macroeconomics of research productivity — four instruments with four different sets of flaws — all point toward consolidation, the direction is more credible than any one of them. Russell Funk himself, a coauthor of the original paper, has since offered a theoretical account of why a real decline might be expected; he is not behaving like a man who thinks he measured nothing.

Intellectual honesty requires the other side of the ledger too. Petersen and coauthors' (2025) finding that disruption rose in the most recent decade is a real tension with "the decline is real," and I don't wave it away — it means my confidence applies to the long postwar arc, not to last year. Klüppel and Knott (2023) find that the productivity slowdown is not a matter of "fishing out" a finite pond of ideas — knowledge production actually rises with the stock of knowledge — so even if the slowdown is real, the most intuitive explanation for it is wrong. And Antonelli and Fusillo (2023) find that in Europe, ideas have been getting cheaper, not dearer. The corroborating evidence is convergent, not unanimous.

The number that actually matters

Here is the result from the original paper that almost no headline mentioned, and it reframes everything. Despite the collapsing average, the absolute number of highly disruptive papers and patents stayed roughly constant over the whole period. Park and coauthors call it the "conservation" of disruptive work: science kept producing about as many genuine breakthroughs per year as it always had — gravity-wave detection, CRISPR, mRNA vaccines, AlphaFold are not figments — even as the average disruptiveness sank.

How can the average crater while the count holds? Because the denominator exploded. The volume of science roughly doubles every dozen-odd years, so over Park's window the number of papers grew more than twentyfold. If the count of breakthroughs is flat while total output multiplies, then the share of work that is disruptive must fall by the same multiple — which looks, through the lens of an average, like a catastrophic 90% decline. Both facts are true at once: each new paper is far less likely to be disruptive, and the world is producing about as many disruptive papers as ever.

That distinction is not a quibble; it is the difference between two completely different worries. "Breakthroughs have stopped" is false and a little hysterical. "We are getting fewer breakthroughs per dollar, per paper, and per researcher" is probably true, more modest, and far more useful — it is a story about the efficiency of the research system, not its sterility. It fits the burden-of-knowledge picture cleanly: as the frontier gets more expensive to reach, the ratio of consolidation to disruption climbs, even though the absolute flow of big ideas keeps up.

Bottom line

So: is science becoming less disruptive? My calibrated answer is yes, but not the way you were told.

I read the long-run direction as real — a genuine shift toward consolidation over the postwar era — because it is corroborated by independent evidence that the citation-inflation critique cannot touch: the changing language of science, the ossification of large fields, and the well-documented decline in research productivity. I'd put that at "the balance of evidence supports it," not "established," because thoughtful critics reproduce a rise in the most recent decade and the cleanest corrections shrink the effect a lot.

But the magnitude is mostly a mirage. The 90-to-100% figures are largely an artifact of fattening bibliographies; the true decline, whatever it is, is far smaller, and no one has a consensus number for it. The patent slump is probably not real at all — once you fix the truncated citation data, it reverses. And the apocalyptic reading is false: the absolute number of breakthroughs has held roughly steady. What has fallen is the rate — the odds that any given paper redirects its field — which is exactly what you'd expect as science scales up and the frontier gets harder to reach.

Trust the direction. Distrust the decimals. And ignore the obituaries.

What we don't know

  • How big is the real decline? Once citation inflation is fully removed, nobody has a number everyone accepts. "Smaller than 90%, larger than zero" is the honest range, which is uncomfortably wide.
  • Is the recent reversal real? Petersen and coauthors (2025) find disruption rising after 2005. Whether that is a genuine turn or a short-window artifact will take another decade of data to settle.
  • What's the cause? Burden of knowledge, canonical ossification, conservative funding, metric-chasing incentives, or measurement — the candidates all predict similar patterns, and no study has cleanly isolated one. Klüppel and Knott (2023) at least rule out simple idea-exhaustion.
  • Can we build a better ruler? Variants like DI5 validate better against expert milestones (Bornmann and Tekles, 2021) and rescaled versions (Yang et al., 2024) tame some biases — but until a disruption metric survives the inflation critique by construction, every time-trend built on it deserves a raised eyebrow.

2026-08-27 · 14 min read · innovation science

The best short answer anyone has given to the question what does science actually do for invention? is that science works like a map. Inventors search by combining things — a molecule with a delivery mechanism, a sensor with an algorithm — and most combinations are useless. In 2004 Lee Fleming and Olav Sorenson argued in Science as a map in technological search that when inventors understand the underlying principles, they go more directly to the useful combinations, abandon dead ends faster, and keep going when an experiment comes back negative. They tested it on every US patent granted in May and June of 1990 — 17,264 of them — comparing patents whose inventors drew on scientific literature against those that did not, and found the advantage was largest where the combined components interact most tightly. It is a correlation across patents rather than an experiment, but the metaphor has been standard ever since, and it is the intellectual justification for a great deal of public money.

Now look at the field where the map should be best. We have spent half a century and extraordinary sums mapping human biology — the genome, the proteome, receptor structures, signalling pathways. And an industry-sponsored drug candidate that begins phase 1 trials still has roughly a 13.8% chance of ever being approved. That figure comes from Chi Heem Wong, Kien Wei Siah and Andrew Lo, who assembled 406,038 clinical-trial records covering 21,143 compounds between 2000 and 2015 and traced each development path through to its end. Roughly seven in eight candidates that enter human testing never make it.

Something is wrong with the map, or with how we are reading it. The claim of this post is narrow and, I think, important:

Fundamental science on its own does not make a drug candidate more likely to reach the market. What predicts approval is a scientific base that combines fundamental understanding with contextualized, applied knowledge — knowledge about how things behave in real, variable bodies.

That is not the same as saying basic research is a poor investment. It very plainly is a good investment, and I will spend a section on why the two claims are compatible. It is a claim about what a given candidate needs in order to survive contact with a human being.

I will march through four things: the direct evidence, the mechanism, the people who carry both kinds of knowledge, and the objections.

5,613 drug candidates

The direct evidence is a single paper, and it is worth being clear about that up front. Paul-Emmanuel Anckaert's When the drugs (don't) work (2025) takes 5,613 early-stage drug candidates from 1995 to 2008, links each one to the scientific publications it builds on, classifies that science as basic (predictive, abstract, mechanism-first) or applied (contextualized, condition-specific), and asks which candidates eventually won market approval. The comparison is between candidates with different mixes of science behind them — no candidate was assigned its knowledge base by anything other than the firm developing it.

The headline: despite everything basic science contributes elsewhere, its predictive and abstract outputs on their own are unlikely to produce a candidate that gets approved. Candidates whose scientific base combines fundamental insight from basic research with contextualized insight from applied science are significantly more likely to make it. The combination effect is strongest for the more novel drugs — exactly where you would expect the uncertainty to bite hardest — and strongest when the firm supplies the applied half from its own prior work rather than borrowing it.

Two honest caveats about how much weight this can bear. First, because firms chose what to build on, the estimate absorbs whatever else distinguishes them: if the firms with better applied science are also the firms with better chemists and better trial designers, that shows up here as an effect of applied science. This is a pattern to explain, not a lever to pull. Second — and I would rather say this than not — I was not able to read the full text of this paper, only its published abstract, so the direction and significance of the result are as the author states them and no magnitude appears anywhere in this post. Elsevier does not serve the article to anything but a browser.

So the right language is the best evidence suggests, not we know. What makes me take it more seriously than a lone correlation is that the outcome variable is unusually hard to argue with — actual market approval, not forward citations — and that four other literatures, with entirely different designs and datasets, point the same way.

Why the map fails inside a body

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth spelling out because it explains why drugs should be different from, say, semiconductors.

Think of every screening model, animal model, or mechanistic theory as a test that tells you whether a compound will work in humans. What makes a test useful is not how fast you can run it but how well its verdict correlates with the truth. John Scannell and Jim Bosley call this predictive validity, and in When quality beats quantity (2016) they build a decision-theoretic simulation of drug discovery around it. This is a model rather than a measurement: they are not estimating what happened, they are working out what must follow if screening tests relate to clinical reality the way the evidence suggests they do.

The result is startling once you see it. Over much of the parameter space they examine, an absolute change of 0.1 in the correlation between a screening model's output and true clinical utility — from 0.4 to 0.5, say — affects your hit rate more than a tenfold increase in how many compounds you can screen. A 0.1 correlation shift feels small and unmeasurable; a tenfold throughput gain feels enormous. The arithmetic says the small, invisible thing dominates.

A toy version makes this concrete. Suppose 1 in 100 compounds really works, and your screen flags 10% of everything as promising. If the screen is only weakly related to the truth, most of what it flags is noise: you screen 10,000 compounds, flag 1,000, and perhaps 20 of them are real — a 2% hit rate among your flags. Now make the screen meaningfully better without touching its speed, so that it catches most real winners and flags far fewer duds. Flag only 200 compounds and get 60 real ones, and your hit rate is 30%. To reach that by brute force instead, you would have to screen well over a hundred thousand compounds — and every one of those extra screens costs money and time. Speed is linear. Validity is not.

That reframes the whole problem. Basic science is very good at producing rules: this receptor does that, this pathway regulates this. Rules are what make a screening model possible in the first place. But the validity of the model — whether its verdict tracks what happens in a living, variable human — is an empirical property that only contact with real bodies establishes. Anckaert's phrase for the gap is the one I would use: between the predictive rules of basic research and the unpredictable outcomes that emerge in variable states of human physiology.

Michelle Gittelman got to the same place from history rather than decision theory. In The revolution re-visited (2016) she compares two research paradigms in medicine. In the older clinical paradigm, discovery starts with observation of actual patients and proceeds by feedback: you notice something, you try something, the patient tells you whether it worked. In the genetics paradigm that displaced it, discovery starts with a precisely specified molecular target, and feedback from patients arrives only at the very end of a long and expensive pipeline. Her argument is that biological complexity favours the first, and — this is the sharp part — that the switch to the second was propelled by post-war institutional changes largely disconnected from any evidence that it worked better.

I want to be careful about the evidentiary status here. Gittelman's paper is a historical and conceptual comparison, not a measurement; Scannell and Bosley's is a simulation. Neither is an estimate of the effect this post claims. What they provide is a reason the effect should exist, produced independently and years before Anckaert's data. That is worth something, but it is not a second data point.

The people who carry both halves

If the argument is right, then the scarce input is not basic science and it is not applied science. It is people and organisations that hold both.

The most direct evidence comes from a study of who actually leads clinical trials. Carolin Haeussler and Anne Assmus examined 3,889 clinical trials (2021), publicly and industry funded, and compared their success rates according to what kind of experience the lead investigator brought. Trials led by investigators with a balanced set of skills across both basic and applied science — they call this horizontal skill — were more likely to succeed, under both funding sources. Investigators whose experience was spread across many different disease areas did worse, though those in industry-funded trials could offset that if they also had basic-research skills. Breadth across the basic-applied divide helps; breadth across diseases does not. This too is a comparison across trials rather than an experiment.

Anckaert has also looked at the supply side of this, with David Cassiman and Bruno Cassiman, in Fostering practice-oriented and use-inspired science in biomedical research (2020). Two findings. First, basic research done by clinical scientists — people who see patients — draws more on the applied literature and triggers more applied and industrial follow-on work than a set of randomly matched basic-science articles. Second, they track what happens to clinicians who win the Senior Clinical Investigator fellowship from Research Foundation Flanders, which partly buys them out of their clinical duties: they publish more and shift toward basic science that integrates bench and bedside. The bottleneck, in other words, is time — the people best placed to produce the combined knowledge base are the people whose days are consumed by patient care.

That fellowship is the closest thing to a policy lever anywhere in this literature, and it is worth being clear that it is a competitive award rather than a coin flip: the clinicians who win one differ from those who do not, in ways that plausibly also affect what they publish.

One mind, not two people

Now the finding that complicates the story, and then resolves it. Ayfer Ali and Michelle Gittelman studied patents and licences from two prominent academic medical centres over thirty years (2016), asking which patents went on to be licensed by a firm — a market test of whether an invention is worth developing. Inventions from teams made up of clinicians were more likely to be licensed than inventions from teams of basic scientists. But inventions by mixed teams of clinicians and basic scientists were not more likely to be licensed. Putting the two paradigms in a room together did not produce the combination benefit. They read this as evidence that the two are distinct cultures of work that "defy a simple arithmetic of combining the skills of individuals on teams."

Read carelessly, that looks like it sinks the claim. Read carefully, it sharpens it, because of one further result in the same paper: what raises the odds of a licence is the presence of an MD or an MD-PhD, and — their words — the only time a PhD scientist has a positive effect on licensing is when that scientist is also a clinician.

That is the distinction the rest of this post needs. It is not that combining basic and applied knowledge fails; it is that the combination has to happen inside one head, not across a table. A clinician-scientist who has done both carries the tacit, contextual knowledge of what actually happens to patients and the formal machinery of mechanism at once, and can tell which of the rules apply here. Two specialists, each holding one half, apparently cannot assemble the same thing by talking.

Anckaert's data point the same way from the firm's side: his combination effect is strongest when the firm supplies the applied insight from its own prior work. Internalised, not outsourced. Two very different settings — academic patent licensing and pharmaceutical approval — landing on the same shape.

I would still hold this a rung below the main claim. Ali and Gittelman's outcome is licensing at two American academic medical centres, not drug approval, and it is an observational comparison with controls rather than an experiment. It is convergence, not proof.

Objection! This is just an argument against funding basic research

It is not, and I want to be emphatic because this is the misreading the claim invites.

The evidence that public basic research pays off is strong and comes from designs far better than anything in this post. Pierre Azoulay, Joshua Graff Zivin, Danielle Li and Bhaven Sampat found a way to compare research areas that got more NIH money against areas that got less for reasons unrelated to how promising they were — the NIH awards grants in rank order within fixed budget envelopes, so an area can end up funded or not because of where an arbitrary cutoff happened to fall. Using that variation (2019), they find a $10 million increase in NIH funding produces a net increase of 2.7 private-sector patents. Ufuk Akcigit, Douglas Hanley and Nicolas Serrano-Velarde, in Back to Basics (2021), estimate large spillovers from basic research inside a structural growth model. And Belderbos, Kelchtermans and Leten, studying large R&D-spending pharmaceutical firms from 1995 to 2015 (2022), find that internal basic research does raise innovation performance — mostly by building the firm's capacity to absorb science done elsewhere.

That last mechanism is the key to reconciling everything. Basic research can be enormously valuable as an input to the process — it generates the rules, it builds the ability to read other people's rules, it seeds the applied work that comes later — while still being a poor predictor of whether this particular compound survives phase 2. Those are different questions with different units of analysis. A field can badly need basic science in aggregate and still not get a specific drug approved with it.

There is a related asymmetry worth noting, because it distorts what gets done. Qing Ke scored 5 million MEDLINE-indexed biomedical papers (2020) on how basic or clinical each one is, and found a systematic citation disadvantage for clinically oriented work: it collects far fewer citations and is far less likely to become a hit paper than basic-science work, and the gap narrows but does not close over longer citation windows. If the contextual knowledge is what converts candidates into drugs, and the metrics we use to evaluate scientists systematically undervalue producing it, we have built an incentive system pointed away from the bottleneck.

Objection! Firms already left basic science, and for other reasons

Large firms have in fact retreated from basic research. Ashish Arora, Sharon Belenzon and Andrea Patacconi matched Web of Science publications to US publicly traded firms and documented the decline of science in corporate R&D (2018) between 1980 and 2006: company scientists publish less, and the market value attributable to scientific research fell while the value attributable to patents held steady. Federico Bignone and Francesco Lissoni, looking at US corporate publications from 1980 to 2014 (2025), find corporate science has become progressively more applied and less basic than academic science — firms drifting, in Stokes's terms, out of Pasteur's quadrant toward Edison's.

Tempting as it is, I cannot read this as firms discovering what this post argues. There are at least two rival explanations, and both are better identified. Arora, Belenzon and Lia Sheer, using 800,000 corporate publications (2021), show that firms produce more research when they use it internally and less when rivals use it — a story about appropriability, not efficacy. And Joshua Krieger, Danielle Li and Dimitris Papanikolaou, in Missing novelty in drug development (2022), measure how chemically similar each new candidate is to drugs that came before, and then watch what firms do when Medicare Part D arrives and unexpectedly enlarges the market for drugs sold to the elderly. Firms whose net worth jumped moved toward more novel candidates — which is what you would expect if they had been holding back out of financial caution rather than scientific judgement. That is a story about finance.

Three explanations, one observed retreat. The corporate-behaviour evidence is consistent with this post's claim but does not distinguish it from the alternatives, and I would not cite it as support.

Objection! Maybe drugs are special

Probably they are, and that is a feature of the claim rather than a defect in it.

Fleming and Sorenson's own paper says the value of science varies systematically across applications — it helps most where components are tightly coupled and the search landscape is rugged. Kwanghui Lim compared semiconductors and pharmaceuticals from 1981 to 1997 (2004) and found innovation in semiconductors depends mainly on applied research while pharmaceutical innovation is tied to both. Anckaert and Bruno Cassiman, tracing who produces the novel, high-value inventions in lithium-ion batteries (2025), find they come from inventors building on their own accumulated knowledge of the field rather than from outsiders arriving with fresh perspectives — another case where the abstract-knowledge story needs a boundary.

The unifying idea is not "science does not help." It is that the usefulness of abstract knowledge depends on how well the systems we can model resemble the system we actually care about. In semiconductors, the fabricated device is close to the modelled device. In medicine, the assay is a long way from the patient. Where that gap is wide, contextual knowledge is not a supplement to fundamental understanding — it is the thing that makes fundamental understanding usable.

Bottom line

I read the evidence as saying: for drugs, a scientific base made only of fundamental, predictive knowledge is not enough to get a candidate approved, and the combination with contextualized applied knowledge is what does the work — with the combination most likely to pay off when it sits inside a single person or firm rather than being assembled across a collaboration.

The best direct evidence is one observational study of 5,613 candidates, whose full text I could not obtain. The mechanism has an independent decision-theoretic and historical case behind it. And three separate literatures — on trial investigators, on academic medical centre inventors, and on clinical scientists — point the same way from different data, with the academic medical centre evidence supplying the "one mind, not two people" refinement.

I would bound that fairly tightly. It is a claim about pharmaceuticals, where the gap between what we can model and what we need to predict is unusually wide. It rests on correlations, not on any experiment. It says nothing against funding basic research — the aggregate case for that is stronger than the case for anything in this post. And no paper here measures the thing you would most want measured: how much the odds of approval actually move.

What follows is more speculative than anything above it. If I had to give a probability that the core finding survives a genuinely causal replication with better data, I would say something like two in three — high enough to act on if you are choosing what kind of science to fund at the margin, not high enough to restructure a research portfolio around.

What we don't know

  • How big is the effect? Nowhere in this literature is there a number a policymaker could use. Does adding applied science to a candidate's knowledge base move approval odds by one percentage point or ten?
  • Does the "one mind" result generalise? It rests on licensing at two American academic medical centres. Whether clinician-scientists inside pharmaceutical firms show the same advantage over clinician-plus-scientist teams is untested.
  • Would it replicate causally? The ideal design would use variation in the availability of applied knowledge in a disease area that is unrelated to how promising the area is — something like the NIH funding-rule trick, applied to the composition rather than the level of science.
  • Does it hold outside pharmaceuticals? Agriculture, materials, and climate technologies all have wide gaps between the modelled system and the target system. Nobody has run this test there.
  • Has it changed since 2008? The sample ends before machine-learning models of protein structure and before large-scale real-world-evidence data. Both are, in effect, attempts to raise predictive validity. Whether they have is an open empirical question — and on Scannell and Bosley's arithmetic, a 0.1 improvement would matter more than any amount of extra screening capacity.

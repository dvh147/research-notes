fleming2001 Fleming (2001) Recombinant uncertainty in technological search Management Science (topman) 10.1287/mnsc.47.1.117.10671 Foundational result: new combinations less useful on average, more variable. Mechanism + toy example.
vbv2016 Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016) Measuring technological novelty with patent-based indicators Research Policy (fieldinnov) 10.1016/j.respol.2015.11.010 The spine: novelty lowers mean, raises dispersion + odds of an extreme positive outlier; 5.3M families.

uzzi2013 Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013) Atypical combinations and scientific impact Science (web, non-corpus) 10.1126/science.1240474 Upside recipe: conventional core + atypical dash ~2x hit rate; pure novelty barely beats baseline.
conti2014 Conti (2014) Do non-competition agreements lead firms to pursue risky R&D projects? Strategic Management Journal (topman) 10.1002/smj.2155 Closest-to-causal anchor: two opposite-signed state law changes (TX 1994, FL 1996) push inventions into BOTH tails + novel areas.
singh-fleming2010 Singh & Fleming (2010) Lone inventors as sources of breakthroughs: myth or reality? Management Science (topman) 10.1287/mnsc.1090.1072 The two tails move independently; collaboration trims failures and fattens breakthroughs (quantile regs).
arts-veugelers2015 Arts & Veugelers (2015) Technology familiarity, recombinant novelty, and breakthrough invention Industrial and Corporate Change (fieldinnov) 10.1093/icc/dtu029 The sharpest counter-evidence: in biotech, recombinant novelty raises the mean AND breakthroughs AND cuts failures -- the authors reject the variance reading outright.
arts-fleming2018 Arts & Fleming (2018) Paradise of novelty -- or loss of human capital? Organization Science (topman) 10.1287/orsc.2018.1216 Exploring new fields: novelty up, value down; downside muted by expert collaborators / science (FE + 2SLS).
kaplan-vakili2015 Kaplan & Vakili (2015) The double-edged sword of recombination in breakthrough innovation Strategic Management Journal (topman) 10.1002/smj.2294 Novelty != value: cognitive novelty (local search) vs economic value (broad recombination); only 20/189 do both.

Verification status (revised 2026-07-28, after reading the full texts). All eleven cited papers with a DOI were obtained and read in full; nine have structured summaries in the post's local (unpublished) summaries/ folder. Remaining caveats:

  • Conti (2014) -- full text now read; the earlier "direction only" caveat is withdrawn. The paper itself, however, reports no marginal effects, odds ratios or predicted probabilities anywhere -- only raw logit coefficients -- so the post still gives direction and significance rather than effect sizes. Identification is two opposite-signed state law changes (Texas 1994, Florida 1996), each with a single treated state.
  • Arts & Veugelers (2015) -- tail magnitudes now table-confirmed (breakthrough rate 2.2% vs 0.9%; zero-citation share 40% -> 31%, Table 2, p. 1231). The paper explicitly rejects a variance interpretation for biotechnology: "the biotech data therefore do not support the increasing variance effect but a mean shifting effect" (p. 1237).
  • Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016) -- correction. Version 1's magnitudes were read from a KU Leuven working paper, cited here as MSI_1509; the file was in fact MSI_1319 (May 2013), which uses a different three-indicator framework (New Origins / New Functionality / New Impact), a sample of 2,297,499 applications rather than 5.3 million families, and reports the opposite sign on the mean. All magnitudes now come from the published Research Policy article. The working paper is retained separately for reference.
  • Wang, Veugelers & Stephan (2017) -- magnitudes verified against the full text, but note that this paper's novelty score is the one questioned by both measurement papers cited below: Fontana et al. (2020) find it "finds it difficult to tell novel and non-novel articles apart", and Bornmann et al. (2019) fail it on convergent validity against expert assessments while passing the Uzzi-derived score. The revised post says so explicitly.
  • Uzzi et al. (2013) -- the PDF obtained by the fetcher is the Supplementary Materials, not the five-page article (both carry the same title, so title-based verification passed). Magnitudes were read from a separate copy of the printed article plus that supplement.
  • Fleming (2001) and Singh & Fleming (2010) -- scanned or damaged text layers; the abstract and introduction of each were transcribed by hand and checked against the page images, and Singh & Fleming's coefficients were read from rendered table images because the text layer strips decimal points.

Take any patent and ask it a slightly strange question: how far are you from science? Not in miles, and not in years — in citations. A patent that lists a scientific paper among its references is one step from science. A patent that cites no paper but cites another patent that cites a paper is two steps away. Keep walking the citation chain until you hit a research article, count the steps, and you have measured something real: the invention's distance to science.

In 2017, Mohammad Ahmadpoor and Benjamin Jones did exactly this, at scale. In "The dual frontier", published in Science, they linked 4.8 million U.S. patents to 32 million research articles through the full web of citations and measured how far each invention stood from the nearest paper. Two things jumped out. First, the two worlds are astonishingly close: 80% of cited research papers eventually link forward to a future patent, and 61% of patents link backward to a prior paper, with most linked work standing just two to four steps from the other side. Second — and this is the thread I want to pull — the patents and papers sitting right on the boundary between science and technology are the most important ones in their own domains.

That second fact is the seed of a claim I think the evidence now supports fairly well: the most valuable inventions are the ones that stand closest to recent science. Patents that build directly on scientific papers are worth substantially more than otherwise-similar patents that don't; the premium shrinks step by step as you move away from the science frontier; and the biggest hits — and the biggest flops — cluster right at the edge.

Why care? Because this is, in the end, an argument about how much basic research a society should pay for. Almost everyone agrees in the abstract that science "underpins" growth. Distance-to-science turns that pious abstraction into a measurable gradient with dollar figures attached — and the figures bear on a budget debate that is very much live.

How far is an invention from science?

Start with the measuring stick, because everything rests on it. Imagine a 2016 materials-science paper, call it P. A 2019 patent, A, cites P directly on its front page: A is distance 1 — it stands on the science frontier. A later patent B cites A but no scientific paper of its own: B is distance 2. A patent C that cites B is distance 3. The distance-to-science is just the length of the shortest citation path back to a piece of published research. Distance 1 inventions are the ones reaching straight into the lab; high-distance inventions are improvements on improvements, far downstream of whatever science set them in motion.

The plumbing that makes this possible is patent-to-paper citation data. The founding observation is old: in 1997 Francis Narin, Kimberly Hamilton and Dominic Olivastro reported an "increasing linkage between U.S. technology and public science," and found that 73% of the papers cited by U.S. industry patents were public science — authored at universities, government labs, and other public institutions — with only 27% coming from industry itself. For two decades that kind of analysis was artisanal and field-specific. Then Matt Marx and Aaron Fuegi industrialized it, releasing the open "Reliance on Science" dataset of front-page patent-to-paper citations worldwide (and a companion set of the in-text citations buried in the patent body). Suddenly anyone could compute distance-to-science for tens of millions of patents.

And the linkage is not a museum piece — it is growing fast. Studying biomedicine, Qing Ke found that citations from patents to science have been doubling roughly every 2.9 years, with more than half of the cited science coming from universities. So the raw material for this post — inventions that visibly stand on published research — is abundant and getting more so. The question is what that proximity is worth.

Closer to science, worth more

Here is the core result, and it has now been found by several teams using different data and different value measures, which is the kind of convergence that should move your prior.

The cleanest dollar figures come from Joshua Krieger, Monika Schnitzer and Martin Watzinger's "Standing on the shoulders of science" (Strategic Management Journal, 2024; the earlier working-paper version circulated as CEPR DP13766). Comparing patents in the same technology class, they find that a patent building directly on science is worth about 5–18% more than one that doesn't when you compare within the same firm, and up to roughly 40% more when you let firms differ in how they do R&D. The earlier draft put the average gap at about $2.9 million per patent. Crucially, the premium is a gradient, not a cliff edge: it decays monotonically with distance and is essentially gone once an invention sits more than four citation-steps from any paper. In their numbers, a patent that cites science directly was worth around $17.7 million on average; at distance two, about $14 million; beyond distance three, $8–10 million.

It is not just whether you touch science but which science you touch. In "Science Quality and the Value of Inventions" (Science Advances, 2019), Felix Poege, Dietmar Harhoff, Fabian Gaessler and Stefano Baruffaldi matched 4.8 million patent families to 43 million papers and scored the quality of each cited paper by its early citations. Two findings stand out. First, there is brutal selection in what science gets used at all: papers below the median in scientific quality are almost never cited by a patent, while the share rises to about 40% at the top 1% and roughly 80% at the very top (the top paper in ten thousand). Second, among patents that do build on science, value rises monotonically with the quality of that science — patents standing on top-rank science are about twice as valuable as those standing on low-rank science, which are themselves no more valuable than patents with no science link at all. As the authors put it, what counts as "excellent" inside science tends to lead to outstanding outcomes in the commercial world too. (They guard against the obvious reverse story — that a hit patent draws attention to the paper — by scoring each paper only on citations in its first three years, before the patent could plausibly have moved them.)

Bibliometric work points the same way from the science side. Martin Meyer showed that papers cited by patents are far more likely to be scientifically influential: against a 4% baseline rate of being highly cited, 13.8% of papers cited once or twice by patents, and 23.5% of those cited more often, land in the top tier of scientific impact. Importance in science and usefulness to technology travel together.

How confident should we be? The correlation — closer to science, more valuable — is about as well established as innovation economics gets: it shows up in independent datasets, across multiple value measures (citations, market value, renewal fees), across fields, and across decades. I'd call it fairly convincing. The leap to causation is where we should slow down, and I'll come back to it.

Where the big hits live

The averages hide the most interesting part. Krieger, Schnitzer and Watzinger find that patents close to science are over-represented in both tails of the value distribution — they produce more big hits and more outright flops — and that science-intensive patents are more novel. Standing on science is not a safe, incremental move. It is a bet with a fatter tail on both ends.

That should sound familiar to anyone who has read the case that novelty is a high-variance bet: reaching for distant or new knowledge doesn't reliably raise your average outcome so much as widen the spread. Reaching into recent science looks like the same trade in a different guise. It buys you the tail.

Why would science do that? The best mechanism I know comes from Lee Fleming and Olav Sorenson's "Science as a map in technological search" (SMJ, 2004). Think of inventing as searching over combinations of components for ones that work. Without a map, you search locally — tweak what already works, take small safe steps. Science, Fleming and Sorenson argue, functions as a map of that landscape: it points inventors toward useful combinations, lets them skip provably dead-end paths, and gives them reason to keep going through discouraging early failures. The map is most valuable precisely where blind search fails worst — when the components are highly coupled, so that changing one thing breaks three others. Those tightly-coupled problems are exactly the hard, ambitious, breakthrough-prone ones. So science concentrates its help where the upside is largest, which is why science-near work piles up in the tails.

The historical record agrees that breakthroughs lean on science more than ordinary inventions do. In his classic 1991 survey of academic research and industrial innovation, Edwin Mansfield asked the R&D heads of 76 major American firms how many of their 1975–85 products and processes "could not have been developed, without substantial delay, in the absence of recent academic research." The industry-wide answer was about 11% of new products and 9% of new processes — but among innovations the firms themselves classed as radical breakthroughs, the figure was 14%, against roughly 7% for inventions overall. More recent text-based work finds the same flavor: studying pharmaceuticals, Keye Wu and coauthors report that patents with scientific references are measurably more disruptive than those without, and that the gap has widened over time. The pattern even shows up in the clean-energy transition, where Tobias Buchmann and Patrick Wolf find that the breakthrough inventions in solar PV and wind lean disproportionately on prior scientific discoveries.

How much is the seedbed worth?

If the most valuable and most radical inventions cluster near science, the natural follow-up is a budget question: what is the return on funding the science in the first place?

Mansfield actually did this back-of-the-envelope, and it is worth walking through because the answer is large. He found that the academic research underlying these industrial innovations reached the market with a mean lag of about seven years, and that the new products which leaned on recent academic research generated roughly $24 billion in 1985 sales across his seven industries. Feeding the benefits, the lags, and a deliberately conservative set of assumptions into a present-value calculation, he estimated a social rate of return to academic research of about 28% — and 25% even under his most cautious assumptions. A quarter on the dollar, year after year, is several times a normal corporate hurdle rate. The estimate is rough and Mansfield said so; but the order of magnitude has held up.

It holds up, in particular, against cleaner identification. Andrew Toole, using data on U.S. National Institutes of Health funding, found that public basic biomedical research raises the entry of new drugs, acting at the earliest, most science-intensive stage of discovery, with a positive social return. That matters because an influential earlier study had found the market size for a disease drives drug entry while detecting no role for publicly funded science; with better measures of NIH investment, Toole finds the basic-research channel is there after all. From Europe, Jay Prakash Nagar, Stefano Breschi and Andrea Fosfuri show that science funded by the European Research Council attracts more patent citations than comparable un-funded research, and that the patents building on ERC science are themselves higher quality. These are not natural experiments of the gold-standard kind, but they push the same direction the correlations do: feed the frontier and valuable invention follows, with a lag.

The "best evidence suggests" rung of the ladder feels right here: a strong and consistent body of work, anchored by a couple of credible quasi-experiments, pointing to a high social return on public science.

Objection: is it the science, or the scientists?

Now the hard part. Everything above is, at bottom, a correlation: inventions near science are more valuable. The uncomfortable possibility is that science isn't doing the work — that the firms, teams, and projects which reach for science are simply better to begin with, and would have produced valuable patents whatever they cited.

This is a real worry, and the literature has chipped at it rather than demolished it. Krieger, Schnitzer and Watzinger's within-firm comparison is the most direct response: holding the firm fixed, so that we compare a company's science-based patents to its own other patents, shrinks the premium from ~40% to 5–18% — but it does not erase it. Poege and coauthors' three-year citation window rules out the reverse story that a blockbuster patent retroactively inflates the science it cites. So selection clearly explains part of the raw gap, and just as clearly does not explain all of it.

There's a subtler version of the objection, and it is the most important qualifier in this whole post. Ashish Arora, Sharon Belenzon and Bernardo Dionisi, in "First-mover advantage and the private value of public science", find that the average private return to using public science is actually small — especially in crowded fields — because public knowledge is, by definition, available to your competitors too. The value accrues mainly to whoever uses a given piece of science first and can fence off a broad patent before rivals pile in. So part of what "distance to science" is picking up is a first-mover race over freely available knowledge, not a magic ingredient in the science itself.

But notice where that leaves us. If the private return to public science is small precisely because the science is public and non-excludable, while the social value (Mansfield's 28%, Toole's drugs) is large, then we have walked straight into the textbook rationale for funding it publicly. A high-social-value, low-private-value input is exactly the thing markets under-supply and governments are supposed to pay for. The same wedge runs through the gap between the private and social value of innovation generally; here it has a name and a mechanism.

Objection: good science isn't always useful science

Here is the finding that complicates the cheerful version of this story, and it deserves its own section because it is genuinely in tension with the rest.

Michelle Gittelman and Bruce Kogut, studying 116 biotechnology firms, asked "Does good science lead to valuable knowledge?" (Management Science, 2003) — and answered, awkwardly, not really. Firms that did more and better science patented more. But the individual scientific papers that were most important as science were negatively associated with the firm's most valuable inventions. Science and innovation, they argued, run on "conflicting selection logics": the qualities that make a paper a landmark in its field are not the qualities that make it a useful input to a marketable product. The people who reconcile the two — the versatile scientists who both publish and patent — are rare and valuable precisely because the two worlds don't naturally align.

How do we square this with Poege's "top science → twice as valuable patents"? I think they coexist once you're careful about the unit. Poege and Meyer are telling us that across the whole system, the science that gets pulled into technology skews toward the high-quality end, and patents that draw on better science are worth more. Gittelman and Kogut are telling us that inside a given firm, betting your R&D on your own most celebrated science is not a reliable route to your most valuable product. Both can be true. But the second should keep us honest: "stand close to great science" is a description of where value tends to be found, not a recipe any single lab can follow by chasing prestige.

Objection: the seedbed may be shrinking

One more honest dent. Arora, Belenzon and Patacconi document a "decline of science in corporate R&D": between 1980 and 2006, large American firms published steadily less science, and the market value the stock market attached to corporate research fell, even as the value attached to patents held up. Their summary is that firms still want the golden eggs of science but are increasingly unwilling to feed the goose — they have outsourced discovery to universities and startups and kept development for themselves. Tellingly, the same authors elsewhere find that firms which do still publish are rewarded with higher market value, which is the flip side of the retreat: science still pays, but fewer big firms are willing to do it in-house.

This doesn't contradict the central claim — inventions near science are still the valuable ones — but it reframes it. The supply of corporate science behind those valuable inventions is thinning, which throws more weight onto public science as the seedbed, exactly when public-science budgets are under pressure.

A measurement caveat worth stating

Distance-to-science rides entirely on citations, and citations are noisy. Martin Meyer warned early that a patent citing a paper "hardly represents a direct link" of knowledge in many cases — some citations are legal boilerplate, examiner additions, or strategic padding. More recently, Jian Wang and Suzan Verberne found that the references on a patent's front page and those buried in its text barely overlap, and that conclusions about patent value can flip depending on which set you use. None of this overturns the gradient — it survives across teams that made different measurement choices — but it should keep anyone from treating a single distance number as gospel.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That science and technology are densely, measurably linked — that you can take any invention and compute how far it stands from the nearest paper — is well established. That inventions standing closer to recent, higher-quality science are on average substantially more valuable, and that the biggest hits cluster near the frontier, is fairly convincingly true: it replicates across the best datasets and survives the most obvious confounders, even if it is not a clean experiment. And that public science is a high-return seedbed for valuable invention is, I think, the best reading of the evidence, supported in direction by NIH and ERC quasi-experiments and by Mansfield's order-of-magnitude social return — while the private return is small, which is the whole reason the funding has to be public.

What I would not claim: that standing near science causes value in the strong sense, cleanly separated from the quality of the people doing it; that "recent" science is unambiguously the right thing to chase (for disruption specifically, the recency relationship is murkier than for value); or that any individual lab can manufacture valuable patents by citing celebrated papers — Gittelman and Kogut are the standing rebuke to that. The honest headline keeps its qualifier: the most valuable inventions tend to stand closest to science, and that tendency is worth a great deal — most of it to society rather than to the inventor.

What we don't know

  • How causal is the gradient, really? We need more variation that moves an invention's distance-to-science without moving the quality of the team — patent-examiner assignment, funding shocks, the sudden disclosure of a new technique — to separate the map from the cartographer.
  • Does "recent" cut the way we think? Building on fresh science raises value, yet some evidence finds recency is negatively related to disruptiveness. Recent science may consolidate as often as it disrupts; the timing story is unfinished.
  • Where does the line between using and doing science settle? If corporations keep retreating from research while leaning ever harder on its output, the system's productivity hinges on a public seedbed whose budget is contested. How thin can the goose get before the eggs stop coming?
  • How much of the premium is appropriability versus knowledge? The first-mover result suggests a chunk of the value near science is a race to fence off public knowledge. Disentangling the racing from the building would sharpen the policy implication considerably.

The deepest thing this literature has done is turn an old slogan — science underpins technology — into a ruler. Vannevar Bush sold the postwar bargain on faith that basic research is "the pacemaker of technological progress." Eighty years on, we can finally measure the pace, in citation-steps and dollars. The measurement is messier than the slogan, the causality is softer than we'd like, and the inventor captures little of the gain. But the ruler keeps reading the same thing: the good stuff is close to the frontier.

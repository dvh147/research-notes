In 1955, most scientific papers had a single author. The lone scholar — Einstein at his desk, Barbara McClintock with her corn plants — was not just a romantic image but the statistical norm. That world is gone. Across virtually every field, most papers today are written by teams, almost all of the high-impact ones are, and the teams keep getting bigger. Wuchty, Jones, and Uzzi (2007), studying nearly 20 million research papers spanning the second half of the twentieth century, showed that teams had come to dominate the production of knowledge — and, crucially, that team-authored work is cited far more often than solo work. By that yardstick, the shift to team science looked like an unambiguous upgrade.

But there is an uncomfortable corollary, and it is the subject of this post. The same enlargement that made science more impactful may have made it less disruptive — less likely to crack open a new direction, more likely to deepen an existing one. The single most-cited piece of evidence is a 2019 Nature paper whose title is also its finding: Wu, Wang, and Evans (2019), "Large teams develop and small teams disrupt science and technology."

Here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence: across science and technology, large teams reliably push existing lines of work forward, while the rarer, direction-changing breakthroughs come disproportionately from small teams and solo authors. I read the pattern as real and unusually general. But two things stop me from stating it more strongly than "mostly." It is measured with a ruler that has come under heavy fire in the last few years. And it is a correlation about what small and large teams tend to do — not proof that adding a co-author smothers a breakthrough. Most of this post is about taking those two caveats seriously, because that is where the interesting action is.

I'll go in four steps: the pattern and how big the effect is; why we built big teams anyway; what a big team actually does differently; and then the two objections — the bent ruler and the chooser problem — that decide how much to believe.

A third co-author is where it starts to fade

First, the measure, because everything rides on it. The "disruption index" was introduced by Funk and Owen-Smith (2017) and applied at scale by Wu and coauthors. The idea is clever and worth a toy example. Take a paper P that builds on earlier work — it cites references R1, R2, R3. Now watch who cites P afterward. If later papers cite P and also keep citing R1–R3, then P slots neatly into an ongoing conversation: it consolidates. But if later papers cite P instead of the references it was built on — if P comes to eclipse its own intellectual parents — then P disrupts: it becomes the new starting point, and the old work fades from view.

Concretely: imagine 100 papers go on to cite P. If 80 of them also cite P's references, P scores strongly negative (consolidating). If 80 cite P but ignore its references, P scores strongly positive (disruptive). The index runs from −1 to +1, and it is doing something genuinely different from counting citations: a paper can be enormously cited and yet purely consolidating (a better mousetrap everyone adopts), or modestly cited and sharply disruptive (a small paper that quietly rerouted a field).

Run that index across roughly 65 million papers, patents, and software products from 1954 to 2014, and the relationship with team size is stark. Disruption falls as teams grow — and it doesn't take a crowd. The decline sets in once a team gains its third or fourth member. At the extreme, Wu and coauthors estimate that a 50-author paper is about 70% less likely to be disruptive than a solo-authored one; for patents the gap is around 30%, for software around 50%. The differences are largest precisely among the highest-impact work, where it matters most.

Two features make me take this more seriously than a single regression. The pattern holds within the same researcher's career — the same scientist tends to produce more disruptive work on her smaller-team projects than on her larger-team ones — so it isn't just that disruptive people happen to work alone. And it survives an external sanity check: Nobel-prize-winning papers are nearly three times more likely to carry the small-team "disruptive" signature than matched controls, which is reassuring evidence that the index is tracking something we'd recognize as a breakthrough rather than a statistical mirage.

The most convincing corroboration, though, comes from a study that doesn't use the disruption index at all. Lee, Walsh, and Wang (2015) measure novelty directly — whether a paper makes unusual combinations of prior knowledge — and find that team size has an inverted-U relationship with novelty: novelty rises as a team grows from one to a few members, then falls as the team keeps expanding. Meanwhile team size has a continually increasing relationship with citation impact. That is the develop/disrupt split stated in different words, from an independent measure: bigger teams reliably buy impact, but novelty — the raw material of disruption — peaks early and then erodes. The "develop" half of the story is on especially firm ground: from Wuchty, Jones, and Uzzi (2007) to Breitzman and Thomas (2015), who find that patents with eight or more inventors are cited significantly more than smaller-team patents, the evidence that large teams produce high-impact, well-developed work is about as solid as bibliometrics gets.

Why we built big teams anyway

If small teams disrupt, why has science marched relentlessly toward big ones? Not out of foolishness. The best answer is Jones's (2009) "burden of knowledge." As a field matures, there is simply more to learn before you can reach its frontier. Innovators respond exactly as you'd expect: they spend longer in training, they specialize more narrowly, and — because no single narrowed specialist can span a hard problem anymore — they combine into teams. Jones documents the fingerprints of this process: the age at which people make their first invention keeps rising, expertise keeps narrowing, and team sizes keep growing.

This matters for how we read the disruption finding. The growth of teams is not a bug; it is a rational adaptation to a real problem. A modern question in genomics or particle physics or machine learning genuinely cannot be answered by one person in a study. So the trade-off, if it exists, is not "we accidentally made science worse." It is "we bought something valuable — the ability to develop hard, knowledge-intensive problems — and may have paid for it in the currency of disruption." That framing is the honest one, and it raises the stakes of the rest of the argument: if the trade-off is real, the question for funders and lab directors is not "teams or no teams" but "have we tilted so far toward scale that we are starving the small-team work that resets fields?"

What a big team does differently

Why would size and disruption trade off at all? Two mechanisms, both with supporting evidence.

The first is the division of labor. Haeussler and Sauermann (2020) show that as teams grow and become more interdisciplinary, they reorganize internally — more specialization, more parceling of tasks among members, more interdependence. That structure is superb for executing a known plan: you can run more experiments, cover more subfields, and polish more thoroughly. But the integrative, slightly reckless leap that changes a field's direction is hard to assign as a sub-task on a Gantt chart. Specialization optimizes the development of a direction you have already chosen.

The second mechanism is about what a team reaches for. Wu and coauthors note that large teams tend to build on recent, popular work — the hot citation of the moment — and get rewarded with immediate attention, whereas small teams and solo authors more often dig into older, less fashionable ideas and are recognized only with a lag. Sheng and coauthors (2023) put numbers on the intuition, showing that the disruptiveness of a paper is systematically related to the recency and the homogeneity of the prior knowledge it draws on: work that reaches back to older and more varied sources tends to disrupt, while work built on a tight, recent cluster tends to consolidate. Reaching backward and outward is where new directions hide — and it is exactly the kind of search a big, deadline-driven, consensus-run team is least built to do.

I find the mechanisms plausible and mutually reinforcing, but I'd flag that this is the most "story-like" part of the evidence: these are associations consistent with a mechanism, not experiments that isolate one. Hold them as suggestive.

Objection! The ruler is bent

Now the objection that genuinely worries me. The whole edifice rests on the disruption index, and over the past three years that index has taken a beating.

The trouble surfaced through a sister finding. Park, Leahey, and Funk (2023) used the same index over time and reported that science is "becoming less disruptive": the disruption score of papers fell by something like 92% to nearly 100% between 1945 and 2010, and of patents by 79% to 92% between 1980 and 2010. It made headlines. It also made a target.

Petersen, Arroyave, and Pammolli (2024, 2025) argued that much of that decline is an artifact of "citation inflation," and the mechanism is worth seeing concretely. The disruption index depends on whether later papers, when they cite you, also cite your references. But the average reference list has been growing for decades — a paper in 1960 might cite 15 works; one in 2010, fifty or more. With longer reference lists, any two papers are mechanically more likely to overlap in what they cite, which pushes disruption scores down over time regardless of whether anything real changed about innovation. It is a bias baked into the ruler by the growth of the bibliography. By their accounting, the famous "decline of disruption" is largely this artifact — and, more pointedly for us, they find that once you account for it, the relationship between disruption and team size is "non-monotonic" and "weak," with a small effect that even flips to positive for teams of eight or more. If they are right, the headline of this post is on shaky ground.

That is the strongest version of the objection, and I won't wave it away. But two things keep the cross-sectional claim standing better than the time-trend claim.

First, the original authors answered specifically on team size. Lin, Li, and Wu (2025) point out that the disruption score keeps evolving as citations accumulate, and that small-team work accrues its citations more slowly than big-team work — so a short measurement window understates small-team disruption. When they lengthen the citation window to ten years or more, the negative team-size relationship reasserts itself. (They are the same group that produced the 2019 finding, so this is not a disinterested referee — but the citation-window point is a real and checkable one.) The upshot is an honest split: the time-trend claim ("science is dying") looks substantially artifactual, while the cross-sectional claim (small teams disrupt more, here and now) is more robust to the critique.

Second, and to me decisive, the develop/disrupt split does not depend on the disruption index alone. Lee, Walsh, and Wang's inverted-U in novelty was measured years earlier with completely different machinery and points the same way. A broader literature review by Leibel and Bornmann (2024) catalogs the index's real validity problems, and Bornmann and Tekles (2021) find only modest agreement between disruption scores and physicists' expert judgments of which papers were milestones — so I would not stake much on any single disruption number. But when an index with known flaws and an independent novelty measure with different flaws agree on the same qualitative pattern, the pattern is more credible than either tool alone. My read: trust the direction, distrust the decimal places, and discount the time-trend cousin heavily.

Objection! Small teams choose to be disruptive

Suppose the pattern is real. There is still a gap between "small teams produce more disruptive work" and the punchy version — "you can't scale your way to a breakthrough." The second is a causal claim, and the cleanest alternative explanation is selection.

Osorio and Bornmann (2021) lay it out as theory: a researcher who is sitting on a genuinely disruptive idea has an incentive to keep the team small — to protect the vision, move fast, and substitute her own intense effort for extra hands — while routine, developmental work naturally recruits more collaborators to share the load. On this account team size isn't a cause of (low) disruption so much as a symptom of what kind of project it is. The disruptive idea picks the small team, not the other way around.

This is not a knockdown of the finding, but it reshapes the lesson. Wu and coauthors' within-author result — the same person more disruptive on her smaller projects — narrows the selection story, because it can't be explained purely by "disruptive people work alone." But it doesn't close it, because the same person might still reserve small teams for her more adventurous ideas. So the defensible claim is about where disruption tends to come from, not a mechanical lever you can pull. The following is more speculative than what came before, but — if I had to bet on the policy reading, I'd say the evidence supports protecting and funding small-team, high-variance work as a distinct category, rather than assuming that a bigger grant and a bigger team will buy you a bigger breakthrough. That last inference is mine, and it runs a step ahead of what the studies strictly show.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That large teams develop and small teams disrupt is, as a contemporary correlation, fairly convincing: it shows up across 65 million records, holds within individual careers, validates against Nobel-recognized work, and is echoed by an independent novelty measure that owes nothing to the contested disruption index. On that, I'm comfortable saying the reflex "more people, more breakthroughs" is wrong — there is a real composition shift, where scale buys impact and incremental development at some cost to the direction-changing work.

Now the bounds, and there are two real ones. The metric is genuinely contested, so I trust the qualitative pattern far more than any specific magnitude, and I treat the famous "science is becoming less disruptive over time" as mostly an artifact of growing bibliographies rather than as support. And the relationship is correlational: small teams plausibly select disruptive problems, so "you can't scale your way to a breakthrough" overstates a causal lever the data don't cleanly establish. My confidence ladder runs fairly convincing on "disruptive work skews small-team," suggestive on the mechanism, and deliberately humble on both the exact size of the effect and the claim that shrinking a team would manufacture disruption. Big teams develop, small teams disrupt — mostly, and for now, as best a bent ruler can tell.

What we don't know

  • The missing experiment. Nobody has randomized team size and watched disruption roll in. Until something closer to an experiment exists — a funding lottery that varies team size, say — the causal question (does scale cause less disruption, or merely accompany it?) stays open.
  • How much is the ruler. The disruption index is improving but contested. We don't yet know how much of any team-size effect would survive a measure that fully corrects for citation inflation, reference-list growth, and field differences. The honest answer today is "less than the raw numbers, more than zero."
  • Where the optimum is. "Small disrupts" is not the same as "smallest is best." Some evidence points to medium teams for interdisciplinary work, and Melero and Palomeras (2015) find that generalists inside a team can recover some of what pure specialization loses. The policy-relevant question is the right portfolio of team sizes, which no one has pinned down.
  • Does it generalize across systems? Liu and coauthors (2021) find China's output is far more big-team-dominated than the rest of the world, with a steeper collapse in small-team work. Whether that predicts a future disruption deficit, or whether different research systems break the pattern, we don't know.

Most policies do their work only while they are switched on. Cut a tax and the behavior it discouraged drifts back; end a subsidy and the activity it propped up subsides. So here is a strange claim from the economics of innovation: a tax or subsidy applied for a while and then removed can permanently change the direction in which an entire industry invents. Switch the policy off, and the new direction stays on.

That sounds like a free lunch, and free lunches deserve suspicion. But the claim falls out of two ideas that each have solid empirical backing, and it has become one of the central arguments for acting early — rather than just acting hard — on the green transition. The logic is worth taking apart carefully, because it is half well-established fact and half elegant theory, and the honest version keeps the two straight.

The claim, stated plainly: the direction of innovation is path-dependent — firms tend to keep inventing in the technologies they already know — so a temporary push can have a permanent effect on which way technical change goes. If that is right, then for innovation policy when and which way you push can matter as much as how hard.

It rests on two ingredients. First, that the direction of invention responds to prices and incentives at all. Second, that invention builds on itself, so a lead, once established, tends to widen. Put them together and you get the payoff — and then two objections worth taking seriously.

Prices steer the direction of invention

Economists have a name for the first ingredient: directed technical change — the idea that the direction of innovation, not just its overall rate, responds to incentives. The intuition is old; John Hicks argued in The Theory of Wages (1932) that a rise in the price of a factor of production spurs invention aimed at economizing on it. The modern theory was formalized by Acemoglu (2002): innovation flows toward whatever is more profitable to improve, which depends on market size and on relative prices.

Does it actually happen? Here the evidence is about as good as this corner of economics gets, because the same pattern shows up across wildly different settings and research designs.

US farm labor, 1960s Bracero program ended, cutting labor Labor-saving mechanization induced San (2023)

Two of these deserve a closer look because they are unusually clean. Walker Hanlon (2015) uses the US Civil War as a natural experiment: when the Union blockade cut off Southern cotton, British textile mills were forced onto lower-quality Indian cotton (the Indian share of imports jumped from roughly 17% to 74% by 1862). British inventors responded by developing machinery specifically suited to the inferior fiber — patents in the relevant categories roughly tripled as a share — and, strikingly, the relative price of Indian cotton then rebounded to its pre-war level even though its relative supply stayed high. New technology had made the once-inferior input genuinely competitive. That is directed technical change caught in the act, driven by a shock (a war on another continent) that British inventors plainly did not cause.

The auto-industry study by Aghion, Dechezleprêtre, Hemous, Martin, and Van Reenen (2016) is the one closest to the climate question, and it is the workhorse for the rest of this post. They built firm-level patent data distinguishing "clean" auto innovation (electric, hybrid, hydrogen) from "dirty" (the internal combustion engine) across more than 3,000 patenting firms and individuals and 80 patent offices. Because fuel taxes differ across countries, firms selling into different markets face different effective fuel prices — and firms tilt their inventing accordingly. A 10% higher tax-inclusive fuel price is associated with roughly 10% more clean patenting and about 6% less dirty patenting — elasticities (the percentage change in patenting per 1% change in price) near +1.0 and −0.6. The mix of invention bends toward clean when dirty energy gets pricier.

So the first ingredient is not in much doubt: the direction of invention responds to prices, and the evidence is pretty unambiguous.

Invention has a memory

The second ingredient is what turns a temporary nudge into a lasting turn. Invention is cumulative: today's inventors build on the stock of what has already been invented. Whoever is ahead in a technology is therefore a more productive place to keep inventing — a self-reinforcing advantage that the theory of directed technical change calls path dependence and that, in plainer words, means invention has a memory.

The same auto paper measures this directly, and the numbers are the heart of the matter. A firm's own past stock of clean patents raises its current clean inventing with an elasticity of about 0.31; its past stock of dirty patents raises current dirty inventing with an elasticity of about 0.56 — two to four times larger than the cross-effects of one type of knowledge on the other. There are spillovers across firms too: a 10% larger economy-wide clean knowledge stock raises a firm's clean patenting by about 2.7%, while a larger dirty stock pushes it the other way. Firms, in short, keep inventing in the technologies they already know, and so does the industry around them.

It helps to see the mechanism with a toy example (the numbers are illustrative, not from a particular paper). Imagine two research lines, clean and dirty, where the productivity of inventing in each rises with that line's accumulated stock of past inventions. Suppose dirty starts with a stock of 100 and clean with 10. Because the dirty platform is bigger, the payoff to improving it is higher, so almost all R&D goes to dirty: next period dirty is 190 and clean is 11. The gap widens. Left alone, the economy locks into dirty — not because anyone decided dirty was better, but because dirty got there first.

Now impose a subsidy that makes clean the more attractive bet for, say, a decade. Research pours into clean; its stock climbs from 10 toward 120 while dirty stagnates. Now clean is the bigger platform. When the subsidy ends, inventors keep choosing clean on their own, because that is now where the cumulative advantage lies. A temporary policy flipped a self-reinforcing dynamic — and that is the whole trick.

How strong is that memory? Take the estimated own-clean elasticity of 0.31 at face value: doubling a firm's stock of clean know-how (a 100% increase) makes its clean inventing about 2^0.31 − 1 ≈ 24% more productive, and that bonus does not expire. Build the stock up once and the higher productivity persists. The best single set of estimates for this — Aghion et al.'s — is fairly convincing, though the authors are careful to note that their path-dependence coefficients are causal only if the knowledge-stock measures can be read that way; they are constructed from firms' historical footprints, not randomly assigned. I read the path-dependence ingredient as well-supported but resting more on one excellent study than on the convergent pile that backs the first ingredient.

Temporary push, permanent turn

Put the two ingredients together and the surprising conclusion follows. If the direction of invention responds to prices (ingredient one) and a lead in a technology is self-reinforcing (ingredient two), then a policy that holds long enough to flip which technology is ahead can be switched off without the economy sliding back. The push is temporary; the turn is permanent.

This is exactly the result in the foundational theory paper, Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn, and Hemous (2012). In their model, "when inputs are sufficiently substitutable, sustainable growth can be achieved with temporary taxes/subsidies that redirect innovation toward clean inputs." The key proviso is substitutability. If clean and dirty technologies are good substitutes — clean can stand in for dirty — then once clean pulls far enough ahead it carries the economy on its own, and the policy can be retired. If they are complements, you can never fully escape dirty, and the comfortable result evaporates.

Is the real world substitutable enough? The best estimate I could find says yes, but not lavishly: Papageorgiou, Saam, and Schulte (2017) put the elasticity of substitution between clean and dirty energy above one — around 1.8 in electricity generation, and 1.4–2.9 across industries. Greater than one means substitutes, which is the condition the "temporary policy works" result needs. But it is worth flagging that the most ambitious calibrations (keeping warming under 2°C in the 2012 model) want substitutability far higher than the data deliver, so the strongest versions of the optimism are on shakier empirical ground than the basic mechanism.

A second, quantitative paper sharpens the policy picture. Acemoglu, Akcigit, Hanley, and Kerr (2016) build a calibrated model of clean-versus-dirty energy innovation and ask what the optimal policy actually looks like. Their answer: act immediately, use both a research subsidy and a carbon tax, and make the intervention temporary — a front-loaded clean-research subsidy that is phased out over the following decades, plus a carbon tax that rises and then falls back toward zero once invention has been redirected. The research subsidy does the heavy lifting of steering inventors; the carbon tax handles the pollution externality. Crucially, you can eventually remove both.

Two numbers from that exercise make the stakes concrete. First, leaning on the carbon tax alone — skipping the research subsidy — forces the tax to climb more than threefold higher to do the same job, because one instrument is being asked to both price pollution and redirect R&D. Second, delay is expensive. Because the dirty lead compounds while you wait, putting off the intervention by 50 years raises the cost of the eventual switch enormously — in their baseline a delay turns a manageable transition into one that takes on the order of 300 years, with welfare losses that balloon under more pessimistic damage assumptions. The 2012 model makes the same point in its own units: a ten-year delay can cost up to several percent of consumption, forever. Path dependence cuts both ways, and waiting lets it cut against you.

Back in the auto data, Aghion et al. run the corresponding thought experiment and find the same shape. Freeze fuel prices and clean innovation overtakes dirty only well after 2030 — the lock-in is real. Impose a large, sustained fuel-price increase (they use +40%, "comparable with the increase that took place in the 1990s") and clean overtakes dirty in about fifteen years. They call path dependence a "double-edged sword": a barrier that holds the economy in dirty without policy, and an accelerant that compounds the shift once policy gets clean moving. Their own bottom line is the temporary-policy logic in plain words — "stronger action now, which could be relaxed in the future as the economy's stock of knowledge shifts in more of a clean direction."

Here I have to be honest about the seam in the argument. The theory delivers "temporary push, permanent turn" cleanly. The empirics establish the two ingredients that make it plausible, and the calibrated models show it working. But no study I found runs the literal experiment — impose a policy, remove it, and watch the new direction persist on its own. Aghion et al. simulate a permanent price increase and then argue that early action could later be relaxed; they do not simulate the relaxation. (What follows is more inference than demonstration:) I read the permanence claim as a well-grounded extrapolation from a measured mechanism — the memory is really there, and it really compounds — rather than a directly observed fact. That is why the title says a temporary push can permanently redirect innovation, not does.

Objection: don't induced bursts just fizzle out?

There is a real tension inside this literature, and it comes from the same paper that helped establish ingredient one. Popp (2002) finds not only that energy patents respond to energy prices, but also that the returns to piling more invention onto a given technology diminish — the marginal value of the existing knowledge stock falls over time. Taken alone, that implies an induced burst of inventing in some technology should be somewhat short-lived: you pick the low-hanging fruit and the surge fades.

Does that sink the permanence claim? I don't think so, but it sharpens what "permanent" can mean. Diminishing returns operate within a technology — clean innovation, like any other, gets harder as the easy ideas are used up. Path dependence operates across technologies — it determines which technology keeps getting cultivated at all. The permanence on offer is directional, not a perpetual-motion machine: a temporary push can durably change whether the economy's inventive effort is aimed at clean or dirty, even though neither line escapes the general tendency for ideas to get harder to find. The honest synthesis is that you can durably flip the direction of progress; you cannot buy yourself permanently faster progress with a one-time shove. Those are different claims, and only the first is what the temporary-policy logic actually requires.

Objection: does a real carbon price move invention at scale?

The cleanest real-world test of "a carbon price redirects invention" comes from Calel and Dechezleprêtre (2016), who study the EU Emissions Trading Scheme. Comparing regulated firms with carefully matched unregulated ones, they find the ETS raised regulated firms' low-carbon patenting by about 36% in the matched sample (around 10% when extrapolated more broadly) — and, importantly, it did not crowd out their other inventing, which actually ticked up slightly. So a carbon price really does bend invention toward clean, with no detectable cannibalization of the rest of a firm's R&D. That is the mechanism working in the field.

But the same paper supplies the sobering caveat. Because the firms the ETS regulated were only a small slice of all European inventors, the scheme's effect on the total stock of European low-carbon patents was tiny — on the order of 1%. A policy can redirect the firms it touches and still barely move the aggregate if it touches too few of the firms that actually do the inventing. And the study covers only the program's first years (2005–2009), so it speaks to redirection, not to permanence — it cannot tell us whether the tilt would survive the policy being withdrawn. The lesson is not that carbon prices fail to direct innovation; it is that coverage and credibility — reaching the inventive firms, and convincing them the signal will last long enough to be worth re-tooling for — are doing a lot of the work.

Bottom line

I come down here: the case that the direction of innovation responds to incentives and is path-dependent is strong — multiple clean designs across energy, autos, appliances, cotton, agriculture, and farm labor agree, and the auto data pin down a self-reinforcing knowledge stock with elasticities you can actually quantify. The further step — that a temporary policy can therefore permanently redirect technical change — is theoretically clean, satisfies its key empirical proviso (clean and dirty look like substitutes), and survives calibration, but it has not been demonstrated by running the on-then-off experiment directly. So I hold it with medium confidence: a well-grounded inference, not a settled fact.

Two practical implications survive even the cautious reading. First, timing matters: because a dirty lead compounds, the cost of redirecting innovation rises the longer you wait — delay is not neutral. Second, a carbon price alone is the expensive way to do it: pairing it with research support that directly steers inventors is, on the best available models, both cheaper and faster, because it works with path dependence instead of fighting it. Even if "permanent" turns out to be merely "very persistent," those two lessons hold.

What we don't know

  • The on-then-off experiment. No study removes a directional policy and tracks whether the new direction persists. A clean test — a credibly temporary clean-R&D subsidy, followed for decades — would move this from inference to evidence.
  • How substitutable clean and dirty really are, sector by sector. The whole result hinges on substitutability above one, and the estimates, while north of one, are far below what the most optimistic climate calibrations assume.
  • How long the memory lasts. Path-dependence elasticities are estimated over a few decades of patent data; whether a redirected trajectory holds for the half-centuries that climate models care about is an extrapolation.
  • Whether patents capture the right thing. Most of this evidence counts patents. Patents are a lumpy, partial proxy for useful invention, and the deployment of clean technology — not just its invention — is where emissions actually fall.
  • External validity beyond energy. The sharpest estimates are from autos and energy. Whether direction is equally steerable, and equally sticky, in software, biology, or materials is largely untested.

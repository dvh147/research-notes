Here is a number that launched a thousand op-eds. Jan De Loecker, Jan Eeckhout, and Gabriel Unger went through U.S. firm accounts back to 1955 and estimated how much, on average, companies charge above what it costs them to make one more unit - the markup. In 1980 a typical dollar of cost was sold for about $1.21. By the end of their sample it was about $1.61. Average profit rates over the same span rose from around 1% of sales to about 8%, and the rise was concentrated in a fattening tail of very-high-markup firms (De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger 2020). The finding lined up with a long list of worries - a shrinking share of income going to workers, sagging business dynamism, fewer startups - and helped put antitrust back on the front foot on both sides of the Atlantic.

It is now close to reflexive to read a high markup as a social problem: market power, a wedge between price and cost, rent extracted from consumers, deadweight loss. That reading is not wrong, and the welfare cost is real - one careful estimate finds the cost of markups to the economy is large (Edmond, Midrigan & Xu 2023).

This post is about something the reflex leaves out. Across firms, the ones with the highest markups are also the ones whose inventions are worth the most - and not only to themselves. Their patents earn more for them privately, and they also throw off larger benefits to other firms. If that is right, then some of what we file under "market power" is bound up with the production of socially valuable invention, and "markups are pure waste" is too blunt a summary.

I want to fix the guardrails before I start, because this is easy to over-read. The claim is a cross-firm association, not a recipe: it does not say that handing a firm a monopoly will make it invent brilliantly. And it is about the inventions high-markup firms do produce, not about whether a more concentrated economy innovates faster overall - a separate question where, as we will see, market power may well cut the other way. With those bounds in place, let me build it up.

Two things market power could do to innovation

Economists have argued about market power and innovation for eighty years, and the theory genuinely points both ways.

On one side is Joseph Schumpeter, who argued in 1942 that the prospect of monopoly profit is precisely what motivates the costly, risky work of innovating: a firm shielded from instant imitation can afford to invest and expects to keep the reward (Schumpeter, Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy, 1942). Call this the appropriability view - market power is both the prize and the piggy bank.

On the other side is Kenneth Arrow, who pointed out in 1962 that a secure monopolist has less to gain from a cost-cutting innovation than a competitive firm does, because it is partly cannibalizing profits it already collects - the "replacement effect." A comfortable incumbent may simply rest on its position (Arrow 1962).

The most famous attempt to settle it found that both forces operate at once. Philippe Aghion, Nick Bloom, Richard Blundell, Rachel Griffith, and Peter Howitt (2005) related competition to innovation across British industries and found an inverted U: innovation is highest at intermediate levels of competition and falls off when competition is either very weak or very fierce (Aghion et al. 2005). Where firms are neck-and-neck they innovate to "escape competition"; where a laggard is hopelessly behind a dominant leader it gives up. So neither "monopoly is good for innovation" nor "competition is good for innovation" is the whole truth - the relationship has a hump.

The inverted U is about how much firms innovate as a function of their market's competitiveness. The question here is a cousin of it, but not the same: holding the act of innovating fixed, whose inventions turn out to be valuable - to the firm and to everyone else? For that we have to look at firms, their markups, and the measured value of what they patent.

The firms with market power produce the more valuable inventions

Start with a result that is twenty-five years old and has aged well. Richard Blundell, Rachel Griffith, and John Van Reenen (1999) followed a panel of British manufacturing firms and asked how a firm's market share related to its innovation. Two findings stand out. First, firms with higher market share produced more innovations and patents. Second - and this is the part that matters here - the stock-market reward for a given innovation was larger for firms with higher market share (Blundell, Griffith & Van Reenen 1999). Dominant firms innovate partly to pre-empt rivals and defend their turf, and the market valued their innovations more highly. (Consistent with the inverted U, they also found that tougher competition at the industry level stimulated innovating. The two are not in conflict: one is about the firm's position, the other about the arena it sits in.)

The anchor for this post brings that picture up to date and adds the genuinely new piece. Ralf Martin, Jenniffer Solorzano Mosquera, Catherine Thomas, and Dennis Verhoeven (2025) assembled over 14,500 EU firms and about 2,400 US firms that were granted patents between 2005 and 2014. For each firm they estimated a markup - using the now-standard production-function method of De Loecker and Warzynski (2012), which backs out how far price sits above marginal cost from the firm's input choices. For each patent they then estimated two different values. The private value is what the invention is worth to its owner, proxied by stock-market value and the firm-value-to-citation link that Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg (2005) made standard (in their data an extra citation per patent is worth about 3% on firm value). The external value is what the invention is worth to other firms - the knowledge spillover - proxied by how much later inventors elsewhere build on it.

Their finding is the title of this post. Innovation by high-markup firms is more valuable privately - no surprise, given Schumpeter and Blundell and coauthors - and it creates more external value, bigger spillovers to other firms (Martin et al. 2025). The pattern holds when they control for the firm's existing stock of past innovation (so it is not merely that big innovators happen to have big markups) and when they value patents in several different ways. The high-markup firms, in other words, are not sitting on a pile of rents and coasting; the inventions they generate are worth more to the rest of the economy, not less.

How much should we believe each half? The private-value half rests on several studies pointing the same way, and I would call it fairly convincing. The external-value half - that market power tracks larger spillovers - is the novel and least-replicated piece. It rests substantially on this one careful, robustness-checked, but cross-sectional study. I read it as a suggestive, well-supported correlation that I would like to see reproduced in other data before I treat it as a law.

It helps that the result fits a broader portrait of who the high-markup firms actually are. When David Autor, David Dorn, Lawrence Katz, Christina Patterson, and John Van Reenen (2020) dug into rising concentration, they found it rose most in the industries where productivity was growing fastest, and that the firms pulling ahead - the "superstars" - combined high markups with high productivity (Autor et al. 2020). Whatever else they are, the high-markup firms are disproportionately the capable, productive ones - exactly the firms you would expect to do valuable R&D.

Why would markups and valuable invention travel together?

Three mechanisms, not mutually exclusive.

Appropriability. A firm that can hold price above cost captures more of the value of whatever it invents before imitators compete it away. A toy version makes the force visible. Imagine two firms that could each develop the same process, worth $100 to the world. The one with pricing power expects to keep, say, $30 of that; the one in a cut-throat market expects to keep $5, because rivals will copy the idea and compete the price down. The first firm will green-light far more such projects, and reach for more ambitious ones - so high-markup firms rationally aim higher. And the inventions that score highest on private value tend to be the foundational ones that others cite and build on, so larger private value and larger spillovers can rise together rather than trading off against each other.

Deep pockets. R&D is hard to fund with outside money: it is risky, its collateral is intangible, and the payoff is distant. Internal cash is the natural funding source, and markup income is internal cash. Hanna Hottenrott and Bettina Peters (2012), studying German firms, show that financing constraints bind hardest exactly on the firms with the most innovative ideas - those firms convert extra internal funds into more innovation, evidence that money, not just opportunity, is a binding constraint (Hottenrott & Peters 2012). A high markup loosens that constraint.

Selection. The most productive, best-managed firms tend to be good at many things at once: they have both the efficiency that earns a high markup and the capability to invent well. On this reading the markup does not cause the valuable innovation; both are downstream of being a good firm. This is the most important caveat to the whole exercise, and it is why the honest sentence is "high-markup firms invent more valuably," not "markups make firms invent more valuably."

Objection! Market power also kills innovation

Everything so far is about the inventions high-markup firms make. The strongest objection is that market power's main effect on innovation runs the other way - through the inventions that never get made because a dominant firm smothered them.

The sharpest evidence is Colleen Cunningham, Florian Ederer, and Song Ma's (2021) study of "killer acquisitions." Working through pharmaceutical project-level data, they show that incumbents sometimes buy promising startups in order to shut their projects down: the acquired drug is quietly discontinued when it would have competed with the buyer's existing product, especially when the buyer's market power is large. They estimate, conservatively, that 5.3% to 7.4% of acquisitions are killer acquisitions - very roughly one in every fourteen to nineteen deals - and that they cluster just below the size thresholds that trigger antitrust review (Cunningham, Ederer & Ma 2021). Here market power does not fund socially valuable invention - it extinguishes it.

Zoom out to the whole economy and a second body of work links rising market power to slowing innovation. Maarten De Ridder (2024) argues that the spread of intangible inputs - software, data, proprietary processes - lets leading firms charge high markups and raises the barrier to entry, so that after an initial boost, aggregate productivity growth and business dynamism fade (De Ridder 2024). Ernest Liu, Atif Mian, and Amir Sufi (2022) make a related argument tied to low interest rates: cheap capital lets market leaders pull decisively ahead of followers, which discourages the followers from even trying and drags down aggregate productivity growth (Liu, Mian & Sufi 2022). And Philippe Aghion, Antonin Bergeaud, and coauthors (2023) offer a model in which efficient high-markup firms spread across product lines - a temporary burst of growth - but eventually deter the rivals whose competition kept everyone innovating, so that growth slows and rents rise together (Aghion et al. 2023).

How do I square these with the anchor? I think they are about a different margin, and that both can be true at once. The firm-level result says: the patents a high-markup firm produces are, on average, more valuable to itself and to others. The aggregate results say: a market structure that entrenches high-markup incumbents can suppress entry - and the inventions entrants would have made - and can dull the leaders' own later incentives. The first is about the value of realized inventions, conditional on the firm. The second is about the rate and breadth of invention across the whole market. A monopolist can be a genuinely excellent inventor and still, by occupying the field, leave the economy with less invention than a more contested market would have produced. Nothing in the anchor licenses "more market power, more innovation overall," and I want to be explicit that I am not claiming it.

Bottom line

Let me commit, and then bound it.

The reflexive equation - high markup equals pure rent equals social waste - is too simple, and the firm-level evidence is why. The best reading of the data is that higher-markup firms produce innovation that is more valuable both to themselves and to others. Schumpeter's appropriability logic shows up not only in private profit but, more surprisingly, in larger spillovers, and it fits the broader finding that today's high-markup firms are the productive superstars rather than sleepy monopolists. A competition policy that treats every markup as deadweight, with no account of the innovation value sitting behind some of them, will sometimes get the welfare sum wrong.

Now the bounds, and they are heavy. First, this is an association, not a causal license: you cannot grant a firm market power and expect valuable invention to follow, and selection - good firms being good at everything - may explain much of the link. Second, it is about the inventions high-markup firms make, not the economy-wide rate of invention, where entry deterrence, killer acquisitions, and fading dynamism plausibly push the other way; the net sign for aggregate innovation is genuinely unsettled. Third, none of this cancels the consumer-side cost of markups, which is real and large.

So the conclusion is not "market power is good for innovation." It is narrower and, I think, more useful. The following is more my reading than a settled result, but: market power and the production of valuable invention are entangled at the firm level, which means the policy instrument has to be a scalpel - distinguishing rent that funds socially valuable R&D from rent that merely guards a moat - rather than a hammer that swings at every markup alike.

What we don't know

The honest gaps are large. First, causality. Nobody has a clean experiment that moves a single firm's markup and then watches the value of the inventions it goes on to produce. Until someone does, selection - capable firms being good at both pricing power and invention - is a live alternative to the appropriability story, and the size of the true causal channel is unknown.

Second, the net aggregate sign. Firm-level complementarity (high-markup firms make valuable inventions) and aggregate entry deterrence (their dominance smothers others' inventions) pull in opposite directions, and no one has convincingly added them up. The economy-wide effect of market power on the rate of useful innovation could be positive or negative; I don't think the current evidence pins down which.

Third, which markups are which. A useful competition policy would need to tell "innovation-funding" market power from "moat-guarding" market power - and we cannot yet measure the difference cleanly. That distinction is more or less the whole ballgame for antitrust, and it is wide open.

Fourth, what a markup even is. The production-function approach to measuring markups is contested; some argue the measured increases partly reflect rising fixed and overhead costs rather than pure market power, and at least one study finds that rising European concentration reflects greater efficiency rather than weaker competition, with markups roughly flat (Bighelli et al. 2023). And the anchor's evidence is patenting firms in the EU and US from 2005 to 2014; it says little about services, non-patenting innovation, or other periods.

In a set of garment factories in southern India, women who sew shirts and trousers for export were put through a training course. It did not teach them to sew faster, or to operate a new machine. It taught them how to speak up, manage their time, solve problems, communicate, and get along with the people around them -- the things we lump together as "soft skills." Eight months after the program ended, the workers who had taken it were about 13.5% more productive, and the firm had earned a net return of roughly 256% on what it spent. That is the headline result of Adhvaryu, Kala, and Nyshadham (2023), a randomized trial published in the Journal of Political Economy, and it is the kind of number that should make you sit up.

Here is why it is surprising. We tend to treat soft skills as fixed personality -- you either are a good communicator and a calm problem-solver, or you are not, and a workplace course is not going to change it. We also have a strong prior, backed by a lot of disappointing program evaluations, that job training mostly does not work very well. And basic economics says that if a worker becomes 13.5% more productive, her wage should rise to match -- yet in this study, wages did not budge.

So this post argues one narrow claim: short, low-cost training in soft skills can causally raise worker productivity, sometimes a lot, and the effect can spread to coworkers. I will be honest about two big caveats that the evidence forces on us -- it has to be the right kind of training, and the worker is usually not the one who keeps the gains -- but the core claim, I think, survives.

To get there I will march through four questions. Are soft skills even economically real? Can you actually teach them? Does the effect spread beyond the trained worker? And if the returns are this high, why on earth does the worker not get a raise -- and why isn't every firm doing this?

Are soft skills even real?

Start with the trait, before we get to training. Is there really an economically meaningful thing called "soft skills," separate from raw intelligence?

The evidence here is about as unambiguous as labor economics gets. The foundational paper is Heckman, Stixrud, and Urzua (2006), who build a statistical model that separates a person's cognitive ability from their "noncognitive" ability (think conscientiousness, self-control, locus of control) and ask how each predicts life outcomes. Their answer: noncognitive ability moves wages, schooling, employment, and a long list of behaviors -- crime, teen pregnancy, smoking -- by amounts comparable to cognitive ability. Cognitive test scores explain more of the variance in wages, but moving someone from the bottom to the top of the noncognitive distribution shifts outcomes about as much as doing the same on the cognitive side.

A complementary study sharpens where soft skills bite. Lindqvist and Vestman (2011), using data from the Swedish military enlistment (where a psychologist interviews every conscript and scores his noncognitive ability), find that men who fail in the labor market -- the unemployed, the very low earners -- are short on noncognitive, not cognitive, ability. Cognitive skill matters more for who climbs to the top; noncognitive skill matters more for who avoids the bottom.

And the market is paying more for these skills over time, not less. Deming (2017) documents that jobs requiring high social interaction grew by nearly 12 percentage points as a share of the U.S. labor force between 1980 and 2012, while math-intensive but less-social jobs -- including a lot of STEM work -- shrank by 3.3 points. His model and evidence make the case that social and cognitive skills are complements: the biggest winners are people who have both. As routine and even analytical tasks get automated and offshored, the part that is hard to ship out is the human coordination -- the "trading tasks" with coworkers that requires reading other people.

But all of that is correlational about the trait. The cleanest causal evidence that social skill is a real, transferable input comes from a lab experiment. Weidmann and Deming (2021) randomly shuffled people through a series of three-person problem-solving teams and watched whose presence made any team better. Such "team players" exist: being one standard deviation higher on their team-player measure raised group performance by about 0.13 standard deviations, roughly 60% as large as the effect of individual task skill. And who were they? Not the highest-IQ people. The team players scored well on a test of social intelligence ("Reading the Mind in the Eyes"). Social skill is distinct from smarts, it is measurable, and it causally lifts the output of the people around you.

So: soft skills are real, they are economically large, and the market increasingly rewards them. The question is whether you can put them into a person who is already grown up and on the job.

Can you teach them?

This is the heart of the claim, and here the best evidence comes from randomized experiments in three settings that could hardly be more different -- and they point the same way.

The seed paper is the strongest. The program in those Indian factories was Gap Inc.'s P.A.C.E. curriculum: about 80 hours of class, spread over roughly a year, half on company time, covering communication, problem-solving, time and stress management, execution, and basic financial and legal literacy. Adhvaryu and coauthors randomized it across production lines and then across workers within lines. The trained workers ended up about 13.5% more productive; they were more likely to be promoted; and the firm's net return came to roughly 256% within eight months. (A note on calibration: the published paper reports 13.5%, while an earlier working-paper version reported a smaller figure -- around 11% -- so treat 13.5% as the citable number and the precise magnitude as somewhat uncertain.)

One factory chain in one country is a single data point. So consider De Grip and Sauermann (2012), a randomized field experiment in the call center of a Dutch telecom. The treatment was a one-week course in communication and conversation techniques -- pure soft skills. Trained agents handled calls about 10% faster, with no loss of quality. Different country, different industry, much shorter course, same direction.

Now go to West Africa. Campos and coauthors (2017), writing in Science, ran a three-arm trial with 1,500 microenterprise owners in Lome, Togo. One group got conventional business training (accounting, marketing, finance). Another got "personal initiative" training -- a psychology-based course in being proactive, self-starting, and persistent, which is to say, a soft skill. Over two years, the personal-initiative group raised profits by about 30% (roughly $60 a month per firm); the traditional business-content group raised profits by about 11%, an effect too small to distinguish from zero. The soft content beat the hard content.

Here is the same evidence at a glance:

Study Setting What was taught Effect
Adhvaryu et al. (2023) Indian garment factories P.A.C.E.: communication, problem-solving, time mgmt (~80 hrs) +13.5% productivity; ~256% firm return
De Grip & Sauermann (2012) Dutch call center 1-week communication/conversation course +~10% performance
Campos et al. (2017) Togolese microenterprises Personal-initiative (proactive mindset) training +~30% profits (vs +11%, n.s., for business training)

There is a fourth strand worth folding in, because it generalizes the lesson from "how to behave" to "how to operate." A famous experiment by Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie, and Roberts (2013) gave free management consulting to large Indian textile firms and raised their productivity by about 17% in the first year, roughly halving quality defects. Management, it turns out, is a teachable skill, not just a trait of the founder. The honest caveat: this was intensive, expensive consulting (hundreds of consultant-hours per plant), not a cheap course, so it speaks to the idea that operational know-how is teachable more than to the cheapness of the intervention. But cheaper versions work too. Gosnell, List, and Metcalfe (2020) found that simply giving airline captains performance monitoring and fuel-saving targets significantly raised their productivity -- a nearly free intervention, on highly skilled professionals. And Iacovone, Maloney, and McKenzie (2022) showed that delivering management advice to small groups of Colombian firms, at about one-third the cost of one-on-one consulting, improved practices just as much and lifted sales and profits -- evidence that this can be scaled down in cost.

Put the strands together and the convergence is what persuades me. The best evidence suggests that teaching adults how to communicate, cooperate, manage themselves, and take initiative raises their measured output -- across factory floors, call centers, microenterprises, and cockpits. No single study would carry the claim; the agreement across wildly different settings is what gives it weight.

Does it spread?

A nice feature of soft skills is that, unlike a faster pair of hands, they are partly relational -- the benefit of one person communicating better shows up in the people they work with. The seed study saw exactly this: untreated coworkers on treated production lines also became more productive. The authors are candid that this spillover is clearer in direction than in precise size; I have not found a clean published point estimate for it, so I will not pretend to one.

For an actual number, return to the call center. De Grip and Sauermann estimate that raising the share of trained teammates by 10 percentage points lifted an untrained worker's performance by about 0.51%. That is real but modest -- train half a team and the untrained half speeds up by a couple of percent, free of charge.

Why would this happen at all? Because workplace productivity is contagious in general, trained or not. The canonical demonstration is Mas and Moretti (2009), who used scanner data on supermarket cashiers and found that a 10% increase in the average productivity of the coworkers on your shift raised your own productivity by about 1.5%. The mechanism is telling: the effect came only from coworkers who could see you (those positioned behind you), and it looked like social pressure -- you slack less when watched -- rather than learning by imitation.

That mechanism also predicts where spillovers will and will not be large, which matters for how far to push the claim. Cornelissen, Dustmann, and Schonberg (2017), looking across an entire local labor market rather than one firm, found that peer effects are small on average and in high-skill jobs, but sizable in low-skill, easily observed jobs -- about half the size of the effects estimated in the cashier-style studies. So spillovers are real, but concentrated where work is visible and interdependent: a garment line, a call center floor. In a building full of remote knowledge workers, expect less. (And one of the training studies, Campos in Togo, found no coworker spillover at all -- though there the "firm" was often a single owner with no coworkers to spill onto.)

My read: the spillover leg of the claim is suggestive rather than nailed down. The contagion mechanism is well established; the training-specific magnitudes are modest where we can measure them, and they probably shrink as work gets less observable. That is why the title says the gains "spread," not that they "double."

Objection! Most training doesn't work

If teaching soft skills pays this well, a skeptic should immediately object: economists have evaluated job-training programs for fifty years, and the modal finding is a shrug. Is this just publication bias dressed up?

It is a fair challenge, and the honest answer is that generic training really does disappoint a lot of the time. The most comprehensive evidence is the meta-analysis by Card, Kluve, and Weber (2018), which pools 857 program estimates from 207 studies. The average effect of active labor-market programs is essentially zero in the short run (about 0.04 standard deviations, not significant), rising to about 0.12 SD after two to three years and 0.19 SD in the longer run. Training, in other words, tends to pay off slowly if at all, and you would not have seen it in a quick follow-up. Similarly, McKenzie and Woodruff (2014) survey the business-training literature and conclude that such programs reliably change what owners do but often fail to move profits or survival -- and that many studies are too small to detect the effects they hope for. In one well-known trial, de Mel, McKenzie, and Woodruff (2014) found that training alone did nothing for existing female business owners' profits; what moved the needle was a cash grant, i.e., capital, not curriculum.

So is the soft-skills result a fluke? I do not think so -- but the counter-evidence does real work in scoping the claim. Three lessons fall out of it.

First, kind matters. The programs that disappoint are mostly generic business content and classroom job training; the standouts teach behavior, mindset, and how to work with people. That is precisely the contrast Campos drew within a single experiment, where initiative training beat business training head-to-head.

Second, timing matters. Card and coauthors show training effects emerge over years, not months. The garment study's eight-month horizon is, if anything, an argument that its effect is real and fast for this type of on-the-job program.

Third, targeting matters. The meta-analysis finds bigger gains for the disadvantaged and the long-term unemployed. The same heterogeneity shows up vividly in Resnjanskij, Ruhose, Wiederhold, Woessmann, and Wedel (2024): a mentoring program raised a combined index of outcomes for low-socioeconomic-status German adolescents by more than half a standard deviation, with effects on grades and labor-market orientation still visible three years later -- and did essentially nothing for higher-SES kids, who presumably already had that support at home.

Concede the objection, then: training in general is no silver bullet. But the claim was never about training in general. It was about a specific, behaviorally focused kind of training, well targeted, given a little time -- and on that narrower claim the skeptic's own evidence is consistent.

The 256% puzzle: why no raise, and why not everyone?

Now the objection that genuinely troubles me, because it is not about whether the effect is real but about whether it makes sense. If a worker becomes 13.5% more productive, basic competition says some employer should bid her wage up by 13.5%. Yet the garment study found no effect on wages at all. And if a firm can earn 256% in eight months, why is every factory on earth not running these courses? A 256% net return means the firm roughly tripled its money in two-thirds of a year. If a machine offered that, every plant would buy ten.

The starting point is Gary Becker's Human Capital (1964), the book that founded this whole field, which makes a sharp prediction. Skills come in two flavors: specific (useful only at this firm) and general (useful anywhere -- and communication and teamwork are about as general as it gets). In a perfectly competitive labor market, Becker argued, a firm will never pay for general training, because the newly-skilled worker can march down the street and sell those skills to a competitor. So the worker pays for general training -- by accepting lower wages while learning -- and the worker captures the return, as a raise, afterward. On this logic, wages should have risen with productivity. They didn't.

The resolution is that the labor market is not perfectly competitive, and the frictions change everything. Acemoglu and Pischke (1998, 1999) showed that when it is costly for workers to switch employers -- search frictions, a bit of monopsony (employers having some power to hold wages below productivity), wage scales that are compressed so that pay rises less than output -- general skills start to behave like specific ones. Competitors do not fully bid up the trained worker's wage, so the firm can pay for general training and keep much of the return. The prediction flips: productivity rises more than wages, and the firm becomes the residual claimant.

A toy version makes it concrete. Suppose training makes a worker produce $100 more per month. In Becker's frictionless world, rival firms bid for her until her wage rises by the full $100; the employer who paid for the course gets nothing, so no employer pays. Now add friction: switching jobs is a hassle, and the wage ladder is compressed, so rivals only bid her up by $40. Her wage rises $40; the firm keeps $60 -- and is now delighted to fund the course. That $60 wedge is the firm's incentive, and it is exactly why we see productivity up and wages flat.

This is not just theory. Konings and Vanormelingen (2015), using firm-level data, estimate that a trained worker's productivity premium is about 23% while the wage premium is about 12% -- productivity roughly twice wages. The firm pockets the difference. The garment factory is the same story with the dial turned up.

So the "no raise" is explained. But notice the explanation only deepens the second puzzle. If frictions let firms capture returns this large, why doesn't every firm chase them? Honestly, this is not fully resolved, and I would distrust anyone who claims it is. The likely suspects are mundane: managers do not know these returns exist or cannot measure them (productivity on a sewing line is far easier to track than in most workplaces); the returns are uncertain and lumpy; credit and attention are scarce, and a course that pays off over a year competes with a hundred other fires. The very fact that a Gap-style program had to be run as an experiment to reveal a 256% return tells you the information was not lying around. Under-provision of something this profitable is itself evidence of how far real firms sit from the textbook.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That soft skills are economically real and increasingly valuable is about as well established as anything in labor economics -- multiple methods, multiple datasets, all agreeing. That you can train them in working adults and raise measured productivity is, in my judgment, fairly convincing: three randomized trials in radically different settings move the same way, reinforced by the management-as-skill literature. I would not bet the house on any single magnitude -- a 256% return is a real estimate from a real RCT, but it is one firm, one sector, one country, and the largest such numbers should be held loosely. That the gains spread to coworkers is suggestive: the contagion mechanism is solid, but the training-specific spillovers we can measure are modest and probably shrink where work is less visible.

Two caveats are load-bearing, not decorative. Generic training often does little; the wins come from the right kind of training (behavioral, initiative-focused), well targeted (especially to the disadvantaged), and given time. And the worker is usually not the one who gets rich -- in a frictional labor market, productivity rises more than wages, and the firm keeps the wedge. "Soft skills, hard returns," yes -- but read the second word of the title as belonging, for now, mostly to the employer.

What we don't know

  • Durability. The flagship results are measured over months to about two years. Do the productivity gains persist, or do soft skills decay without reinforcement? The active-labor-market evidence says effects can grow over years; whether on-the-job soft-skill gains do the same is untested.
  • Which skills, exactly. "Soft skills" bundles communication, self-control, initiative, teamwork, time management. We do not know which ingredients are doing the work, or whether the mix has to be matched to the job.
  • How far it travels. Almost all the clean evidence is from settings where output is easy to see and tasks are interdependent -- factory lines, call centers. Whether the same courses move the needle for remote knowledge workers, where peer effects are small (Cornelissen et al.), is genuinely open.
  • Who should pay. If firms capture most of the return but under-invest anyway, there may be a role for policy (subsidies, portable training accounts). But if much of the gain is firm-specific rent, subsidizing it could just transfer money to employers. The incidence question is unsettled.
  • The honest replication test. What would change my mind: a well-powered, multi-firm, multi-sector replication of a P.A.C.E.-style program that tracked workers for five years and measured wages, productivity, and turnover. Until then, the claim is strong but provisional -- which, given how profitable it appears, is itself the most interesting fact in the file.

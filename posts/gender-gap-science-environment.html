A child who grew up in a top-1% American household was about ten times more likely to become an inventor than a child from a below-median-income family. The striking part, in Bell, Chetty, Jaravel, Petkova, and Van Reenen (2019), is that the gap barely shrinks when you compare children who scored the same in third-grade math. Whatever is sorting kids into invention, it is mostly not their measured ability to invent. The authors have a name for the people on the wrong side of it: lost Einsteins.

The number that should stop you is about gender. Girls grow up to patent in some narrow technology class at higher rates if, as children, they were surrounded by women — not men — inventing in that exact class. The exposure effect is that specific, and that gendered. Run it forward and it implies a counterfactual worth sitting with: if girls were exposed to female inventors at the rate boys are exposed to male inventors, the gender gap in innovation would close by about half.

That single result frames a much larger question, because where a gap comes from dictates what, if anything, fixes it. There are two stories about the persistent under-representation and lower measured output of women in science, and they point in opposite policy directions. In the first, the gap is mostly preferences and ability: women, on average, are less drawn to the winner-take-all tournament of a research career, or sort toward other fields, and the gap is roughly a revealed preference that policy can only wait out. In the second, the gap is mostly environment: it is manufactured by malleable features of women's surroundings — who they saw doing science as children, who they get to collaborate with, what childbirth does to a measured CV, and how a workplace counts output. If the first story is right, there is little to do. If the second is right, the gap is an engineering problem.

Here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence: a meaningful share of the gender gap in science is environmental, not essential — and the best-identified evidence sits on the environment side. I'll concede where the preference story has real bite, because it does, and I'll be explicit about which environmental channels are nailed down and which are merely suggestive.

One scoping note. "The gender gap in science" has two margins: entry (who becomes a scientist) and output (how much active scientists produce, or are measured as producing). I'll march the claim through four environmental channels that span both margins, then give the preference story its strongest hearing. The question that motivates all of this — whether a woman's local scientific environment, holding her talent fixed, changes how much she produces — is one I've been chasing in my own work; what follows is the published evidence around it, not that.

Lost Einsteins, mostly daughters

Start where the evidence is strongest: at the door. Whether a girl ever becomes a scientist turns out to be remarkably sensitive to her environment, in ways that are hard to reconcile with a pure story of fixed taste or talent.

The Bell et al. result is the anchor because of how it identifies environment. The gender- and technology-class specificity is the tell: girls move toward inventing in precisely the niches where they saw women invent, which is what you'd expect from a role-model or network mechanism and is very hard to explain through "girls are innately less interested in inventing" or "girls go to worse schools." A companion paper, Bell, Chetty, Jaravel, Petkova, and Van Reenen (2019) in JEEA, makes the policy edge sharp: childhood exposure does far more to expand the supply of inventors than financial incentives like top-tax-rate cuts, because you cannot tax-incentivize a career that was never on a child's horizon.

If that were one clever observational study, I'd hold it loosely. But the experimental and quasi-experimental evidence converges. Breda, Grenet, Monnet, and Van Effenterre (2023) ran a large field experiment in French high schools: a single one-hour classroom visit from a woman working in science measurably reduced students' gender stereotypes and made high-achieving twelfth-grade girls more likely to enroll in selective, male-dominated STEM programs. Porter and Serra (2020) exposed introductory-economics students to charismatic women who had majored in economics, and lifted women's probability of majoring in economics by 8 percentage points — a large effect against a 9% base rate. Three studies push exposure into settings where assignment is essentially random, killing the worry that motivated girls seek out role models: Canaan and Mouganie (2023) find that being randomly assigned a female science advisor raises women's STEM enrollment and graduation; Riise, Willage, and Willen (2022) find that girls exogenously assigned a female general practitioner sort into STEMM tracks, with the largest effects for high-ability girls with low-educated mothers; and Kofoed and McGovney (2019), using random mentor assignment at West Point, find a female cadet paired with a female mentor is 18 percentage points more likely to rank that mentor's branch among her top three.

This is the rare corner of social science where many designs, several of them randomized, point the same way. I read it as fairly convincing that environment causally moves who enters science.

But "fairly convincing that environment matters" is not "we know the lever." The honest counter-evidence keeps the claim from curdling into a slogan. Brenøe and Zölitz (2020) find that a higher share of female high-school peers actually reduces women's later STEM participation — exposure to women and exposure to role models are not the same thing, and crude "add more women" interventions can backfire. Hoffmann and Oreopoulos (2009) find same-gender-instructor effects on college outcomes that are real but small — at most 5% of a standard deviation. Even Mouganie and Wang (2020), who find that high-performing female math peers pull women toward science, are describing a particular kind of peer, not bodies in a room. So: the direction is well established and the best implementation is not. Environment is a lever; it is not a simple one.

It's who you get to work with

The gap doesn't close once a woman is inside science; it reopens in how productive she gets to be. And a scientist's productivity is not a solo trait — it runs through collaboration, which is itself gendered.

The descriptive picture is consistent. Kwiek and Roszka (2021), in a large-scale study of co-authorship, document strong gender homophily: men's co-authors are disproportionately men and women's disproportionately women, which in a field where men hold more of the senior, well-resourced positions tends to route women toward less-central collaborations. Araujo and Fontainha (2017) find the gender imbalance has a specific network shape, not just a headcount one. If the most productive collaborations are partly closed to you, some of your "lower productivity" is really lower access.

I want to be careful here, because this is where the environment story is most tempting and least nailed down. It would be easy to say "your surroundings determine your output" and reach for the famous natural experiment — and that experiment cuts the other way. Waldinger (2012) used the Nazi dismissal of scientists in 1933 to ask whether losing high-quality departmental colleagues hurt the productivity of the scientists who remained, and found essentially nothing: even very strong local peers did not measurably raise their neighbors' output. The lesson is not that environment doesn't matter; it's that generic proximity — who happens to be down the hall — is not the channel. What matters is the specific, chosen collaborative tie, the kind whose loss does lasting damage (the subject of an earlier post). For the gender gap, that reframes the network story: it is not "put women near productive people," it is "women have less access to the particular collaborative relationships that actually carry productivity." That is plausible, it fits the homophily evidence, and it is still more suggestive than proven. I hold this channel more loosely than the others.

The motherhood scoreboard

Now the channel everyone reaches for first — and which turns out to be real, but smaller and stranger than the folk story.

Gallen (2023) does something most of this literature can't: she measures actual productivity, estimating a production function on Danish matched employer-employee data rather than inferring output from pay. The measured gender productivity gap is about 8%, and it accounts for roughly two-thirds of the residual gender wage gap — so a real chunk of the pay gap is a genuine output gap, not pure discrimination. Motherhood sits at the center of it. But here is the twist that makes it an environment story rather than a preference one: for mothers, lower pay tracks lower measured productivity; for childless women, pay and productivity decouple precisely during their prime childbearing years. That pattern looks less like women choosing to produce less and more like workplaces pricing in an anticipated penalty.

And the reflex that women simply leave science for family doesn't survive contact with the data either. Jennifer Hunt (2016) finds that women's excess exit from technical careers is concentrated in engineering, not science, and that about 60% of that exit gap is explained by women leaving engineering over dissatisfaction with pay and promotion opportunities — with family-related reasons only secondary. The excess exits don't even mostly coincide with leaving the workforce. The finger points at the environment of those jobs — thin mentoring, weak networks, blocked ladders — rather than at a taste for staying home. So the motherhood penalty is genuine, but it is a constraint (childcare, tenure clocks, anticipatory discrimination), not a revealed preference, and "she left to have kids" is doing far less work than the stereotype assumes. I read the motherhood channel as good evidence for a real, environmental component — and good evidence against the simple version of the family story.

Same worker, different scoreboard

The subtlest channel is that even measured output is an artifact of the environment doing the measuring. Change the rules of the workplace and you change the size of the gender gap without changing a single scientist.

Stuart and Galperin (2025) study U.S. patent examiners — the same job, done under different incentive regimes — and find that stronger production incentives make men more lenient, tilting them toward quantity, while heightened monitoring makes women less lenient, tilting them toward quality. The gender gap in measured output is largest under strong incentives and weak monitoring. The "gap," in other words, is partly produced by how the organization counts, not by who the workers are. Hoisl and Mariani (2017) make the cleanest version of the point: female inventors are just 4.2% of inventors and earn about 14% less than men — but the gap is not associated with any difference in the quality of the inventions they produce. Women in this elite technical job are rewarded less for equally good science. That is a gap in the scoreboard, not in the work. And environment shapes even what gets studied: Truffa and Wong (2025) find that when 76 formerly all-male U.S. universities went coeducational, gender-related research output rose 44% — driven substantially by male incumbent researchers shifting their focus, prompted by interaction with more diverse students and peers. I read the workplace-rules channel as good: these are clean quasi-experiments, and they keep finding that surroundings move measured output.

Objection! It's just preferences

Here is the strongest case against everything above, and it deserves a fair hearing because it is real.

There is a robust, replicated finding that women, on average, are less drawn to competition. Niederle and Vesterlund (2007) showed in the lab that roughly twice as many men as women choose to enter a winner-take-all tournament, despite performing equally well. Buser, Niederle, and Oosterbeek (2014) showed this isn't a lab curiosity: measured competitiveness predicts which academic track students choose, and the gender gap in competitiveness explains a substantial share of the gap in those choices. Saccardo, Pietrasz, and Gneezy (2018), with a finer measure, find the tail is even more lopsided than the standard binary test suggests — only about one-fifth of the most competitive quartile are women, and the most competitive tenth are all men. Science is, among other things, a tournament. If women are less willing to enter tournaments, some of the gap is preference, full stop.

I concede that. But three things keep the environment claim standing.

First, the competitiveness gap may evaporate exactly where it would have to matter. Boudreau and Kaushik (2023), in a field experiment with 97,678 adults, find the familiar gender gap in willingness to compete among people outside STEM — and no statistical gender difference among those in STEM fields. By the time you are looking at scientists, the selection has already happened; the average gender difference in competitiveness is not a feature of the scientific workforce.

Second, competition aversion is not a fixed trait but a context-sensitive response. Flory, Leibbrandt, and List (2015), randomizing nearly 9,000 job-seekers across compensation schemes, confirm that women shy from competitive pay — but the gap shrinks sharply when the job is framed as teamwork, when it lacks overt gender associations, and for older applicants. Change the environment and the "preference" moves.

Third, and most fundamentally, preferences are partly downstream of environment, which means they are not a clean alternative to it. Olivetti, Patacchini, and Zenou (2020) show that a woman's labor-supply behavior is shaped by the working mothers she was exposed to as a teenager. Dahl, Kotsadam, and Rooth (2021) randomly assigned women into Norwegian military squads and found men's gender attitudes became more egalitarian — direct evidence that "tastes" are malleable (though, honestly, the effect faded once exposure ended). And Bell et al.'s exposure effects are, at bottom, a story about how environment forms the interests we later read off as preferences. "Preference" is not bedrock under "environment"; it is partly sediment laid down by it.

One more piece of honesty, in the other direction: not every environmental story checks out. Card, DellaVigna, Funk, and Iriberri (2020) examined refereeing at four leading economics journals and found referees to be essentially gender-neutral in their assessments. Whatever is producing the gap, hostile gatekeeping at the referee's desk is not its main engine. The environment story has to be argued channel by channel, not assumed.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. It is fairly convincing that environment causally moves who enters science: the exposure and role-model effects are replicated, several are randomized, and the gender- and niche-specificity of the Bell et al. result is very hard to explain any other way. It is good evidence that the measured output gap is shaped by the rules of the workplace and by the motherhood constraint: equal-quality inventions rewarded unequally, an 8% productivity gap that bends around childbirth and anticipatory penalties, gaps that grow and shrink with incentive design. It is, so far, only suggestive that the collaboration-network channel carries real weight, and I flag it as the soft spot. And I concede that preference differences are real at the entry margin — but they are context-sensitive, partly environmental in origin, and conspicuously absent among working scientists, which is where the productivity gap actually lives.

Put those together and the fixable share of the gender gap in science is not a rounding error. Exposure alone could halve the innovation gap, on Bell et al.'s arithmetic; stack the workplace-rules and motherhood-constraint channels on top and it is plausible that the majority of the gap is, in principle, an engineering problem rather than a fact of nature — though I'd put wide error bars on "majority," because nobody has cleanly added the channels up.

Let me bound that, because I don't want to oversell environment any more than I'd want to oversell biology. I am not claiming gender differences in ability or preference are zero — the competition evidence is real, and "environment explains everything" would be its own overreach. I am not claiming any single intervention works: role-model effects are heterogeneous and occasionally backfire, and the cleanest attitude-change effect we have fades when the exposure stops. The defensible claim is narrower and more useful than "it's all sexism" or "it's all choice": a meaningful, well-identified slice of the gap responds to changing the environment, and the reflexive "it's just innate" story is too simple to survive the evidence.

What we don't know

  • The decomposition. I can show each channel is real; I cannot tell you that it's 30% exposure, 25% workplace rules, 20% motherhood, and so on. Nobody has cleanly added them up, and they surely overlap.
  • Scaling and persistence. Most of the role-model evidence is small, well-identified experiments. Whether the effects survive at scale, and whether they stick rather than fade (as the integration effect did), is largely untested.
  • Measured versus real productivity. Some of the "productivity gap" may be a credit gap — women getting less recognition for the same work — rather than fewer ideas. That deserves its own post; it would push even more of the gap onto the environment side.
  • Which field, which margin. Hunt's result that engineering ≠ science is a warning: these channels likely have different weights across fields, and most of the cleanest evidence is from inventors and patents.
  • The seed question. Whether, holding a woman's ability fixed, her local scientific peer environment changes how much she produces — exactly the margin Waldinger found empty for generic colleagues — is where the cleanest evidence is still missing. That, not coincidentally, is where the open research is.

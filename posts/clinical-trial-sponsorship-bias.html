Through the 1990s and 2000s, drug companies ran a lot of head-to-head trials of antidepressants. A common matchup pitted venlafaxine — sold as Effexor — against fluoxetine, better known as Prozac. If you read only the trials paid for by Effexor's manufacturer, the verdict was lopsided: Effexor came out ahead in 12 of 14 of them. If you read the trials funded by someone without a stake in the outcome, the picture nearly inverted — Effexor beat Prozac in just 1 of 3.

Same two molecules. Similar patients, similar rating scales. Different checkbook, different answer.

That contrast is the heart of a 2024 Journal of Political Economy paper by Tamar Oostrom, "Funding of Clinical Trials and Reported Drug Efficacy", and it is the seed of the claim I want to defend here: when the very same drug is tested against the very same comparator, it is reported as substantially more effective in trials its maker paid for than in trials it didn't — and the distortion comes mostly from which results see daylight, not from faked data.

This matters because the stakes are not academic. Doctors choose prescriptions, insurers build formularies, and guideline committees rank therapies largely on the published trial record. If that record systematically flatters whoever funded it, then a lot of real decisions are being made on numbers with a thumb on the scale. The hopeful flip side — which I'll get to — is that a bias made of selective reporting is, in principle, a fixable information problem rather than a tale of villainy.

A natural experiment hiding in the filing cabinet

The hard part of this question is the obvious confound: maybe manufacturers simply sponsor trials of their good drugs. If Effexor's maker believes in Effexor, of course its trials look good — because the drug is good. To learn anything about bias, you need to hold the drug fixed and vary only the funding.

Oostrom's trick is to do exactly that. The same pair of drugs — say, Effexor versus Prozac — gets tested many times, by parties with different financial interests. So she compares a given drug's reported efficacy when its manufacturer ran the trial against its reported efficacy in trials of the same comparison run by someone else. The molecule, the rival, and the outcome scale are all held constant; the only thing that moves is who is paying. Whatever's left is what she calls the sponsorship effect.

Across 509 published double-blind randomized trials of antidepressants and antipsychotics, that effect is large: a drug is reported roughly 50% more effective (her central estimate is 49%) when its manufacturer sponsors the trial than in trials of the same comparison without manufacturer involvement. Put differently, take the measured advantage a sponsored drug shows over its comparator and about half of it evaporates once you strip out the sponsorship effect.

That the same molecule against the same rival can look half-again better depending on the funder is, I think, a result the direction of which is hard to argue with — and it lands harder because the design has already disarmed the "they just back better drugs" reply. The size, as we'll see, is a number to hold more loosely than the direction.

Bigger than one drug, bigger than one method

A single clever paper, even in a top journal, is one data point. What moves this from "interesting" to "well established" is that completely different methods, applied to completely different slices of medicine, keep finding the same thing.

The most direct corroboration is the meta-analysis that owns this question: the Cochrane review by Andreas Lundh and colleagues, "Industry sponsorship and research outcome" (2017), which pools a large body of drug and device studies. Industry-sponsored studies more often reported efficacy favorable to the sponsor (risk ratio 1.27, 95% CI 1.17–1.37) and more often reached favorable overall conclusions (RR 1.34, 1.19–1.51). Crucially — a point I'll lean on later — that gap "cannot be explained by standard 'Risk of bias' assessments." The earlier systematic review by Joel Lexchin and colleagues in the BMJ (2003) put the raw association even higher: industry-funded studies had outcomes favoring the sponsor at an odds ratio of 4.05 (2.98–5.51).

Those three numbers — a ~50% efficacy gap, a risk ratio of 1.3, an odds ratio of 4 — look wildly different, and the gap between them is itself instructive. An odds ratio on a common "favorable/not" outcome exaggerates; the carefully pooled risk ratio is the more honest summary; and Oostrom's 50% is specific to psychiatric drugs measured on subjective symptom scales, a setting where there's a lot of room for interpretation. So the direction is pretty unambiguous and the existence of a meaningful bias is well established; the magnitude is genuinely method- and field-dependent, and I'd resist quoting any one figure as "the" answer.

Is it fraud? Mostly, no — and that's the interesting part

Here's where the story gets subtler than "drug companies lie." The evidence points away from fabrication and even away from sloppy science, and toward something quieter: a curated record.

Start with quality. If industry trials were just badly run, we could dismiss the gap as noise. But the opposite is true. Lexchin found that industry-funded trials scored as well or better on methodological quality than independently funded ones; the BMJ authors concluded the bias "could not be explained by inferior research methodology," pointing instead to two things — the choice of comparator and publication bias. Lundh's Cochrane review likewise found the industry effect survives formal risk-of-bias adjustment, and that industry studies show more disagreement between their results and their conclusions — i.e., spin in the write-up rather than rot in the data.

Then comes the decisive clue, and it's beautifully simple. Oostrom asks: what happens to the sponsorship effect if I put some of the missing trials back? Adding just one unpublished trial per drug pairing shrinks the effect by about 20%. Scale that up and most of the sponsorship effect is explained not by what happens inside a trial but by which trials get published in the first place.

The canonical demonstration of that mechanism predates Oostrom by 16 years. Erick Turner and colleagues, in a 2008 New England Journal of Medicine paper, "Selective Publication of Antidepressant Trials and Its Influence on Apparent Efficacy", used the FDA's registry as a complete census of trials that were actually run — including the ones that vanished. Of 74 registered antidepressant trials, the FDA judged about half (51%) positive. But of the trials that reached the published literature, roughly 94% read as positive. Negative trials were either never published or, remarkably, published as though they were positive. The consequence: drug-by-drug, the published effect sizes were inflated 11% to 69%, and 32% for the class as a whole.

A toy version makes the logic vivid. Imagine a drug that is, in truth, exactly as good as its rival — a real tie. A company runs ten honest trials. By chance, about five tilt toward its drug and about five toward the rival or to no difference. Publish the five favorable ones, leave the other five in the drawer, and a doctor reading the journals sees a 5–0 sweep. No data were faked. Every published trial is real. And yet the literature now says "clear winner" about a drug that merely tied. That is the file drawer, and a sponsor with a lot of trials and a lot at stake has both more shots and more reason to be selective about which ones it talks about.

The selection isn't only about whether to publish. Jennifer Kao's 2025 Management Science study, "Information Disclosure and Competitive Dynamics", shows firms time their candor strategically: when a competitor's drug gets approved, a firm becomes 13% less likely to report its own trial's results. And Rossella Salandra's 2018 Research Policy paper on selective reporting in trials localizes the lever nicely: the odds of selective reporting are higher for industry-funded studies, but specifically when at least one author is industry-affiliated — consistent with the bias living in human reporting choices rather than in the money as such.

It isn't just medicine

If selective reporting were a uniquely pharmaceutical sin, we might hope it stays contained. It isn't, and that's both the bad news and a reason to trust the pharma findings more.

Take economics — a field with no drug companies, just the ordinary human wish to publish a "result." John Ioannidis, Tom Stanley, and Hristos Doucouliagos surveyed 159 economics literatures (over 64,000 estimates) in a 2017 Economic Journal paper bluntly titled "The Power of Bias in Economics Research". Their findings could have been written about the file drawer above: median statistical power around 18%, and among the adequately powered results, roughly 80% of reported effects are exaggerated — typically by a factor of two, a third of them by four or more. Isaiah Andrews and Maximilian Kasy showed in 2019 that you can actually identify how publication probability rises with statistical significance and correct estimates for it. Abel Brodeur and coauthors document the tell-tale bunching of test statistics just past the significance threshold, and Alwyn Young, re-running 53 experimental economics papers with randomization inference, found 13–22% fewer statistically significant individual treatment effects — and a third to a half fewer when the effects in a table are tested jointly.

Why does the file drawer fill itself? Because the reward system pays for positive results. Felix Chopra and colleagues, in "The Null Result Penalty" (2023), had PhD students and journal editors rate studies and found that null results are judged less publishable, less important, and even less precisely estimated — when, by construction, the precision was identical. Daniele Fanelli has documented both halves of the ratchet: negative results have been steadily disappearing from the literature — the share of papers reporting positive support for their hypothesis rose about 22% between 1990 and 2007 — and, separately, that positive results collect roughly 32% more citations in applied fields like clinical medicine. Sponsorship bias, in this light, isn't a pharmaceutical aberration; it's the general physics of incentivized research, with a profit motive bolted on top. That convergence — medicine and economics leaving the same fingerprints — is exactly why I read the drug-trial result as real rather than as an artifact of one dataset.

Objection! Maybe sponsors just pick winnable fights

The strongest pushback deserves a hearing in its strongest form. There are two versions.

Version one: sponsors back better drugs. True in general, and fatal to a naïve comparison — but not to Oostrom's. Her within-pair design compares the same drug against the same comparator; the drug's underlying quality is differenced out. This objection is answered by the research design, not by faith.

Version two: sponsors rig the matchup — choosing a weak comparator, an unflattering dose of the rival, or an endpoint that suits them. This one has real teeth, and Lexchin explicitly named comparator choice as a channel. But notice what the same-pair design does to it: when Effexor-vs-Prozac is the fixed matchup, the comparator is held constant across the sponsored and unsponsored trials, so a rigged-comparator story can't explain why the same matchup flips with the funder. Dosing and endpoint-selection can still operate within a matchup, which is part of why I treat the mechanism split — pure non-publication versus within-trial choices — as not fully pinned down. What the evidence rules out is the comforting story that this is all just sponsors choosing their spots.

It's worth adding a calibrating non-result here. Colleen Carey, Ethan Lieber, and Sarah Miller, studying drug-firm payments to physicians (2021), found that while payments shift prescribing, the underlying quality of the drugs being promoted was largely unaffected by whether payments flowed. Money bends behavior and presentation; it doesn't turn a bad drug into a good one. The bias we're chasing is about the reported picture of efficacy, not a secret truth that the drugs don't work.

The fix, and how far it goes

If the disease is a curated record, the cure is to make the record complete: register trials before they run, pre-commit to a primary outcome, and require results to be posted whether they flatter or embarrass. Does that work? The best evidence says yes — strikingly — but only as far as it's enforced.

The cleanest illustration comes from Robert Kaplan and Veronica Irvin, who looked at large NHLBI-funded cardiovascular trials (2015). Before 2000, when the NIH began requiring its big trials to register and declare a primary outcome in advance, 17 of 30 (57%) reported a positive result. After 2000: 2 of 25 (8%). The authors judged registration the dominant driver. Reading that as causal requires some faith — it's a small sample of public trials and the world changed in other ways too — but a drop from 57% to 8% is hard to explain entirely by anything else, and it lines up with the mechanism.

Oostrom's own data echo it: the sponsorship effect declined after 2005, when journals began requiring pre-registration as a condition of publication. And in economics, Zohid Askarov and colleagues find that journal data-sharing mandates measurably reduced reported statistical significance — the footprint you'd expect if mandatory transparency curbs selective reporting. There's even a clean theoretical reason it should help: Emeric Henry's model of strategic disclosure shows that when a biased sender can't selectively withhold, he ends up disclosing everything — which is precisely what a binding registry forces.

Now the sobering qualifier, and the reason the title doesn't promise a cure. Mandates only bite when enforced, and enforcement is leaky: Oostrom notes that only about a quarter of pre-registered trials actually report results. And not every transparency tool works. Abel Brodeur and coauthors find that data-sharing policies alone don't reduce p-hacking in economics, and Costanza Naguib finds that the journals' 2016 removal of significance stars had no detectable effect. Speculatively — and here I'm reasoning past the cleanest evidence — I read this as saying registration helps because it changes what exists in the record, while cosmetic reforms that don't change the underlying file drawer mostly don't. The fix that works is the one that makes the missing trials visible.

Bottom line

Discount efficacy claims that come from the seller's own trials. The thumb on the scale is real, it's been found by within-drug experiments, FDA-registry audits, and meta-analyses alike, and in the best-studied corner of medicine it's worth something like half of a sponsored drug's apparent edge over its rivals. On the direction and existence of the bias, the evidence is about as unambiguous as observational evidence gets.

But hold three qualifiers in the same hand. First, the size is field-specific — a ~50% gap in psychiatry, with its subjective endpoints, need not survive in oncology, where the outcome is survival and harder to spin. Second, the bias is mostly a reporting phenomenon, not fraud or bad methodology, which is why it has been so easy to miss and so tempting to deny. Third, and most encouraging, it's the kind of problem we know how to shrink: pre-registration and result-reporting mandates demonstrably work, and they're already working — just unevenly, and only to the extent anyone makes them stick.

The unsettling part is what the bias does to a careful reader. A curated literature is invisible from the inside: every individual trial can be honest, well-powered, and correctly analyzed, and the aggregate can still mislead, because the selection happened in the spaces between the papers. You cannot read your way out of it one study at a time. You need the registry.

What we don't know

  • How far the ~50% generalizes. Psychiatric symptom scales are unusually elastic. The honest expectation is that bias shrinks where endpoints are objective (death, relapse, lab values), but I haven't seen a clean same-pair estimate outside psychiatry to confirm it.
  • The mechanism split. How much is outright non-publication versus outcome-switching versus dose-and-endpoint choices within a matchup? Oostrom shows publication bias is the largest piece; the rest is not cleanly partitioned.
  • Whether the newest mandates close the gap or just move it. Registration plus reporting requirements have tightened over the past two decades. Do they eliminate the sponsorship effect, or does selection migrate to subtler margins — sub-group emphasis, framing, the choice of which approved trial to feature in marketing?
  • The welfare cost. If apparent efficacy is inflated by a third at the class level, how much over-prescribing, mis-ranking on formularies, and wasted spending follows? That number — the price of the curated record — is the one a health economist most wants and the one I can't yet cite.

In his 1991 book Geography and Trade, Paul Krugman threw down a challenge to anyone who wanted to claim that ideas leak out of the firms and cities that produce them. Knowledge spillovers, he wrote, are real enough — but they are also "invisible; they leave no paper trail by which they may be measured and tracked." If you can't measure something, you can't really do economics with it. So as a practical matter, Krugman thought, the geography of knowledge was off-limits.

Two years later, three economists found the paper trail. Adam Jaffe, Manuel Trajtenberg, and Rebecca Henderson realized that patents come with citations to earlier patents — the "prior art" an invention builds on — and that each patent records where its inventors live. A citation is a little arrow pointing from a new idea back to an old one, and you can measure how far that arrow travels. Their 1993 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics asked a simple question: when a patent builds on an earlier patent, is the new one disproportionately likely to come from the same place as the old one?

The answer was yes, and it launched thirty years of argument. This post is about what survived that argument. The short version: knowledge spillovers really are local. Ideas build on nearby ideas more than distance and chance would predict; the effect runs largely through people physically moving around; and it has weakened only slowly, even as email, video calls, and open-access archives have made it trivial to read what someone three time zones away is doing. The first estimates were too big — that part of the critique stuck — but the core finding has been re-confirmed by so many different methods that it is now hard to dismiss.

Why care? Because if ideas are local, then where invention happens decides who captures its benefits. A breakthrough in a city seeds more breakthroughs in that same city, and the gains compound where the idea was born. That is the economic case for clustering, for "build the next Silicon Valley" industrial policy, and it is one of the engines behind the yawning gap between a handful of superstar innovation hubs and everywhere else.

The paper trail, and the trick that makes it work

You cannot prove spillovers are local just by noticing that a Boston patent cites another Boston patent. Maybe everyone in that narrow technology already works in Boston, so citations would cluster there even if knowledge flowed instantly and freely across the planet. Geographic concentration of people is not the same as geographic concentration of ideas.

Jaffe and his coauthors solved this with a control group. Here's the trick, in miniature. Take a cited patent from Boston and the later patent that cites it — say that citing patent is also from Boston. Now find a "control" patent: same technology class, same year, but with no citation link to the Boston original. Where is the control from? If it's from San Jose, that's one point for localization — the real follow-on stayed local while a random same-vintage, same-field patent did not. Do this thousands of times. If the genuine citing patents match the original's location more often than the controls do, then something beyond mere industry clustering is keeping ideas close to home.

When they ran it, the localization was clear and it was strongest at the local level. In the authors' own summary, about 6% of citations came from the same metropolitan area as the cited patent, against roughly 1% you'd expect from the geographic spread of inventors alone — a citation was something like six times more likely to be local than chance would predict. The effect was strongest in the first few years after a patent issued and, in their words, "fades over time, but only very slowly."

Two things to hold onto, because the whole later debate turns on them. First, six-to-one is a big relative effect but a small absolute one: even with the localization, the overwhelming majority of citations came from somewhere else. Jaffe and coauthors were careful to call the magnitude "rather modest." Localized does not mean trapped. Second, the entire result rests on the quality of that control group — on the claim that the control patent really is in the same line of work as the original. Hold that thought.

Objection! It's a measurement artifact

For a decade, the most interesting work on localized spillovers was aimed at killing the finding. There were two distinct attacks, and both landed real blows.

The trail isn't the inventor's. A patent's citations look like a record of what the inventors read. Often they aren't. Patent examiners — the government officials who review applications — add citations of their own to document prior art the applicant didn't mention. Juan Alcácer and Michelle Gittelman exploited a 2001 reporting change that, for the first time, flagged which citations came from examiners. The result was startling: in their 2006 study in the Review of Economics and Statistics, two-thirds of the citations on a typical patent were inserted by examiners, and 40% of patents had every citation added by an examiner. If most of the "paper trail" is written by a bureaucrat in Virginia rather than by the inventor, then citations may not measure what the inventor actually learned from at all.

The control group was too coarse. This was the deeper attack, and it goes straight at the trick from the last section. Peter Thompson and Melanie Fox-Kean argued that the technology classes Jaffe and coauthors used to pick controls were too broad — lumping together inventions that aren't really in the same narrow field. If your control patent isn't truly a substitute for the real citing patent, then your "localization" is partly just industry clustering sneaking back in through a loose control. In their 2005 reassessment in the American Economic Review, they rematched controls at a much finer level of technological detail. When they did, the localization at the state and metropolitan level largely disappeared; only the national-border effect survived. Their reading: most of what looked like local knowledge spillovers was an artifact of coarse controls.

Jaffe, Trajtenberg, and Henderson replied in the same issue, arguing the fine matching threw away most of the data and leaned on too few, oddly chosen controls. But the honest takeaway from this round is that the critics were partly right. The original numbers were inflated; some of JTH's "localization" really was industry geography in disguise. If the story ended here, you'd conclude that spillovers are at most weakly local, and mostly a national-scale phenomenon.

The story does not end here.

The localization survives a better test

The way out of a measurement fight is a better measurement. Over the following decade, researchers attacked the problem from angles the original critique couldn't touch — and localization kept reappearing.

The cleanest rebuttal came from Thompson himself — the same Thompson who co-wrote the critique. If examiner citations are the noise, why not compare inventor-added and examiner-added citations on the very same patents? That within-patent comparison automatically controls for everything about the patent and its field; the only thing that varies is who wrote the citation. In his 2006 follow-up, he found that inventor citations were about 20% more likely than examiner citations to come from the same country, and about 25% more likely to come from the same state or metro area, as the citing patent. Localization lives exactly where a real knowledge flow should leave its trace — in the citations the inventors put there themselves — and not in the examiner's boilerplate. That is close to a smoking gun, and notice which way the contamination cuts: pooling in all those less-local examiner citations had been diluting the measured effect, meaning true inventor localization is, if anything, stronger than the raw numbers suggested.

Then came better controls. Yasusada Murata and coauthors built a distance-based test in 2014 that uses inventors' precise coordinates and estimates a "localization distance" for each technology class, rather than relying on coarse same-place/different-place bins. Revisiting the exact JTH-versus-Thompson-Fox-Kean dispute, they found solid evidence of localization for the majority of technology classes even with finely grained controls — the localization would only vanish if the matching biases the critics worried about were implausibly enormous. Sam Arts and coauthors came at the control problem differently in 2018, measuring whether two patents are really in the same field by text-matching their actual language instead of trusting the classification system — and the localization replicated. Most recently, a 2025 paper showed that citations buried in the body of a patent (more likely written by inventors) display even stronger geographic proximity than the front-page citations everyone had been studying. Every time someone built a cleaner version of the inventor's paper trail, the ideas got more local, not less.

I read this literature as fairly decisive on the narrow question: the localization of patent citations is real, even if the 1993 magnitudes were too high.

Forget citations — watch the people

Still, patent citations are a strange and imperfect proxy for "an idea moved from one head to another." A skeptic could reasonably worry that the whole result is an artifact of how patents cite, not of how knowledge flows. The most convincing evidence on localization, to me, comes from designs that throw out citations entirely.

Start with a genuinely clever one. Sometimes two inventors independently come up with the same invention at nearly the same moment, and the patent office has to hold a proceeding — an "interference" — to decide who got there first. Two people inventing the same thing at once almost by definition drank from the same well of knowledge, and no citation is involved. Ina Ganguli, Jeffrey Lin, and Nicholas Reynolds built a database of these collisions and asked where the rival inventors lived. In their 2020 paper in AEJ: Applied — titled, with a nod to Krugman, "The Paper Trail of Knowledge Spillovers" — they find that inventors locked in an interference are 1.4 to 4.0 times more likely to live in the same local area than matched control pairs, and are more geographically concentrated than citation-linked inventors. A completely independent paper trail points the same way, and suggests the citation-based studies, if anything, understate how local knowledge really is.

Next, a design that gets at cause and effect. If proximity matters because people meet, then anything that makes it easier for inventors in two cities to meet should raise the flow of ideas between them. John Bai, Wang Jin, and Sifan Zhou used the introduction of new airline routes as a shock to travel time between US city pairs. Their 2024 estimates imply that cutting travel time between two cities by 20% raises the knowledge flowing between them by about 0.5% — over 15,000 additional citations in the aggregate. The increase shows up mostly across firm boundaries, is strongest for far-apart cities and complex, new technologies, and — tellingly — operates by moving people: the channel they identify is inventors physically traveling and carrying tacit knowledge that doesn't fit on a page.

And for a sense of scale, Kyle Myers and Lauren Lanahan estimated R&D spillovers from US Department of Energy grants without using any paper trail at all, leaning instead on quirks in state matching policies. They find about three spillover patents for every patent a grant recipient produces — and roughly 60% of those spillovers stay inside the United States. Spillovers are large, and they are bounded by geography even at the scale of a continent.

Why would ideas stay put when information is free? Because the binding constraint was never the information — it's the tacit know-how that rides around inside people's heads, and people don't move far or often. Pankaj Almeida and Bruce Kogut showed this back in 1999: tracking the careers of major semiconductor patent-holders, they found that localized knowledge transfer is driven by the inter-firm mobility of engineers, and is strong precisely in regions like Silicon Valley where job-hopping is common. When an engineer changes employers, her knowledge changes employers with her — but only as far as she's willing to commute. Jasjit Singh and Ajay Agrawal sharpened and bounded this in 2011: hiring an inventor raises a firm's use of her prior inventions by a striking 219%, but about half of that is simply the recruit continuing to mine her own ideas, and what spills to her new colleagues stays largely within her immediate working group. Mobility moves knowledge, but the spillover to everyone else is narrow — and local.

It's borders, not just miles

If the mechanism is people, then the barriers to spillovers should look like the barriers to people — and they do. Some of the most revealing recent work finds that pure distance is not the whole story; political and institutional borders matter on top of it.

Jasjit Singh and Matt Marx disentangled the geographies — metro area, state, country, and raw distance all at once — in 2013. They find that state and national borders independently dampen knowledge flows beyond what distance alone explains. A citation is less likely to cross a state line even between two points that are physically close. Intriguingly, the state-border barrier has weakened over time while the national-border barrier has strengthened — a hint that within-country frictions are slowly eroding even as the world fails to flatten the way the "death of distance" crowd predicted.

And these borders are partly policy. Sharon Belenzon and Mark Schankerman showed that citations to university research fall off sharply with distance and stop at state lines — and that the state-line effect is bigger where non-compete laws are strong and labor mobility is low, confirmed by tracking a non-compete reform in Michigan. That is a clean illustration of the whole mechanism: localization is large where the law makes it hard for inventors to switch employers and carry their knowledge across town, and smaller where workers move freely. Spillovers are local in part because we make them local.

For whom, and for how long?

So far this reads as a one-sided win for localization. It isn't, quite, and the honest qualifications matter.

"Local" is an average, not a universal. Paola Giuri and Myriam Mariani, studying thousands of European inventions, found that highly educated inventors — those with university or doctoral degrees — draw on geographically wider spillovers, reaching past the barriers that constrain everyone else. Education buys range. So does belonging to a far-flung professional network: William Kerr documented that knowledge diffuses internationally along ethnic scientific communities, and Paolo Zacchia showed that spillovers travel through co-authorship networks of scientists regardless of where the nodes sit. For an elite, networked, mobile inventor, geography binds much less.

And the localization may be slowly fading. It was already decaying within the original data ("only very slowly," but decaying), the state-border effect is shrinking, and one could read the long arc of cheaper travel and communication as gradually loosening the grip of place. I'd put this at suggestive rather than established — the striking fact is how little distance has died, not how much. US invention has if anything grown more spatially concentrated since the 1970s, with a handful of tech hubs pulling away, as William Kerr and Brian Chattergoon document. Local spillovers are part of what makes a hub a hub: they are a centripetal force, and the centrifuge is still spinning.

Bottom line

The evidence that knowledge spillovers are geographically localized is, by the standards of this kind of question, pretty convincing. It has been found in inventor citations cleaned of examiner noise, in distance-based tests with fine controls, in text-matched controls, in the body-text of patents, in simultaneous independent inventions that involve no citation at all, and in the causal response of idea-flows to new airline routes. Five or six methods that fail in different ways agree. The original 1993 magnitudes were too big — that critique was right and it stuck — but the existence of the effect is no longer the live question.

I'd commit to this much: a new idea makes its biggest mark on the place it was born, the effect works mainly through inventors carrying tacit know-how as they move between nearby employers, and it is reinforced by state lines, national borders, and the labor laws that govern who can move where. That is enough to give agglomeration and place-based innovation policy a real mechanism to work with, and enough to make the geography of invention a genuine driver of regional inequality.

But bound it honestly. The effect is large in proportion and modest in level — most citations, most spillovers, most ideas still cross the city limits. It binds the average inventor more than the elite, networked one. And it is probably weakening, even if far more slowly than the prophets of the frictionless digital world expected. Ideas don't travel as far as you'd think. They travel further than they used to.

What we don't know

  • How much is tacit knowledge versus labor-market pull? "People carry ideas" and "good inventors get hired into clusters" predict similar geographic patterns. The airline-route and interference designs push toward a genuine knowledge-transfer story, but cleanly separating the transfer of ideas from the sorting of talent is still hard.
  • Will remote and AI tools finally kill distance? Every previous communication revolution was supposed to, and didn't. The pandemic's natural experiment in remote work, and the arrival of AI research assistants, are a fresh test — but the early signal is more concentration, not less.
  • Does any of this generalize beyond US patents? Most of the cleanest evidence is American and patent-based. Patents are a slice of innovation, citations an imperfect proxy (they capture codified knowledge and miss the private, contract-based kind), and other countries have different labor laws and geographies.
  • How much can policy actually move? If localization is partly built from non-compete law and licensing frictions, then loosening those rules should spread ideas wider — but whether that raises total invention or merely redistributes it from the hubs is an open, and politically loaded, question.

2026-06-09 · 14 min read · innovation economics economic geography

America's inventions come from astonishingly few places. Among the people who hold patents in computer science, the ten largest metropolitan clusters are home to 69 percent of them; in semiconductors it is 77 percent; in biology and chemistry, 59 percent. Zoom in further and it gets more extreme: within the entire San Francisco Bay Area, about three-quarters of all patents come from the Silicon Valley core alone. And the bunching is rising, not falling — US invention has concentrated into a shrinking handful of hubs since the 1970s, a pattern William Kerr and Brian Chattergoon trace in detail for the United States and Michael Fritsch and Michael Wyrwich examine across countries — even though the cost of talking to someone on the other side of the country has collapsed to roughly zero.

Here is the question that concentration forces on us. Does packing inventors together actually make them better — would the same person invent more in San Jose than in St. Louis — or does the talent simply sort itself into the same few zip codes, so the map of invention just records where the good people already went? The distinction looks academic. It is the whole ballgame for policy.

Because if clusters cause productivity, then geography is a lever. Agglomeration has an innovation rationale, "build the next Silicon Valley" becomes a coherent goal, and the spatial concentration of invention becomes a genuine engine of regional inequality. If it is mostly sorting, that entire policy edifice mostly evaporates.

My read of the evidence — and I will show you exactly where it is strong and where it just cracked — is this: clustering probably does make inventors more productive, agglomeration economies are real, but the single cleanest proof of it at the inventor level is now contested, so you should hold the size of the effect loosely. What is not in doubt is the flip side. The same forces that concentrate invention also bid up the cost of being there and concentrate the rewards. Clusters raise output and spatial inequality together.

I will march through five things: how concentrated invention really is; the headline causal claim and the coding error that just dented it; the case for agglomeration that does not depend on that one paper; the price of density; and whether a government can build a cluster on purpose.

Invention has a few capitals

The intuition is old. In 1890 Alfred Marshall gave three reasons firms in the same trade huddle together: a shared pool of specialized labor, a thick web of suppliers, and knowledge that leaks between neighbors. His phrase for the third has never been bettered — in a cluster, he wrote, "the mysteries of the trade become no mysteries; but are as it were in the air." For invention, that third force does most of the work.

The modern evidence that the air is local is strong. Adam Jaffe, Manuel Trajtenberg, and Rebecca Henderson showed in 1993 that patents disproportionately cite nearby patents — knowledge leaves a paper trail, and the trail is short. (I dug into that literature in a companion post, so I will not relitigate it here.) David Audretsch and Maryann Feldman found that innovation is even more geographically concentrated than production is, and most concentrated precisely where new knowledge matters most. And Gerald Carlino, Satyajit Chatterjee, and Robert Hunt documented that doubling a place's employment density goes with about 20 percent more patents per capita.

The clusters are also remarkably tight. William Kerr and Scott Duke Kominers argued that the spatial reach of an agglomerative force sets the size and shape of the cluster it builds: the knowledge-citation premium loses half its strength within fifteen to twenty miles, and high-tech fields — computers, semiconductors — form the densest, most localized clusters of all. Buzard and coauthors found that private R&D labs bunch together far more than manufacturing does, with concentration peaking at about a quarter-mile and again around forty miles. Whatever proximity is doing for inventors, it does it at the scale of a neighborhood and a metro, not a nation.

So invention concentrates, the concentration is intensifying, and the leading explanation is that proximity makes inventors more productive. That last clause is where the fight is.

Does the cluster make the inventor, or vice versa?

To separate causation from sorting you have to watch the same inventor in different places — otherwise you cannot tell whether the cluster lifted her output or simply collected her.

Enrico Moretti's 2021 paper in the American Economic Review set out to do exactly that. Tracking 109,846 US inventors over time, he asked what happens to an individual's output when she moves to a city with a bigger cluster of inventors in her own field. The headline: bigger clusters go with more patents and more-cited patents. A cluster 10 percent larger was associated with about 0.67 percent more patents — an elasticity of roughly 0.067 — so moving from a median-sized cluster to one at the 75th percentile lifted a computer-science inventor's output by around 12 percent, with citations (a rough proxy for quality) rising even faster. To argue this was causal rather than sorting, Moretti leaned on a mover event-study and an instrument built from where an inventor's multi-city employer happened to expand elsewhere.

Scale that logic up and the implication is dramatic. Because a positive cluster-size effect is an increasing return, concentrating inventors raises the total: Moretti estimated that if you spread inventors evenly across cities, total US patent output would fall by roughly 11 percent.

It is worth seeing why concentration can raise the total, because the same fact drives the inequality later. Suppose, purely to see the mechanism, that an inventor working alone produces one patent a year, and that every doubling of her local same-field cluster makes each inventor 10 percent more productive. Put four inventors in one city and each sits two doublings above the lone benchmark — about 1.21 patents each, roughly 4.84 together. Split them two-and-two across two cities and each gets only one doubling — about 1.1 patents each, 4.4 total. Same four people, same talent; the concentrated arrangement produces about 10 percent more invention, purely from being together. That convexity is the engine behind the "spreading them out costs ~11 percent" estimate.

But all of that rests on the causal elasticity being real. And that is exactly what just came under fire.

Objection! A missing sort command

In 2026 the AER published a Comment by Michael Wiebe that goes at both pillars of Moretti's causal claim.

First, the event study. Wiebe argues the original specification "doesn't actually use variation from moving" — it is not a clean before-and-after-a-move comparison. When he re-runs it as a standard mover event study, the effect vanishes.

Second, the instrument. Wiebe found that, because of a coding error, the data were not sorted by city before the instrument was constructed, so the instrument was built incorrectly. Fix the sort, and the instrumental-variables estimate collapses to a null. He further documents that the cleaning code is not reproducible — it generates a slightly different dataset each time it runs — and that the estimation sample does not match the paper's own description of it.

Let me concede what is true, because it is a lot: this is peer-reviewed, it lands on both identification strategies at once, and there is no published Moretti rebuttal of the coding points. I take it as decisive against treating Moretti's specific causal numbers — the 0.067, the +12 percent, the ~11 percent aggregate — as established facts. They are best read now as illustrative, not authoritative.

But notice precisely what is and is not in dispute. The correlation is not challenged: bigger clusters really do house more productive inventors. Wiebe's claim is narrower and sharper — that the correlation "may not be causal." So the question snaps back to where it began: is it the cluster or the sorting? And to answer it we need evidence that does not run through Moretti's code.

Fortunately, there is a great deal of it.

The case from everywhere else

Step back from inventors to workers and firms, and the causal evidence for agglomeration economies is some of the most carefully identified work in economics.

Start with the sorting objection met head-on. Pierre-Philippe Combes, Gilles Duranton, Laurent Gobillon, Diego Puga, and Sébastien Roux asked, using French firm data, whether denser places are more productive because they make firms productive (agglomeration) or because only strong firms survive there (selection). Their answer is unusually clean: selection explains essentially none of it. Firms in denser areas are about 9.7 percent more productive on average — and, tellingly, the gain is largest for already-strong firms (about +14.4 percent at the top of the distribution versus +4.8 percent at the bottom). The implied agglomeration elasticity, around 0.03, sits squarely inside the literature's usual 0.02–0.10 band. It is firms and density rather than inventors and clusters, but it is the cleanest refutation we have of the "it's just sorting" story.

Then there is learning. Jorge de la Roca and Diego Puga followed Spanish workers and found that about half the big-city earnings premium is dynamic: experience accumulated in a big city is worth more, it compounds year over year, and — the decisive part — workers keep it when they leave. Someone who spends five years in Madrid and then decamps to a smaller city retains roughly 14.5 percent higher earnings. The thing that looks like "able people sorting into cities" is largely the city growing their ability. And abler workers gain the most from big-city experience, which means more increasing returns — and more inequality.

Closest to a real experiment is the work of Michael Greenstone, Richard Hornbeck, and Enrico Moretti on large plant openings. When a big new plant picks a county, the runner-up county — revealed in a trade magazine that names the also-rans — is a credible counterfactual for what the winner would have looked like. Five years after a "Million Dollar Plant" opens, the productivity of incumbent plants in the winning county is about 12 percent higher than in the losing county — roughly $430 million a year in extra output — and the spillover is biggest among firms that share workers and technologies with the newcomer. Co-location causes productivity, and it flows through exactly Marshall's channels: labor pooling and knowledge.

It is worth being honest that part of this "cluster" effect is really about specific people, not ambient air. Paolo Zacchia showed that when a superstar inventor relocates, the benefit accrues to their direct collaborators rather than to unrelated inventors across the same city — spillovers travel along networks, not through the atmosphere. Pierre Azoulay and coauthors found the mirror image: when a superstar scientist dies unexpectedly, their collaborators' output falls and does not fully recover. (This is why I would resist the most romantic "knowledge in the air" version of the story; a lot of what looks like agglomeration is the value of particular working relationships that happen to be nearby — a theme of my post on losing a key collaborator.) Consistent with the channel being real and local, Antonin Bergeaud and colleagues find sizeable R&D spillovers running from public labs to the private firms physically near them.

Net it out. Agglomeration economies are real — fairly convincing, across countries, designs, and outcomes. What the Wiebe Comment costs us is the clean inventor-specific magnitude, not the existence of the effect. So I would phrase the headline carefully: clustering very likely raises inventor productivity; the honest confidence level is "the best read of convergent evidence," and the true size is genuinely uncertain.

The price of density

Now the other half of the claim — which, conveniently, stands on firmer ground than the first. If a place is more productive, who captures the surplus?

Spatial-equilibrium logic, laid out by Edward Glaeser and Joshua Gottlieb in their survey, says: mostly not the people who move there. Because workers can choose where to live, a city's productivity advantage gets bid into higher wages and especially higher housing costs until the marginal worker is roughly indifferent between the booming hub and everywhere else. The urban productivity premium is real — income rises something like 3 to 6 percent per doubling of city size — but it is not a free lunch. A good chunk of it is a transfer into land.

Whether a productivity boom shows up as more people or just higher prices depends on how much housing a place will build. Albert Saiz showed that the most productive coastal hubs — hemmed in by water, hills, and regulation — have the most inelastic housing supply, so their gains capitalize into prices rather than population. That is precisely why an inventor in the Bay Area earns more and keeps less.

And the distribution is the sting in the tail. Rebecca Diamond showed that as college-educated workers sorted into expensive, high-amenity, high-productivity cities between 1980 and 2000, the gap in well-being between college and non-college workers rose by about 30 percent more than the wage gap alone implies. The skilled did not just earn more; they bought into better cities, with better schools and streets and restaurants, while the less-skilled were priced out of those same places. Conventional wage statistics, in other words, understate how far the geography of opportunity has diverged.

Put the two halves together and you can see the shape of the whole claim. The increasing returns that (probably) make clusters productive are the same convexity that concentrates invention spatially; and the spatial equilibrium then concentrates the rewards — higher incomes and land values for those inside, higher walls for everyone outside. Output and inequality are two readings of one equilibrium.

So should we build the next Silicon Valley?

If clusters are productive, the policy temptation is obvious: engineer one. Can a government actually do it?

The best long-run evidence comes from Patrick Kline and Enrico Moretti, who followed the Tennessee Valley Authority — a vast 1930s federal push of dams, electricity, and infrastructure — across a full century. The good news for cluster-builders: it worked, locally and durably. Manufacturing employment in the Valley kept growing even after the federal money stopped, the fingerprint of genuine agglomeration economies — the cluster became self-sustaining. The sobering news is the national accounting. The Valley's gains came substantially at the expense of other regions; in general equilibrium they roughly cancel out, so the program mostly relocated economic activity rather than adding to the national total. The real national return came from the useful infrastructure itself, not from agglomeration magic.

That is the deep tension this whole literature leaves us with. Concentration may well maximize aggregate invention — that is the convexity argument — but a national government trying to seed a brand-new cluster is mostly redistributing invention across the map, not manufacturing more of it. And redistribution toward a hub widens the very inequality that makes clusters politically combustible in the first place. "Build the next Silicon Valley" can be sensible regional policy and close to a wash as national innovation policy.

Bottom line

Here is what I would commit to. Invention is extraordinarily, and increasingly, concentrated — that is simply a fact. The same forces concentrate the rewards: productive places bid their advantage into wages and land, the skilled sort in, the rest are priced out, and measured by well-being the geographic divide is even wider than the wage data suggest. On the price-and-inequality half of the claim, the evidence is fairly convincing.

On the productivity half, I would say probably true, size unknown. The broad agglomeration literature — French firms, Spanish workers, American plant openings, a century of the Tennessee Valley — makes a convincing case that proximity causes productivity. But the cleanest inventor-specific estimate, Moretti's, just lost a peer-reviewed fight over a coding error, so its headline numbers should travel as plausible illustrations, not established facts. If you want it in one sentence: clusters almost certainly help inventors somewhat, we no longer have a trustworthy estimate of how much, and we should stay honest that part of the "cluster" effect is really the value of specific colleagues who happen to sit nearby.

The policy reading is humbling in both directions. Agglomeration is real enough that place genuinely matters for innovation — but close enough to zero-sum across regions that you cannot subsidize your way to more national invention merely by building hubs.

What we don't know

  • How big is the inventor-level effect, really? With Moretti's estimates contested, we lack a clean causal magnitude for inventors specifically. A re-analysis that survives the Wiebe critique, or a fresh natural experiment, would be worth a great deal.
  • Cluster, or colleagues? How much of the premium is ambient "knowledge in the air" versus the value of specific collaborative ties that merely cluster geographically? The network evidence (Zacchia; Azoulay and coauthors) hints the latter matters more than the romantic story implies.
  • Does density bid up costs for inventors in particular? The price-side evidence is mostly from the broader urban literature. We have less direct measurement of how much cluster costs claw back inventors' own gains.
  • Will remote work and AI finally loosen it? Concentration kept rising right through the internet era. Remote work and AI research tools are a fresh test — but the early signal is more concentration, not less.
  • Can policy ever beat the zero-sum trap? Is there a design — targeting genuine market failures rather than relocating activity — that raises total invention instead of just moving it around the map? That is the open, and politically loaded, question.

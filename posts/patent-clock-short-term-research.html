Here is a number that should bother you. Between roughly 1973 and 2011, drug companies and researchers ran almost 12,000 clinical trials aimed at metastatic cancers — the late-stage diseases where, even today, only about one patient in ten is alive five years later. Over the same period, trials aimed at preventing cancer in the first place numbered fewer than 500. We pour effort into treatments for the people closest to death and almost none into stopping the disease before it starts.

You could tell a comfortable story about why. Prevention is hard to study; the science is younger; maybe the opportunities just aren't there. But Eric Budish, Benjamin Roin, and Heidi Williams, in a 2015 paper called "Do Firms Underinvest in Long-Term Research?", point at a culprit hiding in plain sight: the patent system's clock. A patent lasts twenty years from the day you file it — not from the day your product reaches the market. A cancer treatment you can test in a three-year trial gets close to its full twenty years of protected sales. A prevention therapy that needs a thirteen-year trial to prove it works gets whatever is left. The clock is the same length for everyone, so the longer your research takes, the more of your monopoly it quietly eats.

That mechanism is the subject of this post, and it points to a claim broader than cancer: the fixed patent term steers private research toward projects with short development times and away from long-horizon work. Patents don't just turn the amount of innovation up or down. They bend its direction — and one direction they bend it is toward the short run.

The bargain, and a weaker spur than you'd think

The patent bargain is the default innovation policy of the rich world: invent something, disclose how it works, and in return get a temporary monopoly to recoup your costs. The implicit promise is that more or stronger patents mean more innovation. It is worth pausing on how shaky that promise turns out to be.

The deepest evidence comes from economic history. Petra Moser's "How Do Patent Laws Influence Innovation?" assembles roughly 15,000 inventions displayed at nineteenth-century world's fairs, in countries that had patent systems and countries (like the Netherlands and Switzerland) that didn't. The first surprise is how little of the inventing ran through patents at all: at London's 1851 Crystal Palace, only about 11% of British and 15% of American exhibited innovations were patented. The bulk of invention happened outside the system, protected by secrecy and lead time. The second surprise is what patents changed. Countries without patent laws didn't invent less; they invented differently, concentrating in industries where secrecy worked well (food processing, scientific instruments). Patents, Moser concludes, shaped the direction of technical change more than its quantity — a theme she develops across a body of historical work.

Modern reforms tell a similar story. When Japan broadened patent scope in 1988, Sakakibara and Branstetter (2001) examined 307 firms and found no increase in R&D or innovative output you could attribute to the reform. Looking across 60 countries and 177 policy changes over 150 years, Josh Lerner (2002) found that strengthening patent protection had, at best, few positive effects on domestic innovation. Michele Boldrin and David Levine, in "The Case against Patents", put the strong version bluntly: "there is no empirical evidence that they serve to increase innovation and productivity."

I don't fully buy the strong version, and honesty requires saying why. Patents plainly matter in industries where copying is cheap — pharmaceuticals and chemicals above all, where without exclusivity a generic competitor can reverse-engineer a billion-dollar molecule for pennies. And some reforms do move the needle: a recent study of a Chinese policy that lengthened patents by fast-tracking applications found a 30% rise in patent filings. So the fair reading isn't "patents never spur invention." It's narrower and more useful: "more IP equals more innovation" is not a general law. The spur is real in some places, weak or absent in others. Which raises the question this post is really about. If patents aren't a reliable throttle on the amount of innovation, what are they doing? A large part of the answer is that they steer.

The clock

Start with the mechanism, because it's almost embarrassingly simple once you see it.

Imagine two drugs. Both are filed for a patent the day the research program begins; both, once approved, would earn the same profit per year; both enjoy the standard twenty-year term. Drug A is a treatment for an aggressive late-stage cancer: because patients, sadly, reach the survival endpoint quickly, the trial takes 3 years. Drug B is a preventive therapy: to prove it stops the disease, you must follow healthy people for 13 years.

When each drug finally reaches the market, how much of its patent is left? Drug A has burned 3 of its 20 years and sells under patent for about 17. Drug B has burned 13 and sells under patent for about 7. Same eventual product, same yearly profit — but B's protected revenue window is less than half of A's. Add the fact that money later is worth less than money now, and the gap widens further: those distant year-13-onward profits are discounted hard. A company maximizing returns will tilt toward Drug A, even if Drug B's value to society — a disease prevented rather than treated — is larger.

This isn't a quirk of the model; it's visible in the data on real drugs. The twenty-year term is a sticker price, not what firms actually get. By the time a new medicine clears development and FDA review, the average effective patent life is only about 11.5 years (Grabowski and Vernon's estimate) — and the longer a drug takes to develop, the shorter that window shrinks. The patent clock doesn't reward the most valuable research. It rewards the fastest-to-market research.

The missing prevention trials

Budish, Roin, and Williams turn that mechanism into a test. Cancer is the ideal laboratory because development time is pinned to something observable: how long patients survive. For a cancer where five-year survival is low, you learn whether your drug works within a few years. For a cancer where survival is high — or for prevention — you wait far longer. So the theory makes a sharp prediction: R&D should fall as patient survival time (and thus trial length) rises. That is exactly the wrong way around from what we'd want, since long-survival and prevention settings are where lives are most savable.

The pattern is stark. Line the trial counts up by stage: more than 17,000 trials for recurrent cancers, about 12,000 for metastatic disease (10% five-year survival), just over 6,000 for localized cancers (70% survival), and fewer than 500 for prevention. Put more precisely, they estimate that a ten-percentage-point increase in five-year survival is associated with an 8.7% decrease in R&D investment. More survivable cancers — where the research payoff to society is arguably highest — get systematically less private research.

The obvious worry is that this is just correlation: maybe long-horizon cancers are harder science, and the patent clock has nothing to do with it. Here the paper delivers its cleanest move. For some cancers, regulators accept a surrogate endpoint — a measurable proxy like tumor shrinkage — instead of waiting to count deaths. A surrogate endpoint shortens the trial without changing the underlying biology. If trial length is what's driving the distortion, R&D should jump precisely where surrogate endpoints are available. It does: hematologic cancers, which can use such endpoints, attract about 112% more trials than otherwise-similar non-hematologic cancers — and the perverse "more survival, less research" slope flips to positive for them. The distortion tracks the clock, not the science.

What is the distortion worth? The authors put a number on it that is hard to forget. For US patients diagnosed with cancer in a single year (2003), the research discouraged by long commercialization lags translates into roughly 890,000 lost life-years — and valuing a life-year at $100,000, on the order of $89 billion. Treat that as an order-of-magnitude estimate built on assumptions, not a precise figure; the value-per-life-year and the counterfactual both carry weight. But even discounted heavily, it says the clock isn't a rounding error. It is one of the most expensive side effects in innovation policy.

We built a patch, which tells you the bug is real

Here's a tell that the clock problem is genuine: we already legislated a workaround, and then admitted it wasn't enough.

In 1984, the US Hatch-Waxman Act created patent term restoration — extra patent life to compensate for time lost to clinical trials and FDA review. The very existence of the provision is Congress conceding the core point: long development eats the monopoly, and that discourages the research we want. But the patch is capped — it restores at most five years, and the effective patent life still lands around twelve. A bandage sized for moderate delays, on a wound that gets worse the longer the research takes. The institution we built to fix the clock is itself shaped like the clock.

A bigger pattern: patents steer more than they spur

Step back, and the short-run distortion is one instance of a broader fact the evidence keeps returning: patents bend the direction of research, not just its volume.

Consider follow-on innovation — the work that builds on a patented invention. In a beautiful natural experiment, Galasso and Schankerman (2015) use the random assignment of judges at the US Federal Circuit to ask what happens when a patent is struck down in court. Invalidation raises later citations to the focal patent by about 50% — patents were holding follow-on work back. But — and this is the calibration that matters — the blocking shows up in computers, electronics, and medical instruments, and not in drugs, chemicals, or mechanical technologies, and it's driven almost entirely by large patentees blocking small follow-on firms. Patents distort cumulative innovation, but selectively.

The same selectivity appears in genomics, and it's worth dwelling on because it cuts against a tidy anti-patent story. Heidi Williams (2013) found that the genes held under the private firm Celera's contractual intellectual property saw 20–30% less follow-on research and product development than genes sequenced into the public domain. A clear harm. Yet Sampat and Williams (2019), studying actual gene patents (not Celera's contracts) and carefully correcting for the fact that more valuable genes get patented in the first place, found no quantitatively important average effect on follow-on innovation. Freilich and Shahshahani (2023) help reconcile the two: 87% of follow-on research doesn't actually infringe the patent, and the chilling effect concentrates in patents that are far from expiring. The honest summary is that follow-on harm is real but heterogeneous and often modest — strong where one big owner can hold up many small builders, weak or absent elsewhere (including, notably, in drugs).

Two more data points round out the picture, from opposite ends of history. Forcing patents open releases invention: when the US seized German-owned patents after World War I and licensed them out, Moser and Voena (2012) estimate domestic invention in the affected chemical fields rose by about 20%. And lowering access costs broadens what gets explored: when NIH agreements made certain genetically engineered research mice cheaper to use, Murray and coauthors (2016) found more researchers entered and pursued a more diverse set of questions — with no drop in the number of new mice created. Strong IP, they conclude, carries "a neglected cost": less exploration. Even the theory points the same way. Galasso, Mitchell, and Virag (2018) show that patent races reward speed of discovery over quality — first-past-the-post, not best. Everywhere you look, patents are doing more steering than the textbook spur-the-amount story admits.

Objection! Isn't this just short-termism?

The strongest pushback is that I've mislabeled an ordinary disease. Firms are myopic; capital markets punish managers for distant payoffs; of course long-horizon research is underfunded. The patent clock might be a bystander.

This is exactly the objection Budish and coauthors anticipate, and their design answers it. Generic short-termism would discourage any slow-paying project. The patent clock makes a more specific prediction: the distortion should depend on how long the trial runs, because that's what eats the monopoly — and it should vanish when a surrogate endpoint shortens the trial even though the science (and any financial impatience) is unchanged. The surrogate-endpoint reversal is precisely that test, and it comes out for the clock. I'd put it this way on the confidence ladder: that patents steer the direction of innovation is, across Moser, Budish et al., Galasso–Schankerman, and Moser–Voena, well established; that the fixed clock specifically bends research toward the short run is fairly convincing — one clean causal design plus corroborating theory, an institutional tell, and the effective-patent-life facts — though it leans heavily on a single arena, cancer, and deserves replication elsewhere. Financial short-termism is real too; the patent channel sits on top of it, not instead of it.

Objection! So scrap patents?

No — and this is where the strong anti-patent case overreaches. In pharmaceuticals, patents are doing essential work: without the prospect of exclusivity, even the fast drugs might never be developed, because a generic copy would undercut the innovator the day after approval. A claim that the clock distorts which research gets done is not a claim that patents should be abolished. It's an argument for fixing the clock.

And the fixes are not exotic. Budish and coauthors name three. Lean harder on surrogate endpoints, so that long diseases can be studied in short trials. Use targeted R&D subsidies for long-horizon work — and we have good evidence that R&D tax incentives buy real innovation. Or redesign the patent itself — most directly, start the clock at approval rather than filing, so development time stops cannibalizing the monopoly. Economists have also worked out cleaner reward schemes for sequential innovation, from prize-and-buyout menus to dynamic patent buyouts. One honest caution: starting the clock at approval lengthens the monopoly, which means higher prices and later generic entry — it trades one distortion for another. There's no free fix, only better-targeted ones. And we should keep the patent system's other virtues in view: patents disclose how inventions work and grease markets for technology, benefits a pure subsidy or prize wouldn't deliver.

Bottom line

Patents are a real but blunt and time-biased instrument. They are not the reliable innovation throttle the standard story imagines — strengthening them doesn't dependably raise how much gets invented — and they quietly tax the research that takes longest to pay off. The cleanest, most actionable version of that tax is the clock: a fixed term running from filing means long-horizon projects keep a shorter slice of their monopoly, and firms respond by doing less of them. The evidence for the broad steering claim is strong; the evidence for the specific clock mechanism is good but concentrated in cancer; and the right response is to fix the clock, not to junk the bargain.

What I'd hold onto is the reframe. We tend to debate patents as a dial — stronger or weaker, more or less. The more interesting truth is that the patent system is a compass: it doesn't just set how much we invent, it tilts what we choose to invent toward whatever pays off before the clock runs out. Sometimes that's a cancer prevented twenty years too late to be worth a company's while.

What we don't know

  • How big is the clock distortion outside pharma? Cancer is the cleanest case precisely because development time is measurable. In software or clean energy, the "trial length" is fuzzier, and the magnitude is genuinely unknown.
  • Would starting the clock at approval help on net? It cures the distortion but lengthens monopolies. Whether patients come out ahead depends on price effects no one has cleanly estimated.
  • What's the right mix of push and pull? Subsidies, prizes, advance commitments, and patent redesign all target the problem differently, and we lack head-to-head evidence on which works best for which kind of long-horizon research.
  • Are the general-equilibrium effects negative? Boldrin and Levine argue strong patents can reduce aggregate innovation even where they help any single inventor. That economy-wide question is much harder to settle than the targeted distortions, and remains open.

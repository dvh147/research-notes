"Be original." "Think different." "Disrupt." The advice we give inventors, scientists, and founders is relentlessly pro-novelty, and it comes with an implied promise: novel work is better work. Combine ideas no one has combined before and you'll produce something more useful, more cited, more valuable.

It's a lovely theory. The trouble is that when economists go and measure it — across millions of patents, over decades — the promise mostly doesn't show up. Novel inventions are not, on average, more impactful than conventional ones. If anything they're a touch less so.

What novelty changes is not the average. It's the spread. Reach for an unusual combination of ideas and you widen the range of what can happen: a home run becomes more likely and so does a strikeout. So "be novel" is not advice to raise your expected payoff. It's advice to buy a riskier lottery ticket — perfectly sensible for the right person in the right situation, but a risk choice rather than a free lunch, and treating it as a free lunch leads funders, managers, and inventors astray.

That's the claim. It holds up well for patents, less well than I first thought for science, and in one field somebody measured it properly and found the opposite. Let me build it up, then take it apart.

Invention is recombination — and recombination is a search in the dark

Start with where the variance comes from. The dominant model of invention, going back to Schumpeter, treats a new invention as a recombination of existing components: the automobile is an engine plus a carriage; CRISPR gene editing borrows machinery bacteria already use. On this view, inventing is search — wandering a vast landscape of possible combinations looking for ones that work.

The intuition follows. Recombine components you know well, in ways already tried, and you can predict the result: fine, incremental, unsurprising. Reach for components you've never used, or a pairing nobody has attempted, and you genuinely don't know what you'll get. You might find a new peak. You might fall off a cliff. Unfamiliarity doesn't bias the result up or down so much as it makes the result unpredictable.

Lee Fleming put numbers on this in a foundational 2001 study in Management Science, "Recombinant uncertainty in technological search". His method is the reason the paper matters. Take every US patent granted in two randomly chosen months — May and June 1990, 17,264 of them — and treat the patent office's subclasses as the "components" an invention is assembled from. Then fit a count model of forward citations in which the covariates are allowed to move not only the predicted number of citations but the dispersion parameter governing how far actual citations scatter around that prediction. Before Fleming, that scatter was a statistical nuisance to be absorbed. He made it an outcome. As he puts it, "we should think of varying variance as an opportunity instead of a nuisance."

The finding: patents built from combinations of components that had never been combined before are less cited on average, and at the same time more variable. Novelty, in his words, "implies an increase in the variability that can result in both failure and breakthrough."

Two things about what that design can show. It compares patents from a single two-month cohort with controls and no natural experiment — Fleming is explicit that he "cannot convincingly reject the alternative argument that the results merely reflect the normal life-cycle progression." And a dispersion parameter is blind to asymmetry: it says outcomes scatter more widely, not whether the extra spread went into the breakthrough tail, the failure tail, or both. The step from "more variable" to "more likely to break through" is an interpretation, and later work had to go and check it.

A toy example makes the shape concrete. Say a conventional invention almost always earns between 8 and 12 citations, averaging 10, while a novel one averages 9 — slightly lower — but one in twenty earns 150 and one in three earns nothing. Make a hundred of each: the conventional hundred cluster around 10, no disasters and no moonshots; the novel hundred hold more zeros and more triple-digit hits. Compare only the averages and you conclude novelty is mildly bad — missing that every breakthrough and every flameout is in the novel pile.

Novelty fattens the tails, and thins the middle

Fleming's result has been extended to the whole patent population with better measures of novelty, most directly by Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016) in Research Policy. They build "ex ante" novelty flags — computable from the patent document at filing, before you know how it did — for 5.3 million patent families filed at the European, US and international offices between 1980 and 2011. A patent is novel in recombination if it joins two technology classes never previously joined, and novel in knowledge origins if it cites a technology class or a scientific field its own class had never cited before. Crucially, they validate the flags outside the patent system, against R&D 100 award winners and against applications the European Patent Office refused specifically for lack of novelty — the closest thing to a falsification test this literature contains. For the impact analysis they cut to families filed up to 2003, so every patent has a full seven-year citation window, and run Fleming's mean-and-dispersion model on them.

The result needs care, because it is the most misquotable finding in this literature. Unconditionally, novel patents get more citations, not fewer. The negative sign appears only once you control for how many combinations a patent makes and its technology field — and then novelty in recombination lowers expected citations and raises dispersion, as does novelty in technological origins. Two wrinkles cut against the simple story. Novelty in scientific origins pushes the mean up. And the rare patents novel on all three dimensions at once — 0.3% of the population — are the only group getting both a higher mean and a higher dispersion. They escape the trade-off rather than embodying it, and carry more than double the odds of landing as a five-standard-deviation citation outlier, the kind of runaway hit that defines a technological generation.

Picture the two impact distributions side by side. What a dispersion result implies — and this is an illustration of the implication, not a histogram anyone plotted — is that novelty doesn't slide the conventional curve to the right; it stretches it outward in both directions and hollows out the middle:

Does the same pattern show up in science?

If this were just a quirk of patents, I'd be less confident. The natural place to look is scientific papers — and this is where I have had to walk my confidence back.

Wang, Veugelers & Stephan (2017), again in Research Policy, flag a paper as novel if it makes a first-time-ever pairing of two journals in its reference list, weighted by how distant those journals' citation neighbourhoods already were. They apply it to every Web of Science research article published in 2001 that cites at least two indexed journals — 661,643 papers — and follow them for fifteen years. Novelty does not raise a paper's average citations, but it raises the dispersion: citation dispersion is about 18% higher for the top 1% most novel papers, and both tails thicken. Relative to the middle 80%, the highly novel have about 18% higher odds of landing in the top 10% of citations and about 15% higher odds of landing in the bottom 10%. Over fifteen years they have roughly 57% higher odds of becoming a top-1% "big hit."

Two further findings matter. The upside is delayed: highly novel papers are less likely to be top-cited at windows under three years and only pull ahead from about year four. And they appear in journals with roughly 17% lower impact factor — conditional on field, reference count, authorship and international collaboration. I said in the first version of this post that this held "quality constant." It does not: the authors bring in no quality proxy at all and concede they "cannot completely rule out the possible link between novelty and quality." Unconditionally the relationship even reverses — novel papers appear in higher-impact-factor journals, and only the controls flip the sign.

There's a complementary and rather beautiful result from Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013), who analyzed 17.9 million papers. Their trick is a null model: shuffle every citation link in the Web of Science at random, holding each paper's reference count and each journal's citation total fixed, then ask how much more often two journals actually appear together than the shuffled network predicts. Pairs that beat chance are "conventional"; pairs that fall short are "atypical." The papers most likely to be hits were not the wildly unconventional ones. They were papers with a highly conventional core plus a tail of atypical combinations — mostly familiar ground, with one or two unusual leaps. Those hit the top 5% of citations 9.1 times per 100, against 5.8 for papers with the same conventional core and no atypical tail; papers novel without a conventional anchor managed 5.3.

One thing Uzzi et al. do not do, which I previously implied they did: they never measure the downside. They report the probability of a top-5% or top-1% hit and stop. Whether atypical combinations also raise the chance a paper goes uncited is simply not in the paper. It is evidence about the upside recipe, not about variance.

A natural experiment in risk-taking

Everything above is correlational. Novel things turn out to be more variable — but correlation invites the worry that some third factor, ambitious inventors say, drives both novelty and variance. The closest thing to a clean test comes from Conti (2014) in the Strategic Management Journal.

The design is two experiments, not one. American states differ in how strictly they enforce non-compete agreements, and two of them changed abruptly in opposite directions: in June 1994 the Texas Supreme Court made non-competes substantially harder to enforce, and in May 1996 the Florida legislature made them easier. Conti takes 337,054 patents filed by public companies between 1990 and 2000, assigns each to the state where its first inventor lives, and asks what happened in Texas after 1994 and in Florida after 1996 relative to every other state. The mechanism is asymmetric leakage: a departing researcher carries away the upside of a successful project but none of the downside of a failed one, so weak enforcement taxes high-variance projects specifically.

He measures three things on the same patents: whether it lands in the top 5% of citations, whether it receives no citations, and whether it opens a technology class the firm hasn't patented in for five years. In Florida, where enforcement tightened, all three rose. In Texas, where it loosened, all three fell. That mirrored pattern is the identification argument in one line, and the fact that breakthroughs and dead ends moved together is what licenses reading it as riskiness rather than as productivity.

I called this "variance, caused" the first time round. Having read the paper, I'd soften that. Each experiment has a single treated state; parallel trends is asserted in a sentence rather than plotted; "failure" means a granted patent with zero citations, so the projects a manager would actually call failures never enter the data; and the cross-sectional version — strict states against lax states, rather than before against after — finds nothing at all. The paper also reports only raw logit coefficients, never marginal effects, so there is no honest way to tell you how large the effect is in percentage points. Call it good directional evidence that an institutional nudge toward risk moves both tails at once.

The bet is partly riggable

If the story stopped here it would be a little fatalistic: novelty is a coin flip with a fat tail, take it or leave it. But the most interesting work is about how to tilt the odds — how to keep the breakthrough tail while trimming the failure tail.

The two tails aren't welded together. Fleming's dispersion parameter, remember, cannot see the two tails separately. Singh & Fleming (2010) looked at them one at a time — that is the whole point of the paper. They cut two separate indicators from the citation distribution of 509,840 US patents, top 5% and exactly zero, model each with its own logit, then trace the full distribution with quantile regressions to show the effect is more than a mean shift. Team-invented patents are about 28% more likely than lone-inventor patents to be a breakthrough and about 9% less likely to be a complete dud. Patents assigned to an organization do better at both ends too — though that second half is fragile, disappearing once you stop counting citations from the assignee's own later patents.

The mechanism is the part I got backwards. The intuitive story is that diverse experience opens up recombinant possibilities (fattening the top) while more eyes catch dead ends (trimming the bottom). The paper finds the reverse: the diversity of the inventors' past technology classes does relatively more work at the failure end, their external collaboration network more at the breakthrough end. As the authors put it, this calls "into question the common argument that diversity leads to breakthroughs by enabling wider recombinant search." Diversity is more filter than generator.

In biotechnology, the whole framing fails. This is the sharpest counter-evidence I have, and I under-reported it. Arts & Veugelers (2015) take 84,119 US biotechnology patents filed 1976–2001 and do what Fleming's method could not: measure the breakthrough rate, the average citation count and the outright failure rate separately, instead of inferring the tails from a variance parameter. Patents with at least one never-before-seen pair of subclasses are breakthroughs 2.2% of the time against 0.9% for the rest — and their share of zero-citation failures falls, from 40% to 31%. Higher mean, fatter right tail, thinner left tail. The authors put it flatly: "The biotech data therefore do not support the increasing variance effect but a mean shifting effect."

Their familiarity result, which I previously made the headline, is subtler than I had it. Reusing components you know well does nothing on its own — statistically zero once the interaction is included — and pays only insofar as those familiar components get put into unprecedented pairings. That rhymes nicely with the Uzzi recipe. But the reason biotech is a problem for my claim isn't the familiarity interaction; it's that where someone measured all three quantities directly, novelty shifted the distribution up instead of widening it.

You can hire down the downside. Arts & Fleming (2018) followed 396,336 inventors across 2.7 million patents, comparing each inventor against himself at the same firm on the patents where he enters a technology class he has never patented in. Novelty goes up about 7%; average value goes down 1–3%. The bet again. When the field-switching novice collaborates with someone who has patented in that field, the value penalty is more than erased (+8% on citations); leaning on the scientific literature does about three-quarters as much (+6%).

They then push on causality with an accident: the 1985 Michigan Antitrust Reform Act, which inadvertently repealed the statute banning non-compete enforcement and so pushed inventors changing jobs in Michigan into unfamiliar fields. Used as an instrument, the estimates keep their sign and grow an order of magnitude — novelty up 48–65%, value down 41–59%. Two caveats on those numbers: they describe inventors who switched fields because the law changed, not inventors in general, and the two remedies have no instrument at all, so that half stays correlational. Experts and science look like maps that make unfamiliar terrain less dangerous. We don't know that they are.

And novelty is not the same thing as value. A final cold shower from Kaplan & Vakili (2015). Rather than counting subclass pairs, they run a topic model over the abstracts of 2,276 fullerene and carbon nanotube patents and label the ones that introduce a genuinely new topic — novelty read from the inventors' own words rather than from the patent office's filing cabinet, with three expert coders calibrating the threshold.

Those topic-originating patents do well: 1.4 times the citations of the average patent and roughly triple the chance of a top-5% breakthrough. But they come from local search — distant and diverse recombination is negatively associated with originating a new topic — while distant recombination is what predicts citations directly. The two paths pull against each other, so the total effect of distant recombination on breakthrough status comes out indistinguishable from zero. That's the "double-edged sword," and only 20 of the 189 topic-originating patents were also top-5% cited. Being novel and being valuable are distinct achievements, sometimes produced by opposite search strategies — though this is one narrow corner of materials science, not a general test.

Objection! It's all mismeasured

The honest objection is that "impact" here almost always means forward citations, and "novelty" means some index built from patent classes or reference lists. Both are proxies, and both are contested. When I wrote this post the first time I waved the objection away by counting studies. Having read the two measurement papers properly, I can't.

Fontana, Iori, Montobbio & Sinatra (2020) stress-test the two dominant science novelty indicators on 230,854 American Physical Society articles from 1985–2005, against a randomized null model, a set of Nobel-related and milestone papers, and standard interdisciplinarity measures. The Uzzi-style "atypical combination" measure, they find, largely overlaps with mere interdisciplinarity — and the "first-ever combination" measure, which is Wang, Veugelers & Stephan's, "finds it difficult to tell novel and non-novel articles apart." Bornmann, Tekles, Zhang & Ye (2019) come at it from the other side, checking both scores against expert reviewers at F1000Prime who tag biomedical papers for presenting new findings. Their verdict splits the same way: they "propose the use of novelty score U" — the Uzzi-derived one — "but question the use of novelty score W," which is again Wang, Veugelers & Stephan's.

That lands squarely on this post's science evidence. And my claim that five different operationalizations converge on one shape was too generous: only three of these studies estimate anything about dispersion or both tails — Fleming (2001), Verhoeven et al. (2016) and Wang et al. (2017). Uzzi et al. and Kaplan & Vakili measure the upper tail only. Two of the three share an author, and the third is the one the validation studies question.

What survives is the patent side. Fleming and Verhoeven et al. use classification-based novelty — never-before-seen pairs of technology classes — a different construction from the reference-list measures Fontana and Bornmann tested, and untouched by their critiques; they agree with each other across a two-month 1990 cohort and a thirty-year global population. Conti's law changes move both tails with no novelty index involved at all. And delayed recognition cuts in the claim's favour: if the breakthrough tail keeps filling in for years, short citation windows understate it. The science side, though, is one paper — and one whose measure two other papers say doesn't work.

Bottom line

I read the evidence on the headline claim — novel knowledge combinations raise the variance of impact more than the mean — as well supported for patents and provisional for science. That's a step down from where I started, and it comes with three honest dents. In biotechnology, the one setting where the breakthrough rate, the mean, and the failure rate were measured separately rather than inferred from a variance parameter, novelty shifted the whole distribution up rather than widening it. On the science side, the one study finding the variance pattern uses a score that fails both validation exercises this post cites. And almost none of it is causal: the two quasi-experiments nudge incentives rather than novelty itself, and each rests on a single treated state.

The practical upshot is a portfolio one. Variance is cheap to someone who can hold many bets and expensive to someone holding one. Take the Uzzi hit rates at face value: fund a hundred conventionally grounded projects with no unusual leap in them and you expect about six top-5% hits; add the atypical dash and you expect about nine — but a lone inventor staking a single distant-novelty bet faces mostly the fat failure tail, with no offsetting draw to rescue the average. Novelty is the right call for those built to absorb the variance: diversified funders, large labs, tenured scientists insured against a few flops. It is a much riskier call for the solo founder or the junior researcher who needs this paper to land. And our evaluation machinery has it backwards — short citation windows, impact-factor screens, and one-shot funding decisions all penalize precisely the bets whose value is a slow, fat tail. The levers for more breakthroughs are to hold novelty in a portfolio, judge it on long horizons, anchor it to a familiar base, and pair the bold with the expert — not to exhort everyone to be more original.

So by all means be novel. Just know that you're probably buying a lottery ticket, price it like one, and make sure you can afford to lose.

What we don't know

  • Does the pattern survive a better novelty measure for science? The science-side variance result depends on a score that the two studies which tried hardest to validate it both question. Someone should re-run it with the score that did pass.
  • Is biotech the exception or the rule? Arts & Veugelers found a mean shift in the one field where all three quantities were measured directly rather than inferred from a dispersion parameter. Nobody has repeated that exercise elsewhere, so we don't know whether the variance story is a fact about invention or an artefact of how it is estimated.
  • Is the tail worth the price? Almost no one has computed the risk-adjusted return to novelty — whether the fat upside compensates for the fat downside, and for whom. The portfolio logic above is a sketch, not a measured price for risk.
  • Citations aren't welfare. Forward citations track technological influence, not private profit or social value, and the three can diverge.
  • We may be measuring the upside too early. Most studies use citation windows of 5–10 years, and delayed recognition means the breakthrough tail keeps filling in well past that.

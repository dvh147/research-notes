2026-06-02 · 13 min read · economics of innovation science of science

"Be original." "Think different." "Disrupt." The advice we give inventors, scientists, and founders is relentlessly pro-novelty, and it comes with an implied promise: novel work is better work. Combine ideas no one has combined before and you'll produce something more useful, more cited, more valuable.

It's a lovely theory. The trouble is that when economists go and measure it -- across millions of patents and papers, over decades, with half a dozen different ways of defining "novel" -- the promise mostly doesn't show up. Novel inventions are not, on average, more impactful than conventional ones. If anything they're a touch less so.

What novelty changes is not the average. It's the spread. Reach for an unusual combination of ideas and you widen the range of what can happen: you make a home run more likely and a strikeout more likely at the same time. The average barely moves; the variance balloons.

That reframes the whole pep talk. "Be novel" is not advice to raise your expected payoff. It's advice to buy a riskier lottery ticket -- to trade a predictable, middling result for a small chance at a breakthrough and a large chance at a dud. Which is a perfectly good thing to do, for the right person in the right situation. But it is a risk choice, not a free lunch, and treating it as a free lunch leads funders, managers, and inventors astray.

That's the claim of this post, and the evidence for it is unusually convergent. Let me build it up.

Invention is recombination -- and recombination is a search in the dark

Start with where the variance comes from. The dominant model of invention, going back to Schumpeter, treats a new invention as a recombination of existing components: the automobile is an engine plus a carriage; CRISPR gene editing borrows machinery bacteria already use. On this view, inventing is search -- you're wandering a vast landscape of possible component combinations looking for ones that work.

Here's the key intuition. If you recombine components you know well, in ways that have been tried before, you can predict the result pretty accurately -- it'll be fine, incremental, unsurprising. If you reach for components you've never used, or a pairing no one has attempted, you genuinely don't know what you'll get. You might stumble onto a new peak in the landscape. You might fall off a cliff. Unfamiliarity doesn't bias the result up or down so much as it makes the result unpredictable.

Lee Fleming put numbers on this in a foundational 2001 study in Management Science, "Recombinant uncertainty in technological search". Analyzing 17,264 US patents, he used a count model that estimates not just the mean number of citations an invention earns but its variance directly. The finding: patents built from new combinations of components are less useful on average, and at the same time more variable -- the variance of their impact is significantly higher. Novelty, in his words, "implies an increase in the variability that can result in both failure and breakthrough."

A toy example makes the shape concrete. Imagine impact is measured in forward citations, and suppose:

  • A conventional invention is a draw from a tight distribution: almost always lands between 8 and 12 citations, averaging 10. Predictable.
  • A novel invention is a draw from a wide distribution centered slightly lower -- say it averages 9 -- but with a long right tail (one in twenty earns 150) and a fat left tail (one in three earns zero).

Make a hundred of each. The conventional hundred cluster around 10 and produce no disasters and no moonshots. The novel hundred contain more zeros than the conventional batch and more triple-digit hits -- even though their average is a hair lower. If you only looked at the averages (9 vs 10) you'd conclude novelty is slightly bad and move on. You'd have missed the entire point: all the action -- every breakthrough and every flameout -- is in the novel pile.

Novelty fattens the tails, and thins the middle

Fleming's result has been replicated and sharpened with far bigger data and better measures of novelty. The most direct confirmation is Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016) in Research Policy, who build "ex ante" novelty indicators -- flags you can compute when the patent is filed, before you know how it did -- for 5.3 million patent families spanning 1980-2011. They separate two kinds of novelty: novelty in recombination (this patent joins technology classes never previously joined) and novelty in knowledge origins (it draws on technological or scientific knowledge that is itself brand new).

Then they ask how those flags relate to the eventual distribution of impact, again with a model that lets novelty move the mean and the spread separately. The result is the cleanest statement of the claim I know of: technological novelty lowers the average citations an invention earns while raising the variance of impact and the odds of landing among the extreme positive outliers. The rare inventions that are novel on all dimensions at once -- a new recombination drawing on new technological and new scientific knowledge -- are the most extreme of all: in the authors' data they have more than double the odds of landing as a 5-standard-deviation citation outlier, the kind of runaway hit that defines a technological generation.

Picture the two impact distributions side by side. Novelty doesn't slide the conventional curve to the right; it stretches it outward in both directions and hollows out the middle:

        impact (forward citations) ->
        zero    low     middle      high    extreme
CONVENTIONAL
        ###     #######  ##########  ####    #
                         ^ most inventions land here, predictably

NOVEL
        #######  #####   ####        #####   #####
        ^ more duds            thinner middle    ^ fatter breakthrough tail

Same area under each (these are a hundred inventions either way). But the novel batch has migrated out of the safe middle and into both tails. That is what "higher variance, similar-or-lower mean" looks like. It is the empirical signature that recurs through this whole literature.

The same pattern shows up in science

If this were just a quirk of patents, I'd be less confident. It isn't. The identical shape appears when you measure the novelty of scientific papers.

Wang, Veugelers & Stephan (2017), again in Research Policy, flag a paper as novel if it makes a first-time-ever combination of the research fields it cites, weighted by how hard that combination was to make. Across all 785,000 Web of Science articles from 2001, novelty does not raise a paper's average citations -- but it raises the variance: citation dispersion is about 18% higher for highly novel papers, and both tails thicken. Highly novel papers are about 18% more likely to land in the top 10% of citations and about 15% more likely to land in the bottom 10%. Over a long enough window they have roughly 57% higher odds of becoming a top-1% "big hit."

Two details make this study especially useful. First, the upside is delayed: highly novel papers are less likely to be top-cited when you only look three years out, and only pull ahead from about year four onward. The breakthrough tail takes time to materialize. Second, novel papers are systematically published in lower-impact-factor journals -- around 17% lower, holding quality constant -- because gatekeepers, judging on short horizons, are wary of them. The authors' warning is pointed: evaluation systems that lean on short-window citation counts and journal prestige are structurally biased against high-variance novel work. They will systematically under-fund exactly the bets whose payoff is a fat but slow tail.

There's a complementary and rather beautiful result from Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013), who analyzed 17.9 million papers in Science. They scored each paper's combinations of prior work as typical or atypical relative to what you'd expect by chance. The papers most likely to be hits were not the wildly unconventional ones. They were papers with a highly conventional core plus a tail of atypical combinations -- mostly standing on familiar ground, with one or two unusual leaps. Those papers hit the top 5% of citations about 9 times per 100, versus a baseline of 5 per 100 -- nearly double the hit rate. Papers that were novel without a conventional anchor did barely better than average (5.3 per 100). Hold that finding; it's the key to the most useful nuance in this whole area.

A natural experiment in risk-taking

Almost everything above is correlational: novel things turn out to be more variable. But correlation invites the worry that some third factor -- ambitious inventors, say -- drives both novelty and variance. The closest thing to a clean test comes from Conti (2014) in the Strategic Management Journal, who exploits an accident of US law. States differ, and have changed over time, in how strictly they enforce non-compete agreements. Stronger enforcement keeps inventors (and their knowledge) from leaking to rivals, which makes high-risk, hard-to-imitate R&D relatively more attractive to firms. So enforcement changes act like an external nudge toward riskier projects, independent of any one inventor's temperament.

The prediction, and the finding: where non-competes became more enforceable, firms' inventions moved into both tails of the value distribution at once -- more breakthroughs and more failures -- and into more novel technological areas. (Conti's paper is paywalled and I could not verify the exact effect sizes against the original tables, so I'm reporting the direction of the result, not magnitudes.) That two-tailed movement is the giveaway. A nudge toward novelty and risk doesn't shift the average outcome up; it spreads the outcomes out. That's variance, caused.

The bet is partly riggable

If the story stopped here it would be a little fatalistic: novelty is a coin flip with a fat tail, take it or leave it. But the most interesting recent work is about how to tilt the odds -- how to keep the breakthrough tail while trimming the failure tail. The bet, it turns out, is partly riggable, and this is where the honest qualifier to my claim lives.

The two tails aren't welded together. A natural assumption -- the one Fleming's model makes -- is that anything raising the breakthrough probability raises the failure probability symmetrically. Singh & Fleming (2010) showed that's wrong. Studying 515,816 patents with quantile regressions (which look at the whole distribution, not just the mean), they found that collaboration moves the two tails independently and in the same favorable direction. Relative to lone inventors, team-invented patents are about 28% more likely to be a breakthrough (top 5%) and about 9% less likely to be a complete dud (zero citations). Inventions backed by an organization do better still on both ends. The mechanism is intuitive: more eyes catch the dead ends early (trimming the failure tail), while diverse experience opens up more recombinant possibilities (fattening the breakthrough tail). Variance, in other words, is decomposable -- you can sometimes buy upside without buying symmetric downside.

Novelty over familiar ground is a better-than-fair bet. Recall the Uzzi recipe: conventional core, atypical dash. The patent analogue comes from Arts & Veugelers (2015), who studied 26 years of US biotechnology patents and distinguished what gets recombined from how novel the recombination is. Their finding is the cleanest exception to my headline: making new combinations of components the inventor already knows well raised average usefulness, raised the breakthrough rate, and lowered the failure rate -- all three at once -- so long as those familiar components were genuinely combined in unprecedented ways. This is not the symmetric fat-both-tails pattern. It's closer to a free lunch. The lesson is that the high-variance, lower-mean version of novelty is specifically the distant, unfamiliar kind -- reaching for components you don't understand. Novelty built on a base you've mastered tilts the odds in your favor rather than just spreading them.

You can hire down the downside. Arts & Fleming (2018) followed inventors who explored entirely new fields. Doing so raised the novelty of their output (by about 7%) but lowered its average value (by 1-3%) -- the bet again. Crucially, when the field-switching novice collaborated with an expert in the new field, or leaned on the scientific literature, the value penalty was muted or erased (interaction effects of roughly +8% and +9% respectively). They confirm the pattern using a quirky 1985 Michigan law change that inadvertently pushed inventors into new fields, so it isn't just self-selection. Expertise and science are maps that make unfamiliar terrain less dangerous to explore.

And novelty is not the same thing as value. A final cold shower from Kaplan & Vakili (2015), who used topic modeling to find patents that originate a genuinely new idea (cognitive novelty) and asked whether those were the valuable ones. Mostly not. Originating a new topic was driven by local search and paid only modestly (about 1.4x the citations); economic value came from broad recombination, which pulls in the opposite direction. In their nanotube-patent sample, only 20 of 189 truly novel patents were also among the top-cited. Being novel and being valuable are distinct achievements that even tend to trade off -- so "just be novel" is, on its own, a weak strategy. The jackpots are the rare patents that manage to be both, and they sit far out in the tail.

Objection! It's all mismeasured

The honest objection to everything above is that "impact" here almost always means forward citations, and "novelty" means some index built from patent classes or reference lists. Both are proxies, and both are contested.

On novelty: Fontana, Iori, Montobbio & Sinatra (2020) stress-tested the popular novelty indicators and found they don't all measure the same thing -- the Uzzi-style "atypical combination" measure overlaps heavily with mere interdisciplinarity, and another popular measure struggles to tell novel from non-novel articles apart. A validation study by Bornmann, Tekles, Zhang & Ye (2019) reached a similar split verdict -- one common novelty score lines up with experts' judgments, another mostly doesn't. So any single study's novelty flag should be taken with salt.

Here's why I don't think it sinks the claim. The variance result doesn't rest on one flag or one outcome. It shows up with classification-based novelty (Fleming; Verhoeven et al.), reference-combination novelty (Wang et al.; Uzzi et al.), and text/topic-model novelty (Kaplan & Vakili) -- and with citations, top-percentile "breakthrough" dummies, and patent-renewal value as the outcome. When five different ways of operationalizing a fuzzy idea all produce the same distinctive shape -- thin middle, fat tails -- mismeasurement is an unlikely culprit, because the measures' errors don't line up. If anything, the measurement problems bias us toward under-stating the tail: Wang et al.'s delayed-recognition result means short citation windows miss novel work's slow-arriving upside, so our estimate of the breakthrough tail is, if anything, too thin.

Bottom line

I read the evidence on the headline claim -- novel knowledge combinations raise the variance of impact far more than the mean -- as close to unambiguous. It is one of the more robustly replicated patterns in the economics of innovation, convergent across patents and papers, across decades, across measures, and from descriptive studies up to a couple of quasi-experiments. The corollary that the average return to distant novelty is flat-to-slightly-negative is also well supported, with one honest exception: novelty built by recombining familiar components appears to be a genuinely better bet, lifting the mean while trimming failures (Arts & Veugelers). Where I'd hedge hardest is on full causality -- most of the evidence is correlational, and even the natural experiments nudge incentives rather than novelty itself -- though the mechanism is clean and the quasi-experimental results point the right way.

If the claim is right, the practical upshot is a portfolio one. Variance is cheap to someone who can hold many bets and expensive to someone holding one. Take the Uzzi hit rates at face value: fund a hundred conventional projects and you expect about five top-5% hits; fund a hundred "conventional-core-plus-novel-dash" projects and you expect about nine -- but a lone inventor staking a single distant-novelty bet faces mostly the fat failure tail with no offsetting draw to rescue the average. So novelty is the right call for those built to absorb the variance: diversified funders, large labs, anyone with a portfolio, and tenured scientists insured against a few flops. It is a much riskier call for the undiversified -- the solo founder, the junior researcher who needs this paper to land. And it means our evaluation machinery has it backwards: short citation windows, journal-impact-factor screens, and one-shot funding decisions systematically penalize the high-variance bets whose whole value is a slow, fat tail. If you want more breakthroughs, the levers are to hold novelty in a portfolio, judge it on long horizons, anchor it to a familiar base, and pair the bold with the expert -- not to exhort everyone to be more original.

So by all means be novel. Just know that you're buying a lottery ticket, price it like one, and make sure you can afford to lose.

What we don't know

  • Is the tail worth the price? Almost no one has computed the risk-adjusted return to novelty -- whether the fat upside actually compensates for the fat downside, and for whom. The portfolio logic above is a sketch, not a measured Sharpe ratio for ideas.
  • Citations aren't welfare. Forward citations track technological influence, not private profit or social value, and the three can diverge. A high-variance pattern in citations needn't imply the same pattern in dollars or in welfare.
  • How wide is the familiar-novelty loophole? Arts & Veugelers find that recombining familiar components dodges the variance penalty in biotech patents. How general that is -- across fields, and how far you can push "familiar" before the tails reopen -- is genuinely open.
  • We may be measuring the upside too early. Delayed recognition means the breakthrough tail keeps filling in for years. Most studies use citation windows of 5-10 years; the true tail of the most novel work might be substantially fatter than even these estimates suggest.

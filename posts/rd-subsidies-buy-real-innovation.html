In 2000, three economists sat down to answer what sounds like a simple question: when a government spends money to encourage private research, does that money add to what companies would have spent on their own, or does it just replace it? Paul David, Bronwyn Hall, and Andrew Toole surveyed thirty-five years of econometric studies — laboratory data, firm data, industry data, country data — and came back with a shrug. The findings, they wrote, were "ambivalent"; roughly a third of the studies they reviewed suggested public money was crowding out private money rather than adding to it (David, Hall & Toole 2000). After three and a half decades of work, the honest answer to whether innovation policy did anything at all was: we can't tell.

That shrug is the whole problem in miniature. Governments now spend enormous sums to push private research along — direct grants to firms, and tax breaks that make each dollar of R&D cheaper. The single number that decides whether any of it is worth doing has a name: additionality. It is the amount of extra, real research and innovation a subsidy buys, over and above what the firm would have done anyway. If additionality is high, the policy is a bargain. If it is zero, we are handing money to companies to do what they were going to do regardless — or worse, paying them to rename their existing spending.

This post is about what we have learned since that 2000 shrug. The short version: well-designed R&D subsidies mostly do buy real, additional innovation — but the word "mostly" is carrying two heavy bags, and so is "well-designed." The clean evidence has arrived, and it tells a sharper story than "subsidies work" or "subsidies don't." It says additionality shows up reliably in one specific place — firms that can't easily finance research on their own — and it tends to leak away everywhere else.

Three things a subsidy dollar can do

Before the evidence, the anatomy. Imagine a government hands a firm a single dollar earmarked for R&D. Three things can happen to it.

It can crowd in. The firm spends the dollar on research it would not otherwise have done — and, in the good case, the grant also unlocks money the firm couldn't previously access, so its own spending rises too. Total real research goes up by more than a dollar. Additionality above one.

It can crowd out. The firm was already going to spend that dollar on R&D. It takes the public dollar, quietly withdraws one of its own, and pockets the difference. Reported "subsidized R&D" looks busy; total research is exactly where it would have been. Additionality of zero. This is the dollar-for-dollar substitution the 2000 reviewers were worried about.

It can be relabeled. The firm spends nothing new — it just renames a dollar of marketing or routine engineering as "research" and claims the subsidy. Reported R&D rises by a dollar; real R&D rises by nothing. Additionality of zero, plus a paperwork lie.

Everything that follows asks how much of each actually happens, and what separates the programs that crowd in from the ones that crowd out or get gamed. The post-2000 literature is so much more convincing than what David, Hall, and Toole reviewed because of one methodological upgrade: the regression discontinuity. Many subsidy programs hand out money using a sharp cutoff — an application score, a firm-size threshold, an eligibility rank. Firms just above and just below the cutoff are essentially identical, but one group gets the money and the other doesn't. Comparing them isolates the causal effect of the subsidy cleanly, the way a coin flip would. That trick is what turned a shrug into an answer.

Grants to firms that can't get a loan

Start with direct grants, and start with the cleanest study we have. Sabrina Howell took the U.S. Department of Energy's Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) program, which ranks applicants and funds them from the top down, and compared firms just above the funding line to firms just below it (Howell 2017). An early-stage Phase 1 award — about $150,000 — roughly doubled a firm's chance of later raising venture capital, lifting it from 10 percent to 19 percent. It increased the firm's later cite-weighted patents by at least 30 percent (about two-and-a-half times, in the discontinuity estimates), and it nearly doubled the probability that the firm ever earned positive revenue. These are not the fingerprints of money being shuffled around. They are the fingerprints of companies that exist and ship products because of a grant, and that otherwise might not have.

Two details matter enormously. First, the effects were concentrated among the most financially constrained firms — younger companies, hardware rather than software, less mature sectors. Second, Howell could rule out the cynical alternative: that the grant doesn't fund anything useful but merely acts as a government stamp of approval telling investors "this one is good." That certification story doesn't fit — the effect came from the money (funding a working prototype), not the prestige. And tellingly, the much bigger $1 million Phase 2 grants had no measurable effect at all. Small money to firms that couldn't otherwise get it: that is where the action was.

If one regression discontinuity in U.S. clean energy were all we had, I'd be cautious. It isn't. Pietro Santoleri and coauthors ran the same design on the European Union's SME Instrument — the first EU R&D subsidy aimed squarely at small firms — across more than forty countries and nearly every sector (Santoleri, Mina, Di Minin & Martelli 2024). The grants are substantial, €0.5 to €2.5 million. The results echo Howell with eerie consistency: firms that squeaked over the funding line saw cite-weighted patents rise by 15 to 31 percent, employment grow by 24 to 33 percent, their odds of attracting follow-on private equity roughly triple, and their risk of failure fall by several percentage points.

But the best thing about this paper is that it contains the cleanest test ever run of the funding-versus-certification question. The EU program also hands out a pure quality stamp — a "Seal of Excellence" — to high-scoring applicants it runs out of money to fund. These firms get the prestige and none of the cash. If certification were what mattered, they should improve too. They don't: across firm outcome after firm outcome, the Seal-of-Excellence firms show no improvement at all. The money is doing the work. The mechanism, in both the American and European studies, is the relief of a financial constraint — funding research that a credit market would not.

A third regression discontinuity, on a flagship Czech grant program, puts a number on the good case. Matej Bajgar and Martin Srholec find that among small and medium-sized firms, one koruna of public subsidy came with about 2.3 korunas of additional R&D spending (Bajgar & Srholec 2025) — crowding in, the subsidy more than paying for itself. Hold onto that paper, though: it has a sting in the tail.

Tax credits: cheaper research, more research

Direct grants pick winners. Tax credits do the opposite — they lower the price of R&D for everyone and let firms decide. Does cutting the price actually call forth more research, or do firms just bank the tax saving on research they'd have done anyway?

The strongest causal evidence again comes from a discontinuity. The United Kingdom sets the generosity of its R&D tax relief by firm size, with a sharp threshold. Antoine Dechezleprêtre and coauthors compared firms on either side of that line in administrative tax and patent records (Dechezleprêtre, Einiö, Martin, Nguyen & Van Reenen 2023). For the firms affected, the more generous treatment roughly doubled R&D spending and raised patenting by about 60 percent — and crucially, the extra patents were not junk; their quality held up. The effects persisted for up to seven years, so this is not a firm front-loading research it would have done later. Put in policy terms, the scheme generated about £1.70 of private R&D for every £1 of taxpayer money, and the authors estimate that UK business R&D would have been about 10 percent lower without it. The implied responsiveness of R&D to its after-tax cost — an elasticity of around 2.6 — is large.

It is also, the authors stress, not an economy-wide number: it comes from smaller, constrained firms near the threshold and shouldn't be stretched across the whole size distribution. That caveat lines up with everything else here.

Canada gives us the same lesson from a different angle. When it changed eligibility for its SR&ED tax credit in 2004, Ajay Agrawal, Carlos Rosell, and Timothy Simcoe found a 17 percent increase in total R&D among newly eligible small firms (Agrawal, Rosell & Simcoe 2020). The response was largest for firms that took the credit as a cash refund — that is, firms with no tax bill to offset, which is to say firms that were short of cash. Same fingerprint again: the money bites hardest where finance is the bottleneck.

These large, recent estimates sit on top of an older, more sobering benchmark. Back when tax-credit evidence came from cross-country panels rather than discontinuities, Nick Bloom, Rachel Griffith, and John Van Reenen estimated that a 10 percent fall in the cost of R&D raised R&D by only about 1 percent in the short run, but by nearly 10 percent in the long run (Bloom, Griffith & Van Reenen 2002). In other words: tax credits do work, the long-run response is roughly one-for-one, and it takes years to arrive — a more modest picture than the SME discontinuities, and the gap between them is itself the central finding of this literature.

The pattern: it's the constrained firms, every time

Step back and the studies stop looking like a pile of disagreeing results and start looking like a single law with a single exception. The additionality is real, and it lives almost entirely in small, young, financially constrained firms. For large incumbents, it mostly evaporates.

Watch the exception appear in study after study. In Bajgar and Srholec's Czech data, the 2.3-to-1 crowding-in was an SME story; among large firms, the same program crowded private R&D out. Florian Szücs evaluated the EU's big Framework Programmes and found that, on average, subsidies did not raise the private research budgets of the world's largest research companies — the additionality showed up only for smaller and more research-intensive firms (Szücs 2020). And in perhaps the bluntest demonstration, Chiara Criscuolo and coauthors studied a UK investment-subsidy program using European area-eligibility rules as a natural experiment: a 10-percentage-point bump in the maximum subsidy raised local manufacturing employment by about 10 percent — but only at small firms. Large firms, they write, "accept subsidies without increasing activity" (Criscuolo, Martin, Overman & Van Reenen 2019). They take the money and don't move.

This is the deep structure, and it makes economic sense. A subsidy can only add research if something was stopping the research from happening — and for a young firm with a good idea and no collateral, that something is finance. A large, cash-rich incumbent faces no such wall: it funds the projects it wants to and treats a subsidy as a windfall. The market failure that justifies the policy and the additionality the policy delivers are the same thing, viewed from two sides. So "do R&D subsidies work?" is the wrong question. The right one is "do they go to firms that are actually constrained?" A meta-analysis of the tax-credit literature reaches the matching verdict: the additionality is systematically stronger for smaller firms (Castellacci & Lie 2015).

"But it's just relabeling"

Now the objections, strongest first — because a claim like this is only worth as much as the counter-evidence it survives.

The most damaging is relabeling, and the cleanest evidence for it is alarming. Zhao Chen, Zhikuo Liu, Juan Carlos Suárez Serrato, and Daniel Xu studied a Chinese program that cut taxes sharply for firms whose reported R&D crossed a threshold — a design that practically dares firms to inflate the number (Chen, Liu, Suárez Serrato & Xu 2021). Using administrative tax data, they show reported R&D jumping right at the cutoff, and they estimate that 24.2 percent of reported R&D was relabeled — ordinary expenses dressed up as research to qualify. Nearly a quarter of the "research" the policy bought was a paperwork fiction.

That is the bad news, and it is real. But read the number the other way: about three-quarters of the reported increase was genuine new research. The authors find that doubling real R&D raises productivity by about 9 percent, and that even with the relabeling, modest spillovers make the program worth running. Their constructive point is about design: when relabeling is a serious risk, a threshold ("notch") design with verification beats an open-ended credit, because it concentrates scrutiny where the gaming happens. Relabeling is a tax on the headline numbers and an argument for monitoring — not a refutation of additionality.

"It just bids up salaries — and crowds out"

The second objection is subtler and, to me, more important. Even the genuine extra R&D spending might buy less real research than it looks like, because some of it just becomes higher pay. Austan Goolsbee made this argument long ago: the supply of scientists and engineers is fairly fixed in the short run, so when governments flood the sector with research money, a chunk of it bids up wages rather than funding additional work. He estimated this could make conventional estimates of R&D-policy effectiveness 30 to 50 percent too high (Goolsbee 1998). When you read "R&D rose by 17 percent," you should mentally shade part of that toward "researchers got a raise."

And the old crowding-out worry has not vanished. Marianna Marino and coauthors, studying French firms over 1993–2009, found no additionality on average — and, in places, outright substitution, with crowding-out most pronounced at medium-to-high subsidy levels and under the tax-credit regime (Marino, Lhuillery, Parrotta & Sala 2016). Even the SBIR program that looks so good in Howell's hands looked very different to Scott Wallsten two decades ago: using a different method and focusing on the larger Phase II grants, he concluded that SBIR money crowded out firm-financed R&D roughly dollar-for-dollar and didn't raise employment (Wallsten 2000).

How do Howell and Wallsten reach opposite conclusions about the same program? They measure different margins: Howell isolates small, early-stage grants to constrained firms and looks at innovation outputs; Wallsten looks at larger grants and R&D dollars. Both can be right — the constrained-firm law again. And a careful meta-analysis of 52 studies splits the difference honestly: across the literature as a whole, subsidies do not fully crowd out private investment, but the average additionality is "not substantial" (Dimos & Pugh 2016). The clean discontinuities find big effects because they target the firms where big effects exist; pointed at everyone, the average is muted.

The softest spot: does more research become more innovation?

Here is the objection I am least able to answer. Almost everything above measures inputs — R&D spending — or near-term proxies like patents. The thing we actually care about is innovation and productivity, and the link between them is weaker than the input numbers suggest.

Two of the better studies make the point against interest. Walter Melnik and Andrew Smyth, exploiting the staggered rollout of U.S. state R&D tax credits, find that the credits clearly raised R&D spending — and yet produced no detectable increase in aggregate patenting, and no rise in the scientific quality of patents (Melnik & Smyth 2024). (They did find more patent novelty and higher market value, so it isn't nothing — but the headline output measure didn't move.) And recall Bajgar and Srholec's Czech SMEs, the crowd-in success story: the extra R&D those firms did, impressive as it was, did not show up as more patents, more employment, or higher productivity. Money in, research in — but the innovation out the other end was hard to find.

So the honest scorecard is layered. That subsidies raise R&D spending in constrained firms is, by now, well established. That the extra spending becomes more patents and prototypes is well supported in the best grant studies (Howell, Santoleri) and the UK tax study (Dechezleprêtre et al.). That it becomes more productivity and welfare is the least pinned-down link of all.

Why it can still be a bargain

Given all those haircuts — relabeling, wage-bidding, crowding-out at large firms, a leaky path from spending to output — is the policy a wash? No, because of the number that justifies innovation policy in the first place: research throws off enormous benefits to other firms that the inventor never captures. Nick Bloom, Mark Schankerman, and John Van Reenen estimate the social return to R&D at around 55 percent against a private return of about 21 percent — the social payoff is something like two-and-a-half times the private one (Bloom, Schankerman & Van Reenen 2013), and more recent work puts the ratio closer to four to one (Lucking, Bloom & Van Reenen 2019). The UK and EU grant studies both find exactly this kind of spillover onto technologically neighboring firms, with no offsetting business-stealing.

Run the back-of-the-envelope. Start with the UK's £1.70 of private R&D per £1 of public money. Shade it down hard for Goolsbee's wage effect — say, by 40 percent — and again for the loose link to real innovation, and you might be left with about £1 of genuine extra research per £1 spent, maybe less. But that research carries a social return two-to-four times its private one, so even on conservative assumptions the public gets back more than it put in — the spillovers are the whole point. This is the same wedge between private and social value that makes the case for innovation policy at all — a wedge I've written about before. Imperfect additionality, multiplied by a large enough spillover, still clears the bar.

Bottom line

After the 2000 shrug, the evidence has genuinely moved. I read it like this. That well-targeted, early-stage grants to financially constrained firms buy real additional innovation is now fairly convincing — three independent regression discontinuities on three continents agree, and the cleanest of them rules out the cynical certification story directly. That R&D tax credits raise real private research, especially for smaller firms, is solid, though the average economy-wide response is more modest and slow than the SME headlines. That subsidies to large incumbent firms add much of anything is doubtful — the weight of the clean evidence is that they take the money without moving. Relabeling and wage-bidding are real but bounded leaks, not refutations: they shrink the numbers, they don't zero them. And the step from research spending to measured productivity is the part I'd most want better evidence on before banking it.

So the seed claim survives, with its qualifiers earned rather than pasted on. R&D subsidies mostly buy real innovation, not relabeled spending — when they are aimed at firms that cannot finance research themselves, and monitored so the money can't be renamed. Pointed there, they are among the better-evidenced tools in the innovation-policy kit (Bloom, Van Reenen & Williams 2019). Sprayed indiscriminately at large firms, they mostly subsidize what would have happened anyway.

What we don't know

Several things, and they are not minor. First, output additionality: we are far more confident that subsidies raise R&D spending than that they raise productivity and welfare, and a couple of good studies find the spending without the downstream payoff. Second, the general-equilibrium ceiling: Goolsbee's wage-bidding means that, absent a bigger supply of scientists, pushing more money through a fixed pool of talent has sharply diminishing returns — which is why some economists argue R&D subsidies should be paired with policies that expand the talent pool, like skilled immigration and STEM training (Bloom, Van Reenen & Williams 2019). Third, design: grants versus credits versus notches, basic versus applied, refundable versus not — these choices clearly matter, and a structural literature is only beginning to say how. One striking result: a uniform research subsidy can worsen the allocation of research by over-rewarding applied work, with basic-research support doing more good (Akcigit, Hanley & Serrano-Velarde 2021). Fourth, scale: the prettiest elasticities come from small firms near eligibility thresholds; whether they would survive a program ten times larger is genuinely unknown. The 2000 shrug is over. The fine print is not.

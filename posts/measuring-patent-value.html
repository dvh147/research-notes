Every year, the owner of a patent gets a letter from the patent office asking for money. Pay the renewal fee and the patent lives another year. Don't, and it lapses into the public domain. The fees start small and escalate over the twenty-year term.

Ariel Pakes noticed in 1986 that this letter is a valuation machine. A patent owner who pays a €2,000 fee is telling you they expect at least €2,000 of remaining benefit from holding the exclusion right. One who lets it lapse is telling you the opposite. Watch enough cohorts of patents lapse at enough different fee levels and you can back out the whole distribution of what patents are worth — without ever asking anyone a question.

It is a beautiful idea, and it is one of at least four completely different ways economists put a number on the private value of a patented invention. The others are to watch the stock market react when a patent is granted, to survey the inventor and simply ask, and to find cases where somebody actually paid cash. All four are in active use. All four are cited interchangeably, in the same literatures, often in the same paper.

Here is what I take the evidence to show. The four rulers agree tolerably well about the pile of patents and disagree badly about the patent. Ask them how much of a firm's return on research comes from patent protection and they land in the same broad territory. Ask them which of two specific patents is worth more and they will frequently disagree. And the disagreement is not mostly noise that averages away — they are measuring different quantities on differently selected samples.

That matters because patent value has quietly become an input to a lot of economics. It stands in for innovation output in growth regressions, for research productivity in evaluations of public funding, for the damage done by a policy change. If the ruler is decent in aggregate and poor at the patent level, then studies that aggregate are on firmer ground than studies that compare — and a great many studies compare.

Ruler one: what you'll pay to keep it

The renewal approach is the oldest and the most economically disciplined. Pakes (1986) modelled the renewal decision as the exercise of an option: each year the owner learns something about what the patent is earning and decides whether the expected remaining returns beat the fee. Fit that model to the observed pattern of lapses and you recover the distribution of returns. Schankerman and Pakes (1986) did it across the United Kingdom, France, and Germany.

The estimates were sobering. Mark Schankerman (1998), fitting the model to every patent application filed in France between 1969 and 1987, concluded that patent protection is "a significant, but not the major, source of private returns to inventive activity." Jean Lanjouw (1998) built a richer version — one where owners also pay to enforce the patent against infringers — for West German patents in four technology areas (computers, textiles, combustion engines, pharmaceuticals) from 1953 to 1988, and found the aggregate value of protection running at roughly 10% of related research spending. James Bessen (2008) did the American version on almost every US patent issued in 1991 and put the median patent at about $7,200 and the mean at about $78,000 (in 1992 dollars) — a ratio of eleven, which is the famous skew in one line.

Note carefully what this ruler measures. Not the value of the invention. The value of the legal right to exclude, to this owner, from today forward. If the invention is worth a fortune but a competitor can design around the patent in an afternoon, the renewal ruler reads close to zero — correctly, on its own terms.

It is also a structural estimate, and worth knowing how much rests on assumptions. Bessen fixes the discount rate at 10% a year rather than estimating it, and reports that of the lognormal, Pareto and Weibull distributions, only the lognormal would converge. When he holds the depreciation rate at 15% a year instead of estimating it, one sample's mean patent value lands at $107,906 — a large move from a single modelling choice.

Ruler two: what the stock market says

The second approach lets investors do the valuation. Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman (2017) took patents issued to publicly traded US firms from 1926 to 2010 and read the firm's abnormal stock return in a narrow window around the day the grant became public news. Strip out the market's prior expectation that a grant was coming, filter the remaining noise, and what's left is the market's revision of the firm's value attributable to that patent. They report that these patent-level estimates line up positively with citation counts and predict firm growth better than citation-weighted patent counts do.

There is an older cousin: instead of an event window, regress firm market value (Tobin's q) on stocks of research spending, patents, and citations. Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg (2005) ran this on US firms and found an extra citation per patent associated with about 3% higher market value. Blundell, Griffith, and Van Reenen (1999), using a panel of British manufacturing firms and a count-data model that controls for persistent differences between firms, found the same innovation is worth more on the market when the firm holding it has a larger market share.

This ruler prices something different again: the market's expectation of future cash flows, for a firm whose shares happen to be publicly traded. It sees the invention and the right together, through the filter of what investors believed that week.

Ruler three: ask the inventor

The third approach is the least glamorous and, on some questions, the most informative. Find the inventor and ask what they would have accepted to sell the patent on the day it was granted.

Dietmar Harhoff, Francis Narin, F.M. Scherer, and Katrin Vopel (1999) did this for 964 inventions patented in the United States and Germany, deliberately sampling German patents whose owners had paid renewal fees all the way to full-term expiry in 1995 — the survivors, already revealed to be worth something. They then counted citations. Patents renewed to full term were significantly more cited than those that lapsed early, and the higher the owner's value estimate, the more citations the patent had drawn. This is the founding evidence that citations track value at all, and it holds up.

The approach then went industrial. PatVal-EU surveyed the inventors of 9,017 European patented inventions (Giuri et al. 2007). Working from the 8,217 with usable answers, Gambardella, Harhoff, and Verspagen (2008) estimated a European patent value distribution with a mean of €3.1 million, a median of €397,000, and a mode of about €6,400.

Then they reported the number that should be pinned above every desk in this field. The four standard patent indicators — forward citations, backward references, number of claims, and the size of the international patent family — together explain 2.7% of the variance in patent value. Add 116 patent-class dummies on top and you reach 11.3%. The authors call the rest "the measure of our ignorance."

One honest qualification, because it cuts against the easy reading: in the same paper, citations do a good job at the extreme. Patents in the top few percentiles of the citation distribution are markedly more likely to land in the top value classes. Citations are close to useless for ranking a typical patent and genuinely informative about whether a patent is exceptional.

Ruler four: what somebody actually paid

The fourth ruler is the one you would have designed first if the data existed: find the price.

Timo Fischer and Jan Leidinger (2014) used ten live auctions run by Ocean Tomo between 2006 and 2009 — 678 lots containing 1,784 US patents, of which 573 sold — to test the standard indicators against prices somebody actually paid. Forward citations and family size do predict price: an extra citation in the first five years is worth about $14,200 on a mean patent value of $148,500. But adding forward citations to their model raises the explained variance by 0.6 percentage points, from 75.7% to 76.3%.

Two cautions on that 76%, both of which matter. It is the fit for log price; the same specification on price in levels reaches 24.5%. And the auction sample is selected on exactly the variables under test — patents offered at Ocean Tomo have significantly more citations (15.5 versus 11.0) and larger families than technology- and cohort-matched controls. Odasso, Scellato, and Ughetto (2015) worked the same auction data and separated the seller's asking price from the closing price: forward citations predict both, while the number of claims predicts only the closing price, and only when the buyer is a non-practising entity — a firm that buys patents to license or litigate rather than to make anything.

The oddest result in the auction data is not about patents at all. In Fischer and Leidinger's marginal-effects table, the largest single effect is a dummy for whether the patent was sold alone rather than inside a bundle — worth about $148,000, roughly doubling the value. How a patent is packaged for sale matters about as much as everything intrinsic to it.

Then there is a data source I had not seen before writing this, and which I think is the sharpest evidence in the whole area. Germany's Employee Invention Act of 1957 requires firms to compensate employee inventors in proportion to what the invention actually earns, usually calculated by applying a standard royalty rate to the sales it generates (Harhoff & Hoisl 2007). That produces a paper trail of realized returns. Jesse Giummo (2010) obtained these records from about a dozen large German firms — 1,172 inventions with priority dates between 1977 and 1982 that also received a US patent — and compared them with everything else.

What he found is the crux of this post. These records measure, in his words, "the value of the use of the invention and not the value of patent protection." Regressed on his own estimates of the same patents' renewal value, they produce an R² of 0.05. Two rulers, the same patents, the same country: they share about five percent of their variance.

So where do they agree?

On the aggregate, and more than you might expect after all that.

The renewal studies land in the same broad territory across three countries and four decades: patent rents are a modest fraction of research spending. The exact numbers vary more than that summary suggests. The usual way to express them is as an "equivalent subsidy rate" — the cash subsidy to research that would reward firms as much as the patent system does. Bessen puts the European estimates of that rate at 4% to 35%, averaging about 18%, with his own US firm-level figure near 3%, at the bottom of the range. But nobody using this method finds that patents are how firms mostly get paid for research.

Better, one paper checks two rulers against each other on purpose. Bessen (2009) worried that renewal estimates might be understated, because they fit an assumed distribution to observed lapses and then extrapolate to the valuable patents nobody ever abandons. So he built a theoretically grounded upper bound from Tobin's q regressions on US public firms 1979–1997, and laid the estimates side by side in 1992 dollars. Renewal and re-assignment studies gave $47,000 (Serrano), $62,000 (Barney), and $78,000–$113,000 (Bessen's own). His market-value upper bound: $112,000. Four methods, same order of magnitude.

He also nails down which renewal numbers to trust. The upper-tail extrapolation problem afflicts means; renewal-based medians do not suffer from it at all.

And every ruler finds the same extreme skew — a few patents worth a fortune, most worth almost nothing. That is an earlier post, and I won't relitigate it beyond noting it is the one thing all four methods agree on.

Why they fall apart on the individual patent

Start with the cleanest illustration I know. Take one variable — an additional forward citation on a US patent — and ask two well-regarded studies what it is worth.

Bessen's renewal model says $3,000 to $5,000. Hall, Jaffe, and Trajtenberg's market value regression implies, on Bessen's own recalculation of their coefficients, $327,000. That is a factor of about eighty, and neither party has made an arithmetic error. Bessen explains it in a sentence: "we are measuring different things. Hall et al. measure the relationship between a patent citation and the value of the technology to the firm generally; I measure specifically the effect of a patent citation on the value of the rents generated by a patent per se."

That is the whole post in one comparison. Four reasons it happens, in rough order of how much work each is doing.

They price different objects. The renewal ruler prices the exclusion right. The German compensation records price the use of the invention. The survey asks about the patent as a sellable asset, which bundles the two — Gambardella and coauthors say so explicitly, which is why they expect their numbers to exceed renewal-based ones. Bessen even sizes the gap: comparing his estimate of the rents attached to an invention with the rents attached to its patent, he concludes the former "might be an order of magnitude larger" than the latter.

How big is the wedge between "the invention" and "the invention plus its patent"? That question has a name — the patent premium — and the literature's answer depends entirely on a modelling choice that is easy to miss. Jensen, Thomson, and Yong (2011) sent a survey to every Australian patent applicant from 1986 to 2005 and compared the reported value of the 1,790 inventions whose applications were granted with those that were refused. A granted patent goes with 47% higher reported invention value, or 38% measured against sales revenue to date. That is a comparison across applications, not an experiment: examiners refuse applications for reasons plausibly related to merit, and the authors say so, calling their figure an upper bound because everyone in the sample has already disclosed.

Arora, Ceccagnoli, and Cohen (2008), estimating a structural model of the joint decision to do research and to patent on the 1994 Carnegie Mellon Survey of US manufacturing, report what looks at first like the opposite: patenting the typical innovation lowers its expected value, and patent protection "provides a positive premium on average in only a few industries."

I set these up as contradicting each other in the first draft of this post. They don't. Arora and coauthors' premium conditional on the firm choosing to patent is large and positive — Jensen and coauthors cite it as 0.5, right beside their own 40–50%. The two numbers are different because they are different quantities: what patenting does to a randomly drawn innovation, versus what it does to the innovations firms actually select for patenting. Firms patent when they expect a premium, so the conditional figure exceeds the unconditional one by construction. Which of the two you estimate is a measurement choice, and it changes the answer by more than the gap between any two datasets in this literature.

Each ruler sees a different, selected sample. Auction prices exist only for patents somebody chose to auction and someone chose to buy. Assignment records (Graham, Marco & Myers 2018) cover only patents that were traded, and De Marco and coauthors (2017) show which ones get traded is systematically related to their origin and science-intensity. Gambardella, Giuri, and Luzzi (2007) find in the PatVal data that many owners willing to license never do, which they read as evidence of transaction costs — so observed prices are the deals that survived a filter. The stock-market ruler only works for listed firms. The renewal ruler works for everyone, but as Serrano (2010) shows, renewal and resale are jointly determined, so a renewal-based value is partly a statement about the patent's current owner.

Some value is invisible to some rulers by construction. Torrisi and coauthors (2016), surveying inventors in Europe, the US and Japan on patents filed at the European Patent Office in 2003–2005, found a substantial share of patents "neither used internally nor for market transactions" — blocking patents and sleeping patents. The value of a blocking patent is a cost avoided. No transaction-based or revenue-based ruler can see it. The renewal ruler can, because the owner keeps paying.

This is not a small residual. In Giummo's German compensation records, 73% of inventions show essentially no realized value. In PatVal-EU, only 7.9% of patents were valued below €30,000. For both to be true, survey respondents must be assigning substantial value to patents that never earned a euro from use — option value, blocking value, the value of denying a rival.

Citations aren't even a scalar. Sharon Belenzon (2012) split the citations a firm's patents receive into those the firm itself later builds on and those it doesn't, and found the first positively related to market value and the second negatively related — a citation you don't build on is a rival standing on your shoulders. A raw count adds them together and cancels the sign.

Objection: isn't this just measurement error?

The honest objection is that all four rulers might be noisy readings of one true value, in which case the disagreement is a precision problem, not a validity problem, and it washes out with enough data.

I don't think that survives contact with the most recent evidence. Arora, Belenzon, Ferracuti, and Nagar (2026) examine the stock-market measure specifically and show its error is not classical: it is negatively correlated with the truth, so the method understates the best patents and overstates the weakest ones. Worse, the method assumes every patent is drawn from a single value distribution — which logically implies any two groups of patents have the same expected value. Comparing group means with it therefore tests a hypothesis inside a framework that assumes the answer is zero. When they re-estimate allowing two distributions, the value premium for large-team patents goes from 2.53% to 26.1%, a factor of 9.3.

They are careful about the scope of this, and so should I be: "this paper does not argue that Kogan et al. (2017)'s method is flawed. What is flawed is how the resulting estimates are used in certain applications." Used as an explanatory variable, the measure is unbiased and works well — though the standard errors need bootstrapping. Systematic, value-correlated error does not average away; it biases comparisons.

A second objection deserves a firmer concession: citations do work. The positive relation is real and repeatedly found, against survey values, against market values, and against actual auction prices. Bessen puts it exactly right in his own abstract: highly cited patents are more valuable, "however, patent citations explain little variance in value." How little? In his renewal model, citation statistics never account for as much as 6% of the variance in patent value in any specification, ranging from 2.2% in "other" technologies to 12.0% in computers and communications. And here is the fact that makes it concrete: of the top 10% of 1991 US patents by citations received, 37% were not renewed to full term. Their owners, who knew what they were worth, declined to pay.

The constructive response is to stop using one indicator. Lanjouw and Schankerman (2004) combine several noisy indicators into a single latent quality index, and Fischer and Leidinger's full model explains far more of auction price variance than citations and family size alone. The information is in the patent documents; it just isn't in the two variables everyone reaches for. But a composite still has to decide which object it targets — the right, or the invention — and most don't say.

Bottom line

If you want to know how much of the return to research firms capture through patents, the literature has a defensible answer and several methods support it: a modest share, somewhere in the range of a few percent to a third of research spending, with pharmaceuticals a likely exception at the high end. I'd call that fairly convincing, with two caveats — the renewal studies share a modelling framework, so they are less independent than their count suggests, and the range is wide enough that the point estimate you quote should come with its method attached.

If you want to know whether patent A is worth more than patent B, you should say which ruler you used and accept that another might reverse you. That part I'd call well established: four independent designs reach it in three countries — survey values against indicators (2.7% of variance), renewal values against indicators (under 6%), auction prices against indicators (0.6 percentage points), and realized compensation against renewal value (R² of 0.05).

The practical rule that falls out is unglamorous but real: match the ruler to the question. Studying how patent policy changes firms' incentives to hold rights? Renewal, and quote the median rather than the mean. Studying how much an invention contributed to a firm's value? The market, with Arora and coauthors' correction if you are comparing groups. Studying what an invention was worth in use? You want realized returns, and you will mostly not have them. Studying breakthroughs rather than money? Cite the text-based measures too.

What we don't know

The biggest gap is that almost nobody runs the comparison directly. Giummo (2010) is the closest thing to a head-to-head on the same patents, and it is one paper on about a dozen German firms' compensation records from a 1977–1982 cohort. What the field is missing is a single large sample of patents carrying a renewal-implied value, a stock-market value, a surveyed reservation price, and an observed transaction price all at once, so that the correlation matrix could simply be printed. Everything above is assembled from studies that each hold one or two rulers.

We also don't know how much of the divergence is the objects and how much is the selection. I've argued both matter; I can't tell you the split, and I don't think anyone can yet.

Finally, all of this predates the current wave of machine-learning valuation models trained on patent text. Those may well explain far more variance than 2.7% — Fischer and Leidinger's full model already hints at it. But a model trained to predict auction prices has learned to predict auction prices, on the selected sample of patents that reach auction, where being sold alone rather than in a bundle is worth more than any property of the invention. Better prediction of one ruler is not the same as resolving the disagreement between four.

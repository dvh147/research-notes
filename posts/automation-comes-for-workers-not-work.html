In 1969 the first cash machine in the United States went into a wall in Rockville Centre, New York. Over the next forty years, banks installed something like 400,000 more. The cash machine did exactly what a bank teller did — counted out your money and took your deposit — only faster, cheaper, and at three in the morning. Everyone could see where this was going. The teller was finished.

The teller was not finished. As the economist James Bessen has documented, the number of bank tellers in America rose over the decades that the ATM conquered the lobby. The machine did shrink the staff of a single branch: Bessen estimates the average urban branch went from around 21 tellers to about 13. But a branch that needs fewer tellers is a cheaper branch to run, so banks — newly deregulated and competing for deposits — opened a lot more branches. More branches with 13 tellers each added up to more tellers than fewer branches with 21. And the job that remained changed: a teller stopped being a human cash dispenser and became something closer to a salesperson, steering customers toward loans and accounts, doing the relationship work the machine couldn't (Autor 2015).

The ATM story is the cheerful one economists tell to calm people down about robots and AI. It is true, and it is incomplete. Because somewhere in those numbers is a teller who was good at counting cash, slow at selling, fifty-five years old, and out of a job. The aggregate held. She did not.

That gap — between what happens to the workforce and what happens to the worker — is the whole subject of this post. The automation debate constantly runs two questions together that the evidence answers very differently:

  1. Will automation cause mass unemployment? Will there be enough jobs?
  2. Does automation hurt people? Does it wreck the lives of those it displaces?

Read across the best labor economics of the last three decades and the answers come back: question one, mostly no; question two, emphatically yes. Automation, as a rule, comes for workers, not for work. The jobs reorganize and the count holds up; the specific people whose jobs were automated absorb a large, lasting, sometimes brutal loss. The "(mostly)" in my title is doing real work, and I'll spend a section earning it — there are conditions under which the aggregate cushion fails too. But start with the cleaner half.

The job vanishes, the work doesn't

The best single study of an automation shock I know was published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2024, and it's about telephone operators. In Feigenbaum and Gross (2024), two economic historians study what happened when AT&T tore out its human switchboards.

You have to remember how big this job was. In 1920, "telephone operator" was among the most common occupations for American women — roughly 2% of the entire female workforce, and about 4% of young, white, US-born working women. Counting turnover, Feigenbaum and Gross estimate that nearly 14% of women born around 1900 worked as an operator at some point. AT&T was the largest employer in the country, and operators were about half of it. The fraction of women exposed to this shock, the authors note, is similar to the share of today's American workforce ringing up cashiers and answering customer-service lines.

Then, exchange by exchange between 1920 and 1940, AT&T cut over to mechanical dial switching and the job evaporated. Company engineers had projected the automatic equipment would cut operators in big cities by 70 to 80%, and that is roughly what happened: in cities that converted, the number of young women working as operators fell 50 to 80%, immediately and permanently.

Here is the surprise. Automating away one of the largest female occupations in America did not reduce the employment of the next generation of women in those cities. Feigenbaum and Gross track successive cohorts before and after each city's cutover, and the young women who would have been operators simply went elsewhere — into secretarial and typing jobs, into food service, and notably into kinds of work that hadn't previously hired women like them. The local labor market did not re-equilibrate at lower wages. The work that operators did vanished; the demand for young women's labor did not.

This is not a fluke of one occupation. It's the central tendency of a large literature:

  • Industrial robots raised productivity (about 0.36 percentage points a year of labor-productivity growth across rich countries) and did not significantly reduce total employment, even as they cut the low-skill employment share (Graetz and Michaels 2018).
  • A patent-text measure of automation finds it raises employment in the local labor markets exposed to it, through job growth in services (Mann and Püttmann 2023).
  • Routine-replacing technology displaced a lot of European workers between 1999 and 2010 and still produced net employment growth, because cheaper production expanded demand for goods and thus for labor (Gregory, Salomons, and Zierahn 2022).
  • When you put trade and technology side by side, American local labor markets exposed to technology polarized — more jobs at the top and bottom — but did not suffer a net employment decline, whereas markets exposed to Chinese import competition did (Autor, Dorn, and Hanson 2015). Hold onto that contrast; it matters later.

The evidence here is strong but not unanimous, so let me put it on the confidence ladder honestly: the best evidence suggests that automating an occupation does not, by itself, reduce total employment — the work reorganizes faster than it disappears. That is a "best evidence suggests," not a "law of nature," and I'll come back to the exception.

Where the jobs went: a machine removes tasks, not sectors

Why does the work reorganize instead of just disappearing? The cleanest way to see it is to stop thinking of a job as a thing a machine either does or doesn't do, and start thinking of a job as a bundle of tasks (Autor, Levy, and Murnane 2003; Acemoglu and Restrepo 2018).

A toy example. Picture an office clerk in 1980 whose job is ten tasks: filing, fetching records, adding up columns, retyping drafts, and six other things involving judgment and dealing with people. A computer arrives and automates four of them — the filing, fetching, and arithmetic. Three things now happen at once. First, the displacement effect: the office needs fewer clerk-hours per memo, so demand for clerks-as-they-were falls. Second, the remaining six tasks — the judgment and the people — become more valuable, because they're now the bottleneck, so whoever can do them gets paid more. Third, brand-new tasks appear that didn't exist before: someone has to manage the database, troubleshoot the machine, analyze the reports it spits out. Acemoglu and Restrepo call this third force the reinstatement effect — technology creating new work in which labor has the advantage (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019).

When reinstatement keeps pace with displacement, the job count holds. And historically it has more than kept pace. In a remarkable accounting of eight decades of job titles, Autor, Chin, Salomons, and Seegmiller (2024) find that the majority of employment today is in job specialties that didn't exist in 1940 — solar-panel installer, flight attendant, oncology nurse, web developer. We don't notice the new work because by the time it's ordinary, it's invisible. The reinstatement happens, but it tends to happen somewhere else: in different industries, requiring different skills, often concentrated in dense and educated cities (Lin 2011), and frequently a generation later. Which is exactly why the economy can be fine while the operator is not.

But the operators paid

Now the other half of the ledger, and here the evidence is not mixed at all.

Go back to Feigenbaum and Gross, because the same paper that delivers the reassuring aggregate result also follows the women who were already plugging cables when the dial arrived. Using a clever genealogy-based method to track women through marriage name-changes, they find that incumbent operators bore the cost. A decade after their city converted, the operators were less likely to be working as operators (obviously), but also less likely to be working at all — many of the older ones simply left the workforce — and those who did work were more likely to be in lower-paying jobs. The authors put it precisely: incumbent workers, who had the least time to adjust and the most invested in the occupation, "suffer the largest consequences, whereas future generations are better able to adapt."

That sentence is the thesis of this post, and it generalizes far beyond switchboards. Losing your job to a shock is one of the most damaging things that can happen to a worker's finances, and economists have measured it about as carefully as we measure anything:

  • The foundational study, Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan (1993), found that high-tenure workers who lose jobs at distressed firms suffer earnings losses averaging about 25% per year, persisting for years, and large even for those who find new jobs at similar firms. This has been replicated repeatedly — by Couch and Placzek (2010), and with modern matched data by Lachowska, Mas, and Woodbury (2020) and Schmieder, von Wachter, and Heining (2023), who show the losses come mostly from a fall in the wage rate — stranded skills and broken worker-firm matches — not just lost hours.
  • The losses are not only financial. In a sobering study matching Pennsylvania layoff records to death records, Sullivan and von Wachter (2009) estimate that displacement raises a high-seniority worker's mortality by 50 to 100% in the year afterward, and by 10 to 15% even twenty years later — on the order of one to one-and-a-half years of lost life expectancy for a man displaced at age forty.

And crucially, technology is implicated directly. Braxton and Taska (2023) use the near-universe of online job ads to measure technological change occupation by occupation and find that it accounts for about 45% of the earnings decline after job loss. The mechanism is the dark twin of reinstatement: the same technology that creates new tasks in your old occupation now requires new skills you don't have, so you slide down to a job where your existing skills still pay — for less. The new work and the worker's loss are the same event seen from two sides.

So: incumbents bear large, persistent, sometimes life-shortening losses. This is about as well established as anything in labor economics. Top of the confidence ladder.

I do want to keep one honest caveat in view, because it cuts against melodrama. When Edin and coauthors (2023) follow Swedish workers whose entire occupations declined over 1986–2013 — a slow squeeze rather than a sudden layoff — the average worker lost only 2 to 5% of cumulative career earnings, because people saw it coming and adjusted. But even there, the workers who started at the bottom of their occupation lost 8 to 11%. Whether the shock is fast or slow, the loss lands hardest on those least able to absorb it.

Objection! Sometimes the work shrinks too

If automation only ever reshuffled jobs, this would be a tidy story. It doesn't always. The most influential recent paper on the subject reaches a gloomier conclusion than everything above, and intellectual honesty requires meeting it head-on.

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020) study US commuting zones exposed to industrial robots from 1990 to 2007 and find robust negative effects on aggregate local employment and wages: one more robot per thousand workers lowers the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and average wages by about 0.42%. No reassuring offset — just fewer jobs and lower pay where the robots landed. A review of the long US employment slide ranks robots and Chinese import competition as the two leading demand-side culprits (Abraham and Kearney 2020). So the aggregate cushion is not guaranteed.

How do I square that with the operator and ATM stories? Two ways, and they're the reason for the "(mostly)."

First, geography hides the offset, or its absence. A commuting-zone study can only see the jobs lost here; if the reinstatement happens in another city or another industry, the local estimate looks devastating even if the national picture is neutral. That's a real limitation of the robots design — but it's also a real fact about workers' lives, who live in places, not in the national aggregate.

Second, and more telling, institutions decide who pays. Take the same technology — industrial robots — to Germany and you get a strikingly different result. Dauth, Findeisen, Suedekum, and Woessner (2021) find that German robots destroyed manufacturing jobs that were fully offset by new jobs in services — no aggregate employment loss. But the incidence flipped: German incumbents were protected — kept on, retrained, moved to new tasks inside their own plants, often into better jobs — while the cost fell on young workers who never got the manufacturing entry jobs their parents had. Same machine, same "no net job loss," opposite victims.

Which tells you that the deep regularity isn't really "incumbents specifically lose." It's that the cost concentrates on a particular, identifiable group of exposed workers — incumbents in the American cases, entrants in the German one, and in Battisti, Dustmann, and Schoenberg's (2023) German study, older workers specifically, who exit the workforce while their firms retrain everyone younger. The group changes with the labor-market institutions. What doesn't change is that automation's pain is sharp and local to some people, never the diffuse, everyone-a-little society-wide unemployment that the word "technological unemployment" conjures.

And the trade contrast from earlier (Autor, Dorn, and Hanson 2015; Pierce and Schott 2016) is the exception that proves the rule: when the China shock hit, employment fell and stayed fallen, because — unlike a productivity-raising machine — an import doesn't make the local economy more productive or cheaper to produce in. It just removes the demand. Automation usually carries its own partial antidote (cheaper output, higher productivity, complementary new tasks); a trade shock often doesn't. That's why I'm comfortable saying automation comes for workers, not for work — mostly, with robots-in-a-place as the standing exception.

A back-of-the-envelope, to size the stakes

Put numbers on it. Roughly 6 million Americans work today as cashiers or customer-service representatives — about the same share of the workforce that telephone operators once were, and squarely in the path of the current wave of AI. Suppose AI does to them what dial switching did to operators. The historical pattern says total employment needn't fall: the next cohort finds other work, maybe work we can't yet name. But "no change in the aggregate" would still sit on top of millions of individual incumbents, each facing some slice of the displaced-worker penalty — for the high-tenure among them, on the order of a 25% earnings cut that takes years to heal, and for some, measurably worse health. A statistic that reads as "employment unchanged" can contain an enormous amount of concentrated, invisible private pain. That is not an argument against automation. It is an argument about who we let it land on.

What's different about AI (here I'm speculating)

Everything above is history and identified causal evidence. The following is more speculative than what came before, but the question is unavoidable, so bear with me.

The reassuring half of my claim rests on reinstatement keeping pace with displacement. Two of the best papers on new work quietly warn that the engine may be running slower than it used to. Autor, Chin, Salomons, and Seegmiller find that the demand-eroding force of automation has intensified over the last four decades while the demand-increasing force of augmentation has not. Acemoglu and Restrepo's own decomposition attributes sluggish recent employment growth to exactly this: faster displacement, weaker reinstatement, and slower productivity growth. If AI is general enough to automate the new tasks about as fast as they appear — to come for the database manager and the analyst, not just the filing — then the historical offset thins, and we drift toward the Acemoglu–Restrepo pole where the aggregate, not just the incumbent, takes the hit.

Speed and breadth are the variables to watch. Telephone switching took roughly sixty years to diffuse across the whole network; the operators had time, and the economy had time. If AI diffuses in single-digit years and across many occupations at once, then far more people are "incumbents" simultaneously and the adjustment windows that saved past generations slam shut. I don't think the historical record licenses confident doom — but it doesn't license confident calm either. It licenses attention to the incidence.

Bottom line

The evidence, read as a whole, supports a claim that is more precise and more useful than either "robots will take all the jobs" or "don't worry, they never do." When a technology automates an occupation, the work tends to reorganize so that the total number of jobs holds up and the next generation finds employment — the best evidence says so, with industrial robots in exposed places as a genuine exception. But the people who held the automated jobs pay a large, durable, occasionally devastating price — this part is not in serious doubt. Automation's labor problem is not mainly a scarcity of work. It is the concentrated incidence of adjustment on identifiable workers — and which workers depends on the institutions we build around them.

If that's right, the policy conversation is aimed slightly wrong when it frames automation as a war over the number of jobs to be defended or surrendered. The fight that the evidence actually picks out is about insurance and transition for the exposed — and our safety net for displaced workers is, by most accounts, thin. Beraja and Zorzi (2024) go further and argue that because displaced workers reallocate slowly and can't borrow against their futures, unfettered automation is actually too fast relative to what's efficient, and a more gradual path with better insurance would make almost everyone better off. You don't have to buy the full model to take the point: if the harm is concentrated and the gains are diffuse, the humane and the efficient move is the same — compensate and re-skill the people in the path, rather than trying to stop the machine to save jobs the economy will mostly regrow.

What we don't know

  • Is reinstatement still fast enough? The single biggest uncertainty. If new-work creation keeps decelerating relative to displacement, the aggregate-employment reassurance weakens, and AI is the obvious stress test. I'd change my mind toward the pessimists if the next decade shows employment failing to recover in broad swaths of the economy, not just in particular robot-exposed towns.
  • Does the historical analogy hold for something as general as AI? Every prior wave automated a bounded set of tasks. A technology that can also do the new tasks would break the pattern. We don't yet know how general the current one is.
  • Can we actually protect incumbents without freezing the labor market? Germany seems to shield incumbents — but by shifting the cost onto young entrants. Whether you can cushion the displaced without simply relocating the pain to someone else is an open institutional question.
  • What's the right unit of analysis? Much depends on whether you look at the worker, the firm, the city, or the nation — the robots-versus-Germany contrast is partly a story about which lens you pick. Better evidence would follow the same workers across a broad automation shock, the way Feigenbaum and Gross did for operators, but for something happening now.

The honest summary is the one the telephone operators could have told us, if we'd asked. The calls still got connected. The country didn't run out of jobs for women. And a particular set of women, who were good at a thing the machine could suddenly do, spent the next decade falling down the ladder while everyone reassured them that, in the aggregate, everything was fine.

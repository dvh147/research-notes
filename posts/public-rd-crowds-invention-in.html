There is a reflex, common among economists and almost universal among people who distrust government, that goes like this: every research dollar Washington spends is a research dollar some company won't. The state hires the scientists, rents the labs, and books the patents that private firms would otherwise have funded themselves. On this view public R&D is, at best, a wash — it relabels private effort as public effort — and at worst it actively crowds out the market. ("Crowding out" is the economist's term for public spending that displaces private spending roughly one-for-one, leaving total activity unchanged.)

It's a clean story, and for a long time the data couldn't decisively refute it. But there's a natural experiment sitting in plain sight. During World War II the United States stood up the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD), put the engineer Vannevar Bush in charge reporting straight to Roosevelt, and signed more than 2,200 R&D contracts — spending, in today's money, something like $7.4 billion, one to two orders of magnitude more than the federal government had ever put into science. Radar, the proximity fuze, mass-produced penicillin. If public money mostly crowds out private invention, the postwar private innovation system should have looked roughly as it would have anyway. It did not.

This post weighs a single claim: mission-driven public R&D — wartime crash programs and defense or public research — tends to crowd private invention in, not out, and the knowledge it produces spills into private patenting well beyond the labs that got the checks. I read the modern evidence as fairly convincing on the sign and genuinely uncertain on the size — and there's one honest qualifier, about the scarcity of scientists, that keeps the word "mostly" in the title.

Why you'd expect crowding out

Start by steelmanning the pessimist, because the pessimist has real arguments.

The first is about a fixed pie of talent. Most of an R&D budget is not equipment; it's salaries for a relatively small, slowly-grown population of scientists and engineers. If that population can't expand quickly, then a surge of government demand for researchers doesn't conjure new researchers — it just bids up the price of the existing ones. Austan Goolsbee made this point sharply in a 1998 paper using wage data on scientific personnel: government R&D spending raises the wages of scientists and engineers — especially in defense-linked fields like physics and aeronautical engineering — even at firms that get no federal money. His estimate is that because so much of the new spending leaks into higher salaries rather than more actual research, conventional measures of how much R&D policy "works" may be 30 to 50% too high. That's real crowd-out, operating not through accounting substitution but through the price of scarce talent.

The second argument is about plain substitution. Hand a firm a grant for research it was going to do anyway, and it pockets the cash and cuts its own R&D budget by the same amount. The most cited evidence here is Scott Wallsten's study of the SBIR program (Small Business Innovation Research, the U.S. government's flagship small-firm R&D grant scheme). Looking at firms that won SBIR awards, Wallsten found the grants crowded out company-financed R&D roughly dollar for dollar: a dollar of subsidy in, about a dollar of own-spending out, with no detectable bump in employment.

And the third, most honest argument is that for a long time the evidence simply wouldn't settle. In 2000, Paul David, Bronwyn Hall, and Andrew Toole reviewed the entire econometric literature on whether public R&D complements or substitutes for private R&D and came away openly ambivalent: the studies pointed in different directions, the identification was weak, and no honest reader could declare a winner. So if you held the crowding-out prior in 2000, you were not being unreasonable. You were reading the literature correctly.

What changed is not the ideology. It's the identification.

The modern verdict: crowd-in

The newer papers share a trick. Instead of comparing industries that happened to get more public R&D — which might just be the industries with the brightest private prospects anyway — they find variation in public funding driven by something unrelated to the industry's own outlook, usually swings in the national defense budget.

The flagship is Enrico Moretti, Claudia Steinwender, and John Van Reenen's The Intellectual Spoils of War?, published in the Review of Economics and Statistics in 2025. They build a panel of R&D spending by industry and country across the OECD, plus firm-level data for France, and instrument each industry's government-funded R&D with its initial exposure to aggregate defense-budget shocks. The result is the opposite of crowding out: a 10% increase in government-financed R&D generates about 4.3% more privately-financed R&D in the same industry or firm. The induced private R&D then shows up as higher productivity. And the effect crosses borders — more defense R&D in an industry in one country raises private R&D in the same industry elsewhere.

Here's the accounting in human terms. Take an industry doing $1 billion of private R&D, sitting next to $500 million of government defense R&D. Bump the public side by 10% — an extra $50 million of state money. The naive crowd-out story says firms quietly trim their own budgets by roughly $50 million, and total research barely moves. Moretti and coauthors instead find private R&D rising 4.3%, or about $43 million. So $50 million of public money doesn't displace private money — it pulls in almost as much again. (Compare Wallsten's SBIR case, where the $1 grant produced about –$1 of own spending. Same sign of intervention, opposite sign of response. Hold that contrast; it matters later.)

One study, however well-identified, is one study. What gives me more confidence is that independent designs land on the same sign. Gianluca Pallante, Emanuele Russo, and Andrea Roventini did the analogous exercise across U.S. states in 2023, instrumenting each state's military R&D with its differential exposure to national military-R&D shocks, and again found crowding in — though notably smaller, with elasticities around 0.11 to 0.14 rather than Moretti's 0.43. And reaching beyond defense entirely, Antonin Bergeaud and coauthors studied a multi-billion-euro French program that funded academic research clusters and found that each euro of academic research spending spurred about €0.81 of additional private R&D nearby, while raising the quality of local patents.

Three different countries, three different levels of aggregation, three different instruments, one consistent sign. The size of the crowd-in is genuinely unsettled — somewhere between "modest" and "nearly one-for-one" — but the direction is no longer the coin-flip it was in 2000. The best recent evidence fairly convincingly finds crowd-in, not crowd-out.

The natural experiment: World War II

Elasticities are abstract. The WWII episode makes the same point you can almost see.

Daniel Gross and Bhaven Sampat reconstructed the OSRD's contracts and traced what happened to the technologies and places it funded, in America, Jump-Started (American Economic Review, 2023). Comparing heavily-funded technology fields and locations to less-funded ones, they find the wartime push raised patenting 40 to 50% in the most-funded clusters by 1970 — a quarter-century after the war ended. The effect wasn't a blip that faded when the contracts stopped; it compounded. Funded places became durable technology clusters, with more high-tech entrepreneurship and employment: in communications and electronics manufacturing, a doubling of a location's 1940s OSRD patents is associated with 60 to 65% higher employment there in the 1970s.

Two things about this are worth dwelling on. First, the funding was overwhelmingly public and mission-driven — win the war — and yet what it left behind was a thriving private innovation ecosystem, exactly the thing the crowd-out story says public money displaces. Second, the war didn't just raise the level of American invention; it bent its direction, tilting the whole postwar trajectory toward the fields OSRD had bet on. A temporary push produced a permanent reorientation. (If that mechanism — temporary policy, permanent redirection — sounds familiar, it's the same path-dependence logic that shows up in directed technical change.)

This is one historical mega-episode under unrepeatable conditions, so I'd put it at the best evidence strongly suggests rather than settled. But it's hard to look at the postwar takeoff and maintain that the government merely relabeled research the private sector would have done on its own.

Why it spills beyond the funded lab

The claim has a second half: the invention crowds in beyond the firms that got the money. This is where the mechanism becomes clear — and it's the reason public research is special.

Knowledge is leaky. When a publicly-funded lab discovers something, the discovery becomes an input other people can build on, whether or not they paid for it. The cleanest evidence comes from Pierre Azoulay, Joshua Graff Zivin, Danielle Li, and Bhaven Sampat's study of NIH funding (Review of Economic Studies, 2019). They exploit quirks in the NIH's peer-review funding rules to get quasi-random variation in how much money flows to each research area, then link grants to downstream patents. The headline: a $10 million increase in NIH funding produces about 2.7 additional patents — in the private sector. Those patents are filed by firms, not by the funded academics. Public dollars in one place, private invention in another. And crucially, the extra biomedical patenting did not come at the expense of private research in other areas — no crowd-out hiding in the corner.

That pattern generalizes. Andrew Toole showed that public basic biomedical research feeds industrial pharmaceutical innovation; Bergeaud and coauthors traced the French academic-to-firm spillover through concrete channels — scientists moving from labs to companies, new firms forming, public-private partnerships. The picture is consistent: public research seeds private invention through people and ideas that walk out the door.

Why does this make public money worth spending even when private firms could do research themselves? Because the private firm only captures a slice of what its research teaches everyone else. Nicholas Bloom, Mark Schankerman, and John Van Reenen estimated that the social returns to R&D run to roughly twice the private returns — a wedge that means even well-funded private R&D is under-supplied from society's point of view. (That gap between private and social value is the whole reason innovation gets subsidized.) Ufuk Akcigit, Douglas Hanley, and Nicolas Serrano-Velarde push this further: in their model and French data, basic research throws off the largest spillovers of all, and the public funding of basic research — precisely the kind of work private firms skimp on — is significantly welfare-improving. Mission and basic research aren't competing with private R&D for the same niche. They're filling the niche private R&D leaves empty.

It's not a free lunch

Now the honest qualifier, because the crowd-in story has limits and the pessimists were not simply wrong.

Return to Goolsbee's scarce scientists. If the supply of researchers is fixed, then in the short run a chunk of any new public R&D dollar is just a raise for people who already had jobs — and that mutes how much real new research the dollar buys, and can genuinely crowd out private inventive activity through higher wage bills. The crowd-in estimates above are about R&D spending and output; some of the spending is price, not quantity. The reconciliation, I think, is time horizon: the WWII effort didn't just hire the existing scientists, it trained a generation of new ones and built the university research capacity that supplied them — exactly the margin Goolsbee's short-run wage story holds fixed. So the deepest crowd-out worry isn't a reason to expect displacement in the long run; it's a reason to expect the short-run bang-per-buck to be smaller than the dollars suggest, and to care a lot about whether a program also expands the talent pipeline.

Then there's program design, which is the cleanest way to reconcile the conflicting studies. Wallsten's SBIR result — crowd-out, dollar for dollar — is real, and it's about handing established firms cash for research close to what they'd already do. That is a very different instrument from funding a wartime laboratory or an NIH research program whose output diffuses to everyone. Tellingly, when Sabrina Howell and coauthors studied reforms that opened up U.S. defense research to a broader set of firms, they found real causal effects on innovation — design changed the outcome. The lesson isn't "public R&D always crowds in." It's that mission and spillover-heavy research crowds in, while cash subsidies for incremental corporate R&D can crowd out. The claim in this post is deliberately about the former.

So the strongest objection — "you're just measuring relabeled spending and salary inflation" — lands a partial blow. It dents the magnitude and warns against the wrong instruments. It does not reverse the sign for mission-driven research, which is what the well-identified modern designs and the WWII experiment are picking up.

Bottom line

I come down on the side of the claim, with bounded confidence. For mission-driven public R&D — defense research, wartime crash programs, public and basic research with broad reach — the better-identified modern evidence fairly convincingly finds that it crowds private invention in rather than out: three independent causal designs agree on the sign, a dramatic natural experiment shows a temporary push permanently lifting and redirecting private invention, and two clean studies show the knowledge spilling into patents filed by firms that never got a grant. The reason this isn't a wash is that public research occupies the high-spillover niche — basic, mission-oriented, broadly diffusing — that private firms systematically under-fund.

The two things I'd insist on bounding: size and scope. The magnitude of crowd-in ranges from modest (a 0.11 state-level elasticity) to nearly one-for-one (Moretti's 0.43, Bergeaud's €0.81 per euro), and part of any short-run dollar is just higher wages for scarce scientists rather than more research. And the result is about mission and public research, not a blank check for every subsidy with "R&D" in its name — hand firms cash for work they'd do anyway and you can get Wallsten's dollar-for-dollar crowd-out instead. The sign is fairly solid; the size and the design are where the real policy action is.

The one-sentence version: the instinct that government research just displaces private research has the sign backwards for the kind of research only governments tend to fund — but it's a useful warning about how to fund it.

What we don't know

  • How big is the crowd-in, really? The estimates span a factor of three or four. Until we understand why U.S. states (0.11–0.14) look so much weaker than the cross-country firm estimates (0.43), the right number for any given policy is a guess within a wide band.
  • How much is quantity, how much is price? Goolsbee's wage channel means a chunk of measured crowd-in could be salary inflation. The studies that separate real research from researcher pay — by tracking the supply response of scientists over time — are the ones that would pin this down.
  • Does it generalize past defense and the United States? The sharpest designs lean on U.S. defense-budget swings. Whether civilian mission programs (energy, climate, health) in other institutional settings crowd in as reliably is far less established.
  • What exactly makes a program crowd in versus out? "Mission and spillovers" versus "incremental corporate subsidy" is the right intuition, but we lack a clean taxonomy that would let a funder predict, before spending, which side of the line a new program falls on.

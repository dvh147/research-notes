Picture two assistant professors of economics coming up for tenure. On paper their files look almost identical: the same number of papers in the same journals, similar citation counts, comparable teaching. The one real difference is how they work. One writes mostly alone; the other writes mostly with coauthors. If both are men, the committee barely registers the difference — solo or joint, the papers count. But if the heavy collaborator is a woman, her odds of getting tenure are visibly lower. Same record, different verdict.

That, more or less, is what Heather Sarsons (2017) found when she went looking in the personnel files of academic economists, and what she, Klarita Gërxhani, Ernesto Reuben and Arthur Schram later confirmed and stress-tested in the Journal of Political Economy (2021). It is an uncomfortable result, and it points at a mechanism that is both more specific and more fixable than the usual stories about the gender gap. So here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence and an honest accounting of where it breaks: for the same collaborative work, women get less credit than men — and that credit gap is large enough, at least in economics, to be a real contributor to the gender gap in careers.

Credit is the currency of professional advancement. Tenure, promotion, raises, the next good project — all of them are paid out in proportion to who is judged to have done the work. If that judgment is biased by gender whenever the work is shared, then a world that increasingly runs on teams will tilt against women for reasons that have nothing to do with what they actually contribute. That is the stake. Let me walk through the evidence, then spend the back half of the post on the two objections that genuinely threaten the claim.

Coauthoring is nearly free for men, and costly for women

Start with the headline finding, because it is the most surprising thing in this literature. Sarsons assembled tenure outcomes for economists at roughly the top 35 U.S. departments over 1985–2014 — a few hundred people, about a fifth of them women — and asked a simple question: as you add papers to someone's record, how much does each one raise their probability of getting tenure, and does the answer depend on whether the paper was solo or coauthored?

For men, the type of paper barely matters. In the JPE estimates, an additional solo-authored paper is worth about 9.5 percentage points of tenure probability, and an additional coauthored paper about 7.4 — close enough that a man can collaborate freely without paying for it. For women, the two numbers diverge sharply. A solo-authored paper is worth a lot — about 15.5 percentage points, if anything more than it is for a man. But an additional coauthored paper is worth only about 4.7 points. So it isn't that women's work counts for less in general; their solo work counts for at least as much as men's. It is specifically joint work that gets discounted.

The discount has a revealing structure. When Sarsons splits coauthors by gender, the penalty turns out to be concentrated in papers women write with men: there, the same paper is worth roughly 7.8 points to the man and only about 1.8 to the woman — almost the entire credit goes to him. A woman who coauthors with other women suffers no such penalty (her papers are worth about 10 points each). In other words, when it is ambiguous who did what and one of the names is male, observers resolve the ambiguity in the man's favor.

How much does this matter for the aggregate gap? By Sarsons's accounting, the differential return to coauthoring can explain something like 40% of the raw gender gap in tenure rates in her sample, and a majority of it once you condition on productivity. Here is the back-of-the-envelope that makes the stakes concrete: switching a paper from solo to coauthored costs a man about two points of tenure probability (9.5 → 7.4) but costs a woman about eleven (15.5 → 4.7) — several times as much. Build a tenure case out of several collaborations, as modern economists increasingly must, and that per-paper gap compounds into a real difference in who walks through the door.

Two honest caveats before we lean on these numbers. First, hold the exact magnitudes loosely: this is one sample of a few hundred people, the estimates are imprecise, and the point estimates have drifted across drafts of the paper (the women's-coauthored return has been reported anywhere from near zero to ~4.7 points). Second, the tenure analysis is observational — Sarsons controls for a rich set of productivity measures, but she cannot randomly assign coauthorship, so selection is a live worry. I will come back to that worry, because it is the one the whole claim has to survive.

Is it really credit? Make the contribution visible and the gap disappears

The reason to believe this is about credit attribution and not some artifact is that the same pattern shows up when you strip the confounds away in the lab — and it has a clean on/off switch: ambiguity.

The cleanest natural test is built into the academic conventions themselves. Economists list coauthors alphabetically, which means the author order carries no information about who contributed what; the signal is deliberately switched off. Most other fields order authors by contribution. Sarsons uses sociology — where first authorship signals who did the most — as a comparison case, and the coauthoring penalty for women vanishes there. When the convention tells observers how to assign credit, they assign it correctly; when it doesn't, they fall back on a stereotype. (The same alphabetical norm has other distorting effects: Vakili, Teodoridis and Bikard (2022) show economists whose surnames start near the front of the alphabet capture systematically more credit for joint work, enough to change who chooses to collaborate at all.)

The JPE paper then nails the mechanism down with two experiments that have no selection problem at all. In one, online participants saw a person's output and had to predict their ability; when only a joint score was visible, predicted ability for women on a male-typed (math) task dropped significantly relative to men with identical output — and there was no such gap on a grammar task, so the bias tracks the gender-stereotype of the task, exactly as a credit-attribution story predicts. In a second experiment, nearly 500 real HR recruiters evaluated joint work; male evaluators systematically under-credited women, and controlling for the evaluators' stated beliefs pulled the bias toward zero — so it runs through beliefs, not taste.

This is not a new or isolated finding. The seminal version predates the economics by two decades: the psychologists Madeline Heilman and Michelle Haynes (2005) had observers rate the members of a male–female pair that had jointly succeeded at a male-typed task. Under ambiguity, the woman was rated markedly less competent (about 5.6 versus 7.3 on a nine-point scale), less influential, and less likely to have been the leader — she was simply assumed to have ridden along. And, crucially, the penalty disappeared whenever her individual contribution was made unambiguous: give the raters individual performance feedback, or tell them she had clearly led, and the ratings converged. Ambiguity is the necessary ingredient. Remove it and the bias has nothing to feed on.

So across an observational design, two clean experiments, a cross-discipline comparison, and a thirty-year-old psychology result, the same picture holds: when credit for joint work is ambiguous, it flows toward the man. On this core mechanism the evidence is, by social-science standards, strong and convergent. It is the leap from "this mechanism is real" to "it explains a specific chunk of the tenure gap" that rests on thinner ice.

Two doors to the same place

It would be too simple to say men do all the discounting. The credit gap is fed from two sides at once, and they push in the same direction.

Others discount women. This is the demand side, and the sharpest demonstration is a debiasing experiment by Iris Bohnet, Alexandra van Geen and Max Bazerman (2016). They had evaluators "hire" candidates for a math task based on past performance and gender. When candidates were judged one at a time — the way a tenure case or a promotion usually arrives — evaluators leaned on the stereotype and largely ignored the numbers: 65% chose a lower-performing man over a higher-performing woman. When the very same candidates were judged side by side, attention snapped onto the performance gap, and the bias collapsed: now 57% chose the higher-performing woman, and almost no one chose the weaker man. Same information, different framing, opposite result — and a strong hint that one-at-a-time evaluation is where stereotype does its damage.

Women discount themselves. This is the supply side, and it is just as well documented. Lise Vesterlund, Linda Babcock and coauthors (2017) study what they call "low-promotability tasks" — work that helps the organization but not your own career: serving on the committee, taking the notes, mentoring. In mixed-sex groups, women volunteer for these about 48% more often than men; they are also asked to far more often (39% of the time versus 27%), and tellingly, both men and women do the asking. The gap is not about preferences: in single-sex groups it disappears entirely, which means it is driven by shared beliefs about who should step up. (Their follow-up rules out a tempting alternative story — women are not simply caving to harsher backlash for saying no.) The result is that women spend more of their time on exactly the kind of work that earns the least credit.

Women also claim less credit for the work they do do. Katherine Coffman (2014) had people contribute ideas in a group; in domains stereotyped as male, women withheld correct answers far more often than equally able men — at the top of the ability distribution they failed to put forward a right answer about 14 percentage points more often, nearly twice the male rate — and the gap was driven by confidence, not ability. (Her vivid number: only 16% of women thought they were the best in their group at sports questions, though 55% actually were.) And when people describe their own performance to a potential employer, Christine Exley and Judd Kessler (2022) find women rate themselves about 13 points lower than equally performing men on a 0–100 scale — roughly a quarter below the average — a gap that isn't strategic, isn't about risk, is only partly about beliefs, and shows up as early as the sixth grade.

Put the two doors together and the credit gap looks overdetermined. Observers under-credit women for ambiguous joint work; women, anticipating or internalizing that, claim less and shoulder more of the work that is never credited at all. Either channel alone would tilt the playing field. Both together make the tilt hard to avoid.

Objection! Isn't this just selection?

The most serious objection to the headline result is the observational one. Maybe women who coauthor a lot differ from women who don't, in ways tenure committees can see but the econometrician cannot. Maybe they take on weaker projects, or coauthor because they are struggling. If so, the "penalty" is just the committee correctly reading a signal, not bias.

I take this seriously, but I think it mostly fails, for three reasons. First, the lab experiments have no selection problem by construction — output is held fixed and randomized, and the bias is still there, tracking task-stereotype and running through stated beliefs. Selection cannot explain a result where there is nothing to select on. Second, the cross-discipline comparison is hard to reconcile with a pure-selection story: there is no obvious reason women's coauthorship would signal weakness in economics but not in sociology, whereas the credit-convention difference between the fields predicts exactly the pattern we see. Third, the Babcock result that the volunteering gap vanishes in single-sex groups tells us these dynamics are belief-driven, not preference- or ability-driven. None of this makes the observational tenure estimate airtight — it isn't — but the selection story has to explain away the experiments too, and it can't.

Objection! Don't women actually get more recognition now?

This is the objection that genuinely bounds the claim, and it comes from a heavyweight source. David Card, Stefano DellaVigna, Patricia Funk and Nagore Iriberri (2022) studied gender differences in elite peer recognition among economists — election as a Fellow of the Econometric Society and similar honors — conditioning richly on each candidate's publication and citation record. Historically, they find exactly the discrimination the claim predicts: before 1980, women were far less likely than equally productive men to be made Fellows (a gap big enough to call roughly fourfold) — a textbook "Matilda effect," women's contributions credited to others. But the gap shrank to nothing by the 1980s–90s and then flipped: in the most recent decade, conditional on output, women are more than twice as likely as men to be selected, and the same pro-female pattern holds for other major honors.

It gets more pointed. Card and coauthors look specifically for a Sarsons-style coauthorship penalty in who gets elected — and do not find one. In their words, they do not find that women receive less recognition for coauthored work in fellowship selection.

What do I make of this? Two honest things. First, the historical half of their result strongly supports the claim — the under-crediting of women was real and large for most of the twentieth century. Second, the modern half genuinely bounds it: at the very top, where candidates are intensely scrutinized and institutions have made deliberate efforts to correct the record, the conditional credit gap has not just closed but reversed. So the clean reading is that the credit penalty Sarsons documents lives at the marginal, everyday, ambiguous level — the ordinary tenure case, the routine evaluation, the unread CV line — and does not generalize to elite honors decided under a spotlight. That is a real limit on the claim, and it cuts in an optimistic direction: scrutiny and conscious correction can evidently overpower the bias. The claim that survives is narrower than "women always get less credit." It is "women get less credit for joint work when the attribution is ambiguous and no one is watching closely — which is most of the time."

What would actually fix it

The encouraging thing about a credit-attribution problem is that the lab keeps handing us the off-switch: make the individual contribution legible.

The most direct lever is the credit convention itself. Economics could do what most sciences already do and signal contribution — through contributor statements (who designed, who ran the data, who wrote), or at minimum by treating alphabetical order as the uninformative thing it is rather than reading seniority or leadership into it. The sociology comparison is essentially a natural experiment showing this works: where the convention assigns credit, the penalty disappears. A second lever is how evaluation is structured. Bohnet's result says that judging candidates side by side instead of one at a time forces attention onto performance and erases the stereotype; comparative evaluation is a cheap institutional change with a measured payoff. A third is to stop letting low-promotability work vanish: tracking and rotating service, and rewarding it explicitly, blunts the supply-side channel — and a recent field experiment finds that public social recognition can close much of the volunteering gap.

Here I will step past the cleanest evidence and offer a reading rather than a result: I suspect the single highest-leverage move is simply to reduce ambiguity wherever credit is assigned, because every strand of this literature converges on ambiguity as the thing the bias needs to operate. That is a hopeful diagnosis — it means the fix is a design problem, not a battle over hearts — but I hold it as a hypothesis, since none of these fixes has been tested at the scale of an actual tenure system.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That observers under-credit women for ambiguous joint work is well supported — it holds across clean experiments, a thirty-year-old psychology literature, a cross-discipline comparison, and field behavior, and it has a clear and repeatable on/off switch in ambiguity. That this credit gap meaningfully shapes careers is most sharply shown in economics, where coauthored papers do far less for a woman's tenure than a man's; I believe the qualitative result, while holding the exact magnitudes loosely, because it is one imprecise observational sample even if its own experiments back the mechanism. And the strongest counterweight — Card and coauthors' finding that elite recognition has flipped to favor women conditional on output — keeps me from overclaiming: the penalty is a feature of marginal, everyday, unscrutinized judgment, not an iron law, and concentrated attention can reverse it. So: women get less credit for group work, mostly where it is unclear who did what and no one is looking closely — which, in ordinary professional life, is most of the time. The fix is not to make women collaborate less; it is to make contribution legible.

What we don't know

  • Does it generalize beyond academia? The cleanest career evidence is about economists' tenure. The lab mechanism surely travels, but we have little field measurement of the credit gap inside ordinary firms, where most group work happens.
  • How big is it, really? The tenure magnitude rests on one smallish, imprecise sample and has not been independently replicated. A second clean estimate, in another setting, would move this from "the best study we have" to "established."
  • Supply versus demand. Both channels are real, but their relative weight — how much is others discounting women versus women discounting themselves — is not pinned down, and it matters a lot for which fix to reach for.
  • Do the fixes work at scale? Contributor statements, joint evaluation, and structured recognition all work in the lab or in small field trials. Whether a real tenure or promotion system can adopt them without the bias simply migrating elsewhere is untested.
  • Why did elite recognition flip? Card and coauthors document the reversal but cannot fully say whether it is deliberate correction, changing beliefs, or compositional change — and the answer determines whether the everyday gap will close on its own or has to be engineered shut.

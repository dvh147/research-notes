The recipe sounds almost too obvious to question. Invention is recombination — the telephone is a wire plus a voice, the smartphone is a telephone plus a computer plus a camera — so if you want more and better inventions, build teams that carry more different kinds of knowledge. Sit the chemist next to the materials scientist next to the software engineer, and ideas none of them could have reached alone will fall out of the conversation. Call it the diversity bonus: breadth in, breakthroughs out.

Almost everyone who organizes invention believes some version of this. Firms staff cross-functional teams on the strength of it. Funders increasingly require interdisciplinary teams, on the theory that mixing fields is where the good stuff comes from. It is one of those propositions that feels less like a hypothesis than like common sense. And a lot rides on it being true: if breadth really manufactures invention, then a manager or a program officer can buy more innovation simply by recomposing a roster.

Here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence: the strong correlation between how broad a team's knowledge is and how much it invents is mostly selection, not cause. Abler inventors, better-resourced firms, and more promising projects assemble broader teams — so when you strip that sorting out with something close to random variation, the effect of breadth on the amount a team invents shrinks toward zero. Breadth may still change the kind of invention you get; it just doesn't reliably buy you more of it.

One scoping note before we start. By "breadth" I mean diversity of knowledge — the range of fields, skills, and technical experience on an inventing team. That is a different thing from demographic diversity (gender, nationality, age), which has its own large and contested literature, and I will touch it only where it illuminates the knowledge question. The target here is the specific, recombination-flavored belief that mixing what people know makes a team invent more.

The bonus is wobblier than the slogan

Start with the uncomfortable fact that even the correlational evidence — the easy evidence, the kind that doesn't worry about causation at all — is much softer than the slogan implies.

Management researchers have been running diversity-and-performance studies for decades, and the meta-analyses that pool them tell a deflating story. Horwitz and Horwitz (2007), aggregating the team-demography literature, find that task-related diversity (skills, functions, expertise — exactly our kind) is positively associated with team performance, but that bio-demographic diversity is not significantly related to it at all. So far, so encouraging for the knowledge story. But the averages are small — pooled correlations in this literature typically sit below 0.1 — and so heterogeneous across studies that the average barely describes any particular team.

Then it gets worse for the slogan. van Dijk, van Engen, and van Knippenberg (2012), pooling 146 studies and 612 effect sizes, ask who measured the performance. The conventional wisdom — job-related diversity good, demographic diversity bad — turns out to hold mainly when performance is rated by someone who can see the team. With objective outcome measures, immune to a rater's expectations, the apparent job-related-diversity benefit largely fades. Part of the "bonus," in other words, is in the eye of the beholder: people expect diverse teams to be more creative and grade them accordingly.

And where the relationship does show up, it is rarely the straight line the recipe implies. Hoisl, Gruber, and Conti (2017), studying R&D teams in Formula 1 racing, find an inverted-U: experience diversity helps performance up to a point, then hurts, as communication and coordination costs swamp the recombinant gains. The classic Ancona and Caldwell (1992) study of new-product teams found that functional diversity improved external communication and managers' ratings of innovation — and yet the overall effect of diversity on performance was negative, because the same differences that fed creativity impeded execution. More recent work formalizes the tension as a genuine trade-off: Aggarwal and Woolley (2019) show cognitive-style diversity both helps and hinders a team's shared cognition, and Lix, Goldberg, Srivastava, and Valentine (2022) find that diverse software teams perform well only when they can modulate that diversity to the task. The honest summary of the correlational evidence is not "breadth boosts invention." It is "breadth is weakly, unstably, and conditionally related to performance, partly in ways that depend on who's holding the clipboard."

Who builds a broad team?

Even taken at face value, none of that correlational evidence can tell us that breadth causes invention — because teams are not assembled by a coin flip.

Think about who ends up on a broad team. A star inventor attracts collaborators from many fields and is invited onto ambitious, multi-disciplinary projects. A well-funded lab at a deep-pocketed firm can afford to bring in a specialist the small lab does without. A project that management already believes is promising gets staffed generously and broadly; the routine project gets two people who already sit next to each other. In every one of these stories, breadth is a symptom of something — talent, resources, expected payoff — that independently drives invention. The broad team invents more, but breadth didn't do it.

This is not a hypothetical worry; you can watch it happen in the data when someone gets the identification right. Lee (2010) studies one of the most celebrated correlations in the field — that inventors who occupy "brokerage" positions, bridging otherwise disconnected groups, are more innovative. He shows first that inventors with strong track records are the ones who come to occupy those bridging positions. Then he adds inventor fixed effects — in effect, comparing the same inventor to himself across positions — and the position-performance correlation disappears. The network position wasn't making people inventive; inventive people were ending up in the good positions.

A 2025 paper pushes the same knife in further. Hernandez, Lee, and Shaver (2025) want a cleaner test of whether a firm's alliance-network position causes its innovation. Their trick is to separate network change the firm chose (self-driven) from network change forced on it by others' decisions (other-driven, and so closer to exogenous). Structural holes — the bridging positions network theory prizes — raise firm innovation when the firm engineered them, but not when they arose from other firms' moves. The authors are admirably blunt about what this implies: either network position "spuriously captures unobservable firm characteristics," or our theories have to be rewritten to make the firm's own agency the active ingredient. Either way, the part of the "network effect" that looks like the position itself doing work largely evaporates once you take the firm's choices out of it.

Brokerage and breadth are not identical, but they rhyme: both are features of a team or an inventor that abler, better-resourced actors select into. And when two independent designs strip the selection away, the celebrated structural advantage mostly goes with it.

Kill the selection, and the bonus mostly dies

The cleanest way to settle this would be to reach into firms and randomly make some inventing teams broader than others. Nobody can do that. But death can.

Verhoeven (2021), in the paper that seeds this post, uses the unexpected death of an inventor's collaborator as a source of quasi-random variation in the breadth of the surviving team — losing a teammate from a distant field narrows your knowledge base in a way you didn't choose and couldn't have anticipated. The headline is a null: there is no evidence that broader teams go on to invent more. The familiar positive relationship between breadth and invention quality, he shows, is not robust to accounting for the sorting of inventors into broad teams — positive selection in particular. The provocative reading, which I think the design earns: the diversity bonus that managers and funders are chasing is, to a first approximation, the shadow cast by who assembles broad teams, not a return to breadth itself.

A second death-based study, with a different question, lands in a compatible place. Pöge, Gaessler, Hoisl, Harhoff, and Dorner (2025) use 845 unexpected inventor deaths to ask what losing a collaborator does to the people left behind. Losing a high-relevance external collaborator hurts. But losing an internal one — a teammate inside the same firm — has an effect on the survivor's inventive productivity that is, in their words, virtually zero, because the organization quietly fills the gap with its hiring and knowledge-management machinery. Read for our purposes, that is a striking fact about fungibility: inside a firm, the specific bundle of knowledge a teammate brought to the team is replaceable enough that losing it barely dents output. That is hard to square with a world where each increment of team breadth is an irreplaceable engine of invention.

A toy version makes the logic legible. Picture two five-person teams. Team A is broad — five different specialties — and files ten patents a year. Team B is narrow — five people from one field — and files five. The naive inference is that breadth doubled output. But suppose Team A is broad because it formed around a star who draws talent from everywhere, and Team B is the leftovers. Now a coordinating member of each team happens to die. If breadth were the engine, Team A should fall much further — it just lost a chunk of its prized diversity. In the death-design studies, that extra fall doesn't show up: the broad team's advantage was riding on the star and the project, not on the breadth, so knocking the breadth around leaves output roughly where selection put it. The gap between "ten versus five" and "the change you see when breadth moves exogenously" is the selection effect, laid bare.

Objection! Maybe breadth changes the kind, not the amount

I have been pushing hard, so let me state the strongest case against the null — because it is real, and it keeps the claim honest.

First, not every clean design finds a zero. Genin, Ma, Bhagwat, and Bernile (2023) study board-level experiential diversity and, using instrumental variables built from exogenous variation in the local supply of diverse directors — backed up with difference-in-differences — find that it raises both the quantity and the quality of a firm's path-breaking patents. This is a credible causal design, and it points the other way. Note what it is, though: an effect on radical innovation, at the level of the board, not an effect on the routine rate at which a bench of inventors produces patents. It suggests breadth can matter causally — for the unusual, direction-setting stuff.

That points to the real qualification. Breadth may change the kind of invention a team produces even if it doesn't change the amount. Singh and Fleming (2010), looking at half a million patents, find that collaboration and experience diversity mostly reshape the tails of the outcome distribution — trimming the worst outcomes more than they fatten the best — rather than shifting the average up. Wu, Wang, and Evans (2019), across 65 million papers, patents, and software products, show that small teams disrupt science and technology while large teams develop it: team structure changes the type of work, not merely the count. And Vakili and Kaplan (2021) find that the team configuration that maximizes breakthroughs — broad versus deep — depends on the technology, so there is no single recipe to begin with. The picture that survives is not "breadth is inert." It is "breadth reshuffles the distribution and the character of what you invent, which is a subtler and more contingent thing than a tap that pours out more patents."

Two distinctions keep this from collapsing into other claims. Breadth is not the same as size: teams grew over the twentieth century largely because the burden of knowledge (Jones 2009) forced specialists to band together, and team size is a separate lever with its own effects. And breadth is not the same as a particular valued tie: losing one irreplaceable collaborator is a real and lasting blow (the subject of an earlier post), but that is about the value of a specific relationship, not about whether generic breadth causes output. The selection critique bites on the third thing — the idea that more different kinds of knowledge, in general, mechanically yields more invention.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That the cross-sectional diversity bonus is heavily inflated by selection is fairly convincing: the correlational base is itself small, heterogeneous, and partly an artifact of who rates the work; and four independent attempts to remove the sorting — inventor fixed effects, the self-versus-other-driven split, and two inventor-death designs — all push the apparent benefit toward zero. If you are a manager or a funder hoping to buy more invention by bolting breadth onto a team, the honest expected value of that move is much smaller than the raw correlation advertises, and plausibly near nothing for the sheer quantity of output. The bigger lever, the evidence suggests, is getting good people and good projects — the very things that produce broad teams — not the breadth per se.

Now the bound, because I don't want to oversell a null any more than I'd want to oversell the bonus. That the true causal effect is exactly zero is a stronger statement than the evidence supports. The death designs identify a specific margin (what happens when you perturb an existing team), they rest on relatively small numbers of tragic events, and they live mostly in the world of patenting inventors. At least one clean design finds a real positive effect for radical innovation, and a good deal of evidence says breadth changes the kind of invention even where it doesn't change the amount. So my confidence ladder runs fairly high on "the bonus is mostly selection," moderate on "the causal effect on output rate is small," and deliberately humble on "breadth doesn't matter at all" — which I think is false. Broader teams don't invent more. But they are where the good inventors already are, and that is a different and more useful thing to know.

What we don't know

  • The missing experiment. Nobody has randomly assigned breadth to inventing teams and measured output against a control. The death designs are the closest we have, and they identify one particular margin on small samples.
  • Which breadth, which outcome? Skill breadth, field breadth, cognitive style, and demographic diversity are different things, and "amount," "novelty," and "radicalness" are different outcomes. The map from each kind of breadth to each kind of outcome is mostly blank.
  • What exactly is doing the sorting? Selection is a label, not a mechanism. How much of it is inventor ability, how much firm resources, how much project quality — and whether you could intervene on any of them — is unresolved.
  • Does the "kind, not amount" effect generalize and can you manufacture it? The Genin and Wu results suggest breadth shapes radical/disruptive work; whether that is robust, and whether a manager can deliberately engineer it rather than merely select for it, is open.
  • Outside patents. Almost all of this evidence is about inventors and patents. Whether the same null holds for teams writing software, designing drugs, or running labs is largely untested.

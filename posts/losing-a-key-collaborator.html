Sometime in the past few decades, an academic superstar died suddenly — mid-career, at the height of their powers, with no warning. It was a private tragedy. It was also, for a certain kind of economist, a natural experiment, and a small, careful literature has grown up around the obituaries. The question these papers ask is cold but revealing: when a scientist dies, what happens to the productivity of the people who used to work with them?

You might guess "nothing much." The collaborators are themselves accomplished people; they have their own labs, their own ideas, their own hands. The dead colleague was an input, sad to lose, but surely the survivors carry on at roughly their own level. That is what a standard theory of human capital predicts, because in that theory your productivity is a property of you — your training, your talent, your accumulated skill — which you carry from job to job and partner to partner.

That prediction is wrong. In the foundational study, Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang (2010) tracked the coauthors of 112 life-science "superstars" who died prematurely and unexpectedly, and found that those collaborators suffered a lasting 5% to 8% decline in their quality-adjusted publication rates. Not a dip and a recovery — a step down that didn't come back. Some of what looked like each collaborator's own productivity turned out to be tied up in a relationship, and it did not survive the relationship.

This post is about that finding and the literature it launched. The one claim I want to defend is this: a meaningful share of your measured productivity isn't really yours. It's team-specific — it lives in particular collaborations, not inside you — and when you lose a key collaborator, part of your output goes with them and doesn't fully come back. What carries the loss, as we'll see, is the collaborative tie in idea-space, not the office down the hall or the prestige of your department.

Why you should care that productivity is partly relational

If this is right, we are systematically mismeasuring talent, and a lot of consequential decisions rest on the mismeasurement.

Start with the fact that knowledge work is now overwhelmingly team work. In a landmark count of 19.9 million papers and 2.1 million patents, Wuchty, Jones, and Uzzi (2007) showed that teams have come to dominate the production of knowledge across nearly every field: the share of social-science papers written by teams rose from 17.5% in 1955 to 51.5% in 2000, and team-authored work is both more common and more highly cited than solo work, increasingly so over time. The lone genius was always partly a myth; today it's a rounding error.

So when we credit a CV, value a star hire, hand out tenure, or rank inventors by their patents, we are reading individual ledgers for output that was substantially co-produced. If a chunk of a person's record is really the record of their partnerships, then the "war for talent" is partly a war for talent that won't transfer, retention math is off, and our instinct to treat a productivity number as a portable personal attribute is a category error. The stakes here aren't only academic — they're in every organization that hires, funds, loses, or bets on key people.

Let me build the case in four steps: the experiment itself; what exactly gets lost; why; and the strongest objection — that losing a star might actually be good.

The experiment nobody wants to run

The beauty and the horror of the death design is that premature, unexpected death is about as close to random as social science gets. A star who chooses to stop collaborating with you might be doing so because your work is fading; a star who dies in a car accident is not. That cleanly isolates the effect of losing the collaboration from everything that's usually tangled up with it.

Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang ran it for academic scientists and got the 5–8% lasting decline. The natural worry is that life-science superstars are exotic — maybe this is a quirk of how labs and grants work, not a general fact about collaboration. So it matters a great deal that the result replicates in a completely different world, with completely different data.

Jaravel, Petkova, and Bell (2018) take the entire population of U.S. patent inventors from 1996 to 2012, linked to administrative tax records, and ask what happens to an inventor when one of their co-inventors dies prematurely. The surviving co-inventor's output falls — hard and durably. Eight years after the death, their citation-weighted patents are down about 15% and their earnings are down about 4%, with no sign of bouncing back. Different country of the mind entirely — corporate and independent inventors, not academic biologists; patents and dollars, not publications — and the same shape of result: lose a collaborator, lose a piece of yourself you can't replace.

Two clean natural experiments, in two unrelated domains, pointing the same way. By the standards of this kind of design, this part of the claim is about as well established as it gets — top of the confidence ladder. Losing a close collaborator causes a lasting, only-partly-recovered decline in the survivor's productivity. That much I'd bet on.

It's the collaboration, not the corridor

Here's where it gets interesting, because the obvious explanation is wrong.

The obvious story is proximity: you lose a brilliant person from your environment, the ambient brilliance drops, and you do worse. If that were it, then losing any high-quality colleague — collaborator or not — should hurt. It doesn't.

The cleanest test comes from a grim corner of history. When the Nazi government dismissed scientists in 1933, it ripped high-quality people out of German university departments for political and ethnic reasons that had nothing to do with the productivity of those who remained. Waldinger (2012) built a panel of the universe of scientists at German universities from 1925 to 1938 and used these dismissals as an exogenous shock to the local peer group. The result is a clean null: he finds no evidence of local peer effects. Even losing very high-quality departmental colleagues did not lower the productivity of the scientists who stayed.

Put Waldinger's null next to the death results and the boundary of the claim snaps into focus. Losing a brilliant person who happens to share your building: no measurable effect. Losing a brilliant person you actually write papers or patents with: a lasting 5–15% hit. It is not about being near smart people. It is about the collaborations themselves. The comforting "great department" story — surround yourself with stars and you'll rise — gets little support here. The right unit is the working relationship, not the org chart.

Azoulay and coauthors saw the same thing inside their own data. The decline after a superstar's death, they report, is "circumscribed in idea space, but less so in physical or social space" — meaning the collaborators who suffered most were the ones intellectually closest to the star's line of work, not the ones who merely sat nearby or socialized with them. The loss travels along ideas, not hallways. They call what the death reveals the boundary of the star's "invisible college": the real, working intellectual community that a productive scientist anchors, which is invisible precisely because we usually only see the individuals, never the ties.

One honest caveat, because peer effects clearly do exist in some settings. Moretti (2021) shows that inventors who move into a big same-field cluster — Silicon Valley for software, say — get a sizeable, lasting boost to the number and quality of their patents. That's a real agglomeration effect and it's not nothing. But notice it's consistent with the idea-space story: a dense cluster is a dense supply of potential collaborators and intellectual exchange, not just warm bodies in expensive zip codes. Proximity matters when it converts into collaboration. When it doesn't — Waldinger's departmental peers — it appears to wash out.

What, exactly, are you losing?

So a collaboration holds something valuable that dies with it. Economists have a name for the thing — team-specific capital — and the literature has gotten fairly specific about what it's made of.

A toy version makes the accounting vivid. Imagine two inventors, A and B, who together produce 10 citation-weighted patents a year. The naïve ledger credits 10 to A and 10 to B, as if each were a self-contained engine. Now B dies, and A's output falls 15%, to about 8.5. That missing 1.5 was never really A's alone; it was jointly owned by the A–B partnership, and it was non-portable — it couldn't be packed up and carried to A's next project. Run a career forward with three or four such key partnerships and a large slice of "A's record" is, on inspection, the record of A's collaborations. The individual ledger was double-counting all along.

Where does this joint capital come from? Jaravel, Petkova, and Bell go looking and rule out the easy answers. It isn't mainly firm disruption (the effect shows up even holding the workplace fixed), it isn't the loss of the rest of the dead inventor's network, and it isn't simple top-down mentoring from a senior star to a junior. What's left, and what the data point to, is an "experience" component: the value of a collaboration grows the longer two people work together, and the damage is concentrated in close-knit teams. You build something with a particular partner — a shared shorthand, a division of cognitive labor, a sense of what the other will catch — and it is worth more in year eight than year one. That's the asset that vanishes.

What kind of help is it, concretely? Oettl (2012) found a clue by separating scientists along two axes — how productive they are and how helpful they are (measured through the acknowledgments they rack up for advice and feedback). When a helpful star dies, their coauthors' work gets measurably worse in quality — about 14% to 16% — while when a merely productive but unhelpful star dies, their coauthors are essentially unaffected. And the help that matters most isn't lending equipment or sharing data; it's conceptual feedback — critique and advice. That's a strong hint that what you lose is the person who made your ideas better, not a famous name on your papers or a key to the supply closet.

Network position matters too, in a way that sharpens the same point. Mohnen (2022) shows that when the star you lose was your broker — the coauthor who connected you to non-redundant knowledge elsewhere in the network — the damage is larger: a one-standard-deviation increase in a deceased star's "brokerage" is associated with roughly a 10% drop in their coauthors' annual publications. You can lose a collaborator who was your bridge to a whole region of ideas, and the bridge goes down with them.

Is any of this special to scientists and inventors — unusually fragile creatures, perhaps? No. The same mechanism shows up in life-and-death frontline work. Chen (2021) uses Medicare records for two heart procedures and finds that when the doctor performing the procedure and the doctors managing the patient have more shared work experience, patients are substantially less likely to die: a one-standard-deviation increase in shared experience cuts 30-day mortality by 10% to 14%, while using fewer resources. And Bartel and coauthors (2014), studying hospital nursing units, find that the departure of experienced nurses — and the churn of absorbing new hires and temps — significantly lowers a unit's productivity, beyond anything explained by raw skill or experience levels. Surgeons, nurses, inventors, scientists: in every case, who you've worked with before is part of how good you are right now.

Reading these together, I land here, with fairly high confidence: team-specific capital is real, it's built by working together over time, and it's largely about knowledge complementarity — the partner who improves your thinking — rather than access, prestige, or proximity. Each individual sub-result is a single careful design, so I won't oversell any one of them; but they triangulate, across four different settings, on the same mechanism. That convergence is what makes me believe it.

"But maybe it's good when stars die"

Now the strongest objection, and it's a real one, because it comes from inside the same research program.

If losing a star is so costly, you'd think a field would be set back every time one dies. Azoulay, Fons-Rosen, and Graff Zivin (2019) — two-thirds the same team as the original study — looked at what happens to an entire scientific field after the premature death of one of its eminent figures, across 452 such deaths. The collaborators decline, exactly as before. But the non-collaborators do something surprising: their flow of articles into the dead star's field rises by about 8.6%, and this new outsider work is disproportionately highly cited and draws on a different body of prior science. The provocative title is the punchline — "Does Science Advance One Funeral at a Time?" — and the answer is, a little bit, yes. While the luminary was alive, outsiders seem to have been reluctant to challenge the reigning approach; once the gate-keeper is gone, the field opens to fresh entrants and fresh ideas.

So which is it — is losing a star a disaster or a renewal? Both, and they're not in contradiction, because they're answering different questions. Privately, for the people who worked with the star, it's a lasting loss. Socially, for the field as a whole, the loss to insiders can be more than offset by the gain from outsiders. A back-of-the-envelope shows how easily the signs diverge: a star might have a dozen or two close collaborators who each lose 5–8%, while the pool of potential entrants to a whole field is vast — so an 8.6% surge in highly-cited outsider work can outweigh the insiders' decline in the aggregate even as every individual collaborator is genuinely worse off. The private ledger and the social ledger come apart.

Here I'm going to allow myself a more speculative read than the evidence strictly licenses, so treat the next paragraph as interpretation rather than established fact. I think these results all belong to a single logic: your productivity depends on who occupies your idea-space, and the sign depends on whether they're complements or competitors. Lose a complement — a collaborator — and you fall (Azoulay 2010; Jaravel et al.). The flip side appears when you gain competitors: Borjas and Doran (2012) show that when a wave of elite Soviet mathematicians arrived in the U.S. after 1992, the American mathematicians whose fields overlapped with theirs saw their output fall, with some pushed to lower-ranked institutions or out of publishing altogether. And "one funeral at a time" is the same competitor logic viewed from the outside: to a non-collaborator, a dominant living star is a competitor for attention and a barrier to entry, so the star's death is, for them, the removal of a competitor — and they bloom. The death simultaneously destroys complementarities (bad for collaborators) and clears the field of a competitor (good for outsiders). One mechanism, opposite signs, depending on which side of the idea-space you're standing on.

A related caveat keeps the renewal story from being too rosy. There's a vertical version of these ties — mentor to student — and there the loss is unambiguous: Waldinger (2010), using the same Nazi-era expulsions, finds that losing high-quality faculty badly hurt their PhD students (a one-standard-deviation increase in faculty quality raised a student's odds of publishing the dissertation in a top journal by about 13 percentage points). So losing established colleagues you don't work with: no effect. Losing your collaborators: lasting harm. Losing your advisor: serious harm. The pattern is consistent throughout — it's the working/mentoring relationship, the live tie through which ideas actually flow, that carries the human capital.

Bottom line

Read the literature as a whole and it supports a claim that is sharper and stranger than "good collaborators are nice to have." A real and measurable share of what we record as individual productivity is team-specific capital — co-produced inside particular relationships, grown through years of working together, and non-portable. Lose the relationship and you lose the capital, durably; this part, anchored by two clean natural experiments in two separate domains, is not in much doubt. And the thing you lose is specifically the intellectual complementarity of a partner who made your ideas better — not proximity, not prestige, not the org chart. The "great department" loses to the "great collaboration."

The honest bound on all this: the field-level silver lining — that a star's death can refresh a discipline — rests on a single (excellent) study, so I hold it loosely. And the unifying "complements and competitors in idea-space" frame I offered is my synthesis, not a tested theorem. Strip those away and the core still stands.

If the core is right, some practical instincts need adjusting. A star hire may be worth less than their record suggests, because part of that record was a partnership they're leaving behind — and part of their future value will depend on whether they can rebuild team-specific capital in the new place. Retention isn't just about keeping the star; it's about keeping the pairs. And when we evaluate people, the individual CV is a leaky abstraction: it credits to one person what two people built. The most productive thing an organization owns may be a set of working relationships it can't see on any roster — its own invisible colleges — and the cheapest way to destroy value is to break them up without noticing they were there.

What we don't know

  • How much of total productivity is team-specific? We can measure the effect of losing one tie. We don't have a clean decomposition of a whole career into "portable" and "relational" capital. The 15% figure is the cost of one death, not a budget for the whole person.
  • Does it generalize past elites? The cleanest evidence is about superstar scientists, prolific inventors, and surgeons. Whether the same magnitudes hold for ordinary teams in ordinary firms is plausible — Bartel's nurses suggest yes — but thinly tested.
  • Lost capability or lost motivation? A collaborator's death could remove a genuine cognitive complement, or it could just be demoralizing and disruptive. The "experience" and "conceptual feedback" results lean toward capability, but the grief confound is hard to fully purge from a death design.
  • Is the "renewal" real progress? Outsider work after a star dies is more cited. More-cited is not the same as better or truer; it could be fashion, or a field chasing novelty. I'd want evidence that the post-funeral ideas actually hold up before celebrating too hard.
  • Can you protect the ties without ossifying? If breaking up collaborations destroys value but a dominant star also blocks a field's renewal, then there's a genuine tension between preserving team-specific capital and keeping the door open for newcomers. Nobody has worked out where the optimum is.

The tidiest summary is the one the death experiments keep whispering. We like to think of productivity as a thing we own and carry. A lot of it, it turns out, we only rent — jointly, from the people we work with — and when one of them is gone, the books come up short in a way no amount of individual talent quite refills.

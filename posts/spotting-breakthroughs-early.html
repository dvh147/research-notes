In 2006, three social scientists ran an experiment that should unsettle anyone who claims to spot the next big thing. Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts built an online "music lab," recruited more than fourteen thousand teenagers, and let them listen to and download forty-eight songs by forty-eight unknown bands. Some visitors saw only the song titles. The rest were split into eight separate "worlds," and in each world people could see how many times each song had already been downloaded — but only by others in their own world.

The eight worlds began identical. They did not stay that way. A song that became a hit in one world sank without trace in another; the same forty-eight songs sorted themselves into wildly different rankings on the strength of nothing but which early clicks happened to snowball. Quality mattered, but only at the margins: the best songs rarely did terribly and the worst rarely did brilliantly, yet in between — which is to say, for almost every song — "almost any other result was possible." Letting people see what others were choosing didn't just make success more unequal. It made success unpredictable.

Now swap songs for scientific papers, patents, or startups, and you have the uncomfortable question behind this post: at the moment a piece of work is born, can anyone actually tell whether it will be a breakthrough? We run a vast amount of machinery on the assumption that the answer is yes. Grant panels score proposals to two decimal places. Hiring committees read tea leaves in a candidate's early citations. Funders, journals, and venture capitalists all behave as though greatness is legible at the start. The claim I want to defend is that it mostly isn't: the eventual impact of a paper or invention is only weakly predictable up front. Not random — weakly predictable. And the gap between "weakly" and "reliably" is exactly where a lot of our institutions quietly fall over.

This matters because the alternative to picking winners is not paralysis. It's a different design: spread the bets, tolerate failure, and give good work a second look. If we can't see breakthroughs coming, that's not a counsel of despair — it's an argument for portfolios over prophets.

The sleeping beauties

Start with the most direct evidence that impact isn't legible early: some of the most important papers ever written looked like nothing for years. Bibliometricians call them "sleeping beauties" — papers that lie dormant, barely cited, then suddenly wake up and explode.

In a sweep of more than twenty million papers, Qing Ke and colleagues built a "beauty coefficient" to measure how delayed a paper's recognition was, and turned up some spectacular sleepers. Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen's 1935 paper — the one that introduced quantum entanglement — sat almost ignored for decades before becoming one of the most-cited papers in physics. Karl Pearson's 1901 paper that founded principal component analysis, and a 1906 chemistry paper on adsorption, slept even longer. The single most important caveat is honesty's: Ke's team showed that sleeping beauties don't form a tidy separate class you could screen for. Delayed recognition runs along a continuum, with no natural line between "normal" papers and the deep sleepers, and the most extreme cases are a small minority. So this isn't "every breakthrough is ignored at first." It's something subtler and more awkward: a non-trivial share of the biggest hits were indistinguishable from duds for a decade or more, and there was no obvious flag at birth that said wake me later.

If even the eventual giants can look ordinary for twenty years, the idea that a three-year citation window tells you who the winners are starts to wobble.

The early signal is faint

Maybe that's too pessimistic. Plenty of smart people have tried to predict a paper's destiny from its first few years, and one famous attempt claimed real success. Dashun Wang, Chaoming Song, and Albert-László Barabási proposed a model that gave each paper three numbers — a fitness, an immediacy, and a longevity — and argued that, fitted to early citations, it could forecast a paper's ultimate citation count and collapse messy citation histories onto a single universal curve.

It was a beautiful result. It also did not hold up cleanly. A formal critique in the same journal, together with related work, showed the model's long-run predictions were frequently far off, and a separate, blunter paper by Daniel Penner and colleagues put a finger on why prediction in this domain flatters itself. Much of the apparent forecasting power, they showed, is an artifact: cumulative measures like total citations or the h-index only ever go up, so "predicting" next year's total from this year's is partly just measuring the same growing pile twice. Strip out that autocorrelation — and look at the people for whom prediction actually matters, early-career researchers — and the accuracy collapses. Their verdict was that the state-of-the-art models "do not do a good enough job of predicting future impact to be used with confidence."

The same skepticism extends to the signals institutions actually use. When Balázs Győrffy and coauthors compared grant reviewers' scores against what applicants went on to produce, they found that a researcher's past output predicted future output better than the reviewers' scores did — the expert scoring added little forward signal beyond a simple track record. The best evidence here suggests the early citation count and the panel score, the two instruments we lean on hardest, are noisy gauges of what a piece of work will eventually be worth.

There's a mechanism lurking under all this. Early attention tends to compound: a paper that gets cited gets seen, gets cited more — the rich-get-richer dynamic the sociologist Robert Merton named the "Matthew effect." When success feeds on itself, an early lead partly causes later success rather than revealing underlying quality. Which brings us back to the music lab.

Run the tape again

Salganik's worlds are the cleanest demonstration we have that cumulative advantage breeds genuine unpredictability. Because the only thing that differed across his eight social-influence worlds was the random sequence of early clicks, any divergence in outcomes is caused by path dependence, not by quality. And the worlds diverged a lot — the more visible the download counts, the more unequal and the more unpredictable the results became. This is as close to "running the tape of history twice" as social science gets, and the tape comes out different each time.

You might object that music is frivolous and science is meritocratic. Here the evidence gets genuinely interesting. Roberta Sinatra and colleagues reconstructed the careers of nearly three thousand physicists and found what they called the random impact rule: a scientist's single highest-impact paper is equally likely to be their first, their last, or any one in between. The biggest hit arrives at a random point in the sequence — you cannot tell from where a paper falls in a career whether it's the one that will matter.

But — and this is the honest other half — Sinatra's team also found something stable. Model a paper's impact as roughly ability × luck, and the ability term (they call it Q) turns out to be close to constant across a scientist's whole career, and it varies a lot between scientists. So talent is real and persistent; it sets the distribution you're drawing from. Luck decides which draw becomes the hit. That is a precise way to state the whole post: who is good is somewhat predictable; which specific thing will be the breakthrough is mostly not.

We flinch from the breakthroughs

If prediction were merely noisy, we could average over it. What makes the problem worse is that the noise isn't symmetric — our evaluation machinery is biased, and biased against exactly the novelty that produces breakthroughs.

The sharpest evidence is experimental. Kevin Boudreau, Eva Guinan, Karim Lakhani, and Christoph Riedl ran a real grant competition at a research university in which they randomly assigned evaluators to proposals — 2,130 evaluator-proposal pairs in all. The more novel a proposal, the lower the scores it received, and the pattern wasn't explained by novel proposals simply being noisier or worse. Expert reviewers, faced with genuinely unfamiliar ideas, marked them down.

Why would they? A field-scale study by Jian Wang, Reinhilde Veugelers, and Paula Stephan suggests both the cost and the reason. Measuring novelty as first-time combinations of previously unconnected research, they found that highly novel papers are more likely to become top-1% hits in the long run, to seed follow-on work, and to travel into distant fields — but they are also higher-variance, suffer delayed recognition (they're far less likely to look like winners in a short citation window), and end up published in lower-impact-factor journals. Novel work is a high-risk, high-return bet that the short-term metrics systematically under-price. A complementary field experiment by Jacqueline Lane and coauthors found that evaluators weigh negative information more heavily when a project is novel — a single red flag sinks an unconventional idea faster than a conventional one — so, in their words, "conservatism gets funded." And Sen Chai and Anoop Menon, sifting 5.3 million life-science papers, found the same fingerprint from the other side: work on rarely-addressed topics is more likely to break through, even as the system tilts toward the crowded and familiar.

Put the pieces together and the bias compounds the noise. We can't see breakthroughs well at the best of times, and when we do catch a glimpse of something unfamiliar, our instinct is to flinch.

Objection! Surely the experts know something

Now the strongest counterargument, which I think is partly right and worth stating at full strength: prediction is not zero, and treating it as zero would be its own mistake.

Three findings keep me honest here. First, there are real structural signals. Brian Uzzi and colleagues, across 17.9 million papers, found that the highest-impact work tends to pair a deeply conventional core with a dash of unusual combination — and papers with that profile are about twice as likely to be highly cited. That's a genuine ex-ante tell. Second, crowds forecast well even when individuals don't. When Stefano DellaVigna and Devin Pope asked experts and non-experts to predict the results of fifteen experiments, the average forecast was superb — it beat 96% of individual forecasts — even though no individual marker of expertise (citations, rank, field) predicted who would forecast accurately. Third, collective belief can anticipate replication: when Colin Camerer and a large team ran prediction markets on whether social-science findings would replicate, the market prices tracked the eventual outcomes remarkably well (a rank correlation around 0.8), and the researchers concluded that "peers were very effective at predicting future replication success."

So why not just convene the experts and trust the crowd? Two reasons to stay humble. The replication-market result is encouraging but not airtight: the earlier economics version of the same exercise, with only eighteen studies, found the market's predictions positive but not statistically significant. And predicting whether a clean, finished result will replicate is a far easier task than predicting whether a brand-new, unfinished idea will eventually change a field. The honest synthesis is the one the Sinatra model already gave us: there's signal in ability and in structure, but it's swamped by variance and timing once you get down to the individual bet.

Here's the arithmetic that makes the point concrete. Suppose top hits are about 1% of papers, and suppose the best ex-ante signal we have roughly doubles a paper's odds of joining them — generous, given Uzzi's estimate. Then even acting on the best available tell, you move from being wrong 99% of the time to being wrong 98% of the time. That's a real edge. It is nowhere near enough to pick the winner of any single race. It's an argument for buying a lot of tickets, not for divining the one.

So design for it

If you accept that breakthroughs are weakly predictable and that evaluation is biased against them, a set of policies follows that look strange only if you still believe in prophets.

Tolerate failure, and lengthen the leash. The cleanest causal evidence comes from Pierre Azoulay, Joshua Graff Zivin, and Gustavo Manso, who compared scientists funded by the Howard Hughes Medical Institute — which gives long, forgiving grants — against similar NIH-funded researchers on short, deliverable-driven cycles. HHMI's people produced high-impact papers at a markedly higher rate and pushed into more novel territory. They also produced more flops. That is not a flaw in the program; it is the price of admission. You cannot harvest the upside of high-variance work without paying for the misses, and a system that punishes every miss will get conventional, predictable, forgettable research. The same logic shows up in the private sector: Xuan Tian and Tracy Wang found that startups backed by more failure-tolerant venture capitalists are significantly more innovative.

Randomize at the margin. If reviewers genuinely can't tell the proposals near the funding line apart — and the inter-rater reliability of grant scores near the threshold is low, as Manuela Heyard and colleagues document for the Swiss National Science Foundation — then forcing a fine-grained ranking is theater. A growing number of funders have drawn the obvious conclusion and added a lottery: the SNSF now breaks ties by random draw, the Volkswagen Foundation's "Experiment!" scheme has used partial randomization since 2017, New Zealand's Health Research Council has run a modified lottery since 2013, the British Academy randomizes among above-threshold small grants, and Austria's "1000 Ideas" program pairs double-blind review with a random draw. A lottery among the fundable isn't an abdication of judgment; it's an honest admission of where judgment runs out — and a way to stop quietly screening out the unconventional.

Give second chances, and don't over-read the early metrics. If sleeping beauties are real and short-term citations are biased against novelty, then re-evaluating work after a longer window, and resisting the urge to crown or bury people on three years of data, are not soft-heartedness — they're calibration.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That our evaluation systems discount novelty is fairly convincing, resting on a randomized experiment and several large-scale studies that point the same direction. That early citations and peer scores are noisy guides to eventual impact is well supported, from the autocorrelation critiques to the weak predictive validity of reviewer scores. That delayed recognition is real is established, though the deep sleepers are a minority. The leg I hold most lightly is the size of the pure-luck component: Salganik's worlds are a clean experiment, but they're about music in a browser, and the leap to scientific impact is an analogy, not a measurement — Sinatra's random-impact rule makes me more confident, but it's observational. Net, the best evidence suggests you can shade the odds of spotting a breakthrough but not reliably call one, and that the instruments we use to try are tilted against the very work most likely to surprise us.

So the reflexive posture — fund the best, rank to the decimal, trust the panel — is too confident by half. The right response to deep uncertainty isn't a better crystal ball. It's a portfolio, a tolerance for failure, a lottery where the ranking is fiction, and the humility to look again later. Shade the odds with what signal there is — but buy a lot of tickets, and keep the receipts.

What we don't know

  • How much of the luck is irreducible? Salganik's unpredictability is real, but some of what looks like luck is surely just quality we haven't learned to measure. Nobody has cleanly partitioned "intrinsically unpredictable" from "predictable, but not yet." The honest answer is that we don't know the ceiling on prediction.
  • Does machine prediction move that ceiling? Text-based and machine-learning models now claim to flag breakthroughs earlier than citations do. Whether they find genuine signal or just relabel cumulative advantage faster is an open, and testable, question.
  • Do the funding experiments scale? The HHMI evidence is about elite life-scientists, and the grant lotteries are young and small. We don't yet have the long-run study that watches a randomized, failure-tolerant portfolio play out against a "fund-the-best" control and measures the realized return.
  • Where is prediction actually good? This whole argument is drawn mostly from science and culture. There may be domains — late-stage clinical trials, perhaps, or incremental engineering — where the signal-to-noise is high enough that confident winner-picking really does beat a portfolio. Knowing the boundary matters as much as knowing the rule.

Here are two pieces of conventional wisdom about where great ideas come from. You have heard both, and both have real evidence behind them.

The first: the biggest breakthroughs happen at the borders between fields. Combine two areas nobody thought to combine -- microbiology and information theory, finance and statistical physics, linguistics and computer science -- and you get the discoveries that redraw the map. Funding agencies believe this. Deans believe this. The word "interdisciplinary" appears in roughly every university strategic plan written this century, always as a virtue.

The second: interdisciplinary work gets punished. It is harder to fund, harder to publish, slower to be cited, and riskier for a career. Anyone who has tried to place a paper that sits between two literatures -- or watched a cross-field grant proposal die in a panel where no single reviewer felt it was theirs -- knows the feeling in their stomach.

Both of these are true. That is the puzzle this post is about, and the resolution turns out to be a single fact about distributions. Bridging distant fields really is over-represented among the genuine breakthroughs. It is also, on average, a bad bet that fails most of the time. Distance doesn't raise your expected payoff; it buys you a lottery ticket with a fatter jackpot and a much bigger pile of losing stubs. The celebration and the punishment are both rational responses to the same lopsided distribution -- the enthusiasts are staring at the jackpot, the gatekeepers at the losing stubs.

And here's the part with teeth: because our machinery for funding and judging research runs on averages and short horizons, it systematically under-rewards exactly the bets whose entire value lives in a rare, slow-arriving tail. That's the claim. Let me build it.

(A companion post, Novelty is a high-variance bet, makes the variance argument for novelty in general. This one is its sharper, nastier cousin: it's specifically about distance between fields, about why most distant bridges collapse, and about the funding and evaluation system that the distance penalty runs through.)

The breakthroughs really are at the borders

Start with the optimists, because they're right. The cleanest evidence comes from Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013), who analyzed 17.9 million papers in Science. For each paper they looked at the pairs of prior journals it cited and asked, relative to chance, how typical or atypical each pairing was -- a clean way to measure whether a paper yokes together bodies of work that rarely appear side by side. Then they asked which papers became "hits" (top 5% of citations).

The winners were not the wild ones. The highest hit rate -- 9.11 per 100, nearly double the 5-per-100 baseline -- went to papers that were mostly conventional but carried a tail of atypical, distant combinations. Papers that were high on only one dimension (very conventional or very atypical, but not both) did about half as well. The pattern held whether you defined a hit as the top 1%, 5%, or 10%, and it held in every decade from 1950 to 2000. The reach into a distant field is real and it matters -- but it pays off when it's anchored to a solid conventional base, not when it's reaching with both hands.

The same shape shows up in technology. Kneeland, Schilling & Aharonson (2020) went hunting for "outlier" patents -- the genuine far-from-everything inventions -- and found they emerge through a distinctive set of processes: long search paths, reasoning from science, and distant recombination. The tail of the patent distribution is disproportionately built from reaches across technological distance. And Esposito (2023), mapping a century of US breakthrough inventions, finds they cluster in the metros with the highest local knowledge variety and, increasingly, depend on long-distance collaboration. Where many different kinds of knowledge can collide, breakthroughs are more common.

So the enthusiasts have their evidence: when you look in the breakthrough tail, you find an unusual concentration of bridges between distant fields. I read this part as well supported -- big samples, clean measures, convergent across science and patents.

But the typical bridge is a bad bet

Now the part the enthusiasts skip. Being over-represented in the tail is not the same as being good on average. A distribution can throw more jackpots and still have a lower mean, if it also throws far more zeros. And that is what the distance literature finds, again and again, in a shape so consistent it deserves a name: the inverted-U of distance.

The canonical statement is Nooteboom, Van Haverbeke, Duysters, Gilsing & van den Oord (2007), one of the most-cited papers in this corner of the field. Studying firms in technology alliances, they find that cognitive distance -- how different two partners' knowledge is -- has an inverted-U relationship with innovation performance. Too little distance and there's nothing new to learn; too much and the partners simply can't understand each other well enough to combine anything. Somewhere in the middle is a peak. The far end of the distance axis is not where the magic is; it's where the failures are.

This is not one paper's quirk. The same inverted-U turns up:

  • in biopharmaceutical patents, where Keijl, Gilsing, Knoben & Duysters (2016) find an intermediate level of recombination -- a blend of local, adjacent, and distant knowledge -- carries the highest technological impact, beating both purely local and purely distant inventions;
  • in firms solving complex problems, where Caner, Cohen & Pil (2017) find that very low and very high internal knowledge variety both produce weaker solutions than a moderate range;
  • in R&D alliances, where Subramanian, Bo & Kah-Hin (2018) find an inverted-U of technological distance on how much firms actually learn from each other;
  • and in alliance partner novelty (Kok, Faems & de Faria, 2020) and green-tech diversification (Ning & Guo, 2022), both inverted-U.

When a relationship shows up this reliably across patents, papers, alliances, and problem-solving -- with different data, different measures, different research teams -- I take the shape as fairly well established, even though almost all of it is correlational (more on that caveat below).

And here's a toy version of what that means for a portfolio of bets, using Uzzi's own hit rates. Fund 100 safely-conventional projects and you expect about 5 top-tier hits, with very few outright failures -- a tidy, predictable pile. Fund 100 projects that pair a conventional base with a distant reach and you expect about 9 hits -- but you also collect a much bigger heap of duds, because most attempts to span a real gap don't connect. Now fund 100 projects that reach for maximum distance with no conventional anchor, and you're back down around the baseline, ~5 or worse, with the most duds of all. The middle column is the only free-ish lunch; the far column is mostly losing stubs with a couple of jackpots mixed in.

That last fact -- "mostly losing stubs" -- is the half of the story the borders-are- magic crowd leaves out. Jung & Lee (2016) put it bluntly: when they look at firms searching genuinely original knowledge, local search outperforms boundary-spanning search at producing high-impact breakthroughs. Reaching across is not automatically better; done carelessly it is worse. The average bridge underperforms. Most bridges fail.

Why the far bridges collapse

The inverted-U has a mechanism, and it's the same one Nooteboom named: you can only combine what you can absorb. To weld two distant fields together you need enough of a shared base to understand both well enough to see the join -- what the literature calls absorptive capacity. Past some distance, the two bodies of knowledge are so foreign to each other that nobody in the room can hold both, and the "combination" never actually happens. The reach is attempted; the bridge isn't built.

The supporting evidence is all about what rescues a distant bridge -- which tells you what's missing when it collapses. Caner, Cohen & Pil (2017) find that firms struggling with too much or too little internal variety do better when an alliance partner supplies knowledge in a familiar domain -- a handhold. Subramanian, Bo & Kah-Hin (2018) find the costs of distance are mitigated when partners share a more homogeneous knowledge base underneath. Kelley, Ali & Zahra (2013) find that breakthrough patents draw on diverse knowledge from other organizations -- but lean on geographic proximity to integrate it, because integration is the hard part. And Huo (2021) finds the net effect of technological distance on a co-invented patent's quality flips sign depending on the structure of the collaboration: negative when partners are otherwise similar, positive when the bridge is properly built across distinct regions and networks. The distance is the same; whether it pays depends entirely on whether it gets crossed.

So "bridge distant fields" is incomplete advice. The complete version is: reach for a distant field, but only as far as you can still absorb, and only if you can supply the shared base -- or the people -- to actually integrate it. Which brings us to the two halves of the puzzle.

Celebrated and starved

If distant bridging is a fat-tailed, mostly-failing bet, then a system that judges research on averages and short time windows will systematically punish it -- even when the bet is collectively worth making. And that is exactly what we see.

Funding. The most striking single result is Bromham, Dinnage & Hua (2016), who took all 18,476 proposals submitted to the Australian Research Council over five years and measured how far each one spanned across fields. The more interdisciplinary the proposal, the lower its probability of being funded -- a consistent negative relationship that survived controls for the number of collaborators, the primary field, and the type of institution. The thing every funding agency says it wants is the thing its panels are quietly least likely to fund.

Publishing and prestige. Rafols, Leydesdorff, O'Hare, Nightingale & Stirling (2012) show how this compounds: the top-ranked journals in a field span a narrower set of disciplines than lower-ranked ones, so evaluation systems built on journal rankings exhibit a "systematic bias in favour of mono-disciplinary research." Interdisciplinary units look worse on the metrics not because they do worse work but because the metrics are shaped like a single discipline.

Citations and time. Even when interdisciplinary work does land, the reward arrives late. Zhang, Wang, Du & Havlin (2024) find that interdisciplinary papers take significantly longer to reach their citation peak -- a delay that's nearly universal across fields and decades, and that goes beyond the usual rich-get-richer dynamics. So any evaluation using a short citation window mechanically understates interdisciplinary work, because it's measuring the tail before the tail has arrived. And the citation relationship itself is, once again, an inverted-U: Yegros-Yegros, Rafols & D'Este (2015) find that variety, balance, and disparity each have a hump-shaped relationship with citations, with distal (most disparate) interdisciplinarity the part that gets discounted -- either because it fails more often or because audiences are wary of papers that mix wildly different traditions.

Careers. Put it together at the level of a scientist and you get the title of Leahey, Beckman & Stanko (2017): "Prominent but Less Productive." Across roughly 900 scientists and 32,000 papers, doing interdisciplinary work lowers a scientist's publication output -- the cognitive and coordination costs of spanning fields are real -- even as it raises the visibility of the papers that do get out. A production penalty with an impact premium: you publish less, but what you publish travels further. For an individual deciding how to spend a career, that is a genuinely double-edged deal, and a rational person facing a tenure clock might well decline it.

None of these gatekeepers is being irrational. They're responding to the real distribution: most distant bridges fail, and the failures are visible now while the jackpots are rare and slow. The trouble is that averaging over a fat-tailed bet throws away the whole reason to make it. The expected value of a hundred distant attempts can be excellent even though the median attempt is a dud -- and a system that funds the median starves the portfolio.

A quick back-of-the-envelope on the stakes. If a distant-bridge portfolio hits the top tier at ~9 per 100 versus ~5 for the safe pile, then an evaluator who can't see the tail -- who funds on average impact or a three-year citation count -- and so defaults to the conventional projects gives up something like 4 extra top-tier breakthroughs per 100 funded. That is the price of judging a lottery by its losing stubs.

The team is the bridge

There's a way out of the absorptive-capacity trap, and it's the most useful nuance in this whole area: you don't need one mind to span the distance if you have several minds who collectively do. This is where the distance story becomes a story about teams -- and where it diverges most sharply from the lone-inventor framing of the novelty post.

The sharpest evidence is Vestal & Danneels (2022), who study 834 multi-cluster R&D teams in nanotechnology -- teams whose members sit in different geographic and technological "hot spots." The technological distance between those clusters has -- you can guess by now -- an inverted-U with the team's chance of a breakthrough: a moderate gap between specialties is ideal, too large a gap and the team can't integrate. But the crucial finding is what determines whether the gap gets bridged: the density of inter-cluster ties, the actual collaborative links running across the distance. Teams with the connective tissue to move knowledge between their distant pockets turn distance into breakthroughs; teams without it just have distance.

Two more pieces fit here. Back in the Uzzi data, teams were 37.7% more likely than solo authors to insert a novel, distant combination into an otherwise familiar base -- the winning recipe is easier to pull off with more than one specialist in the room. And Gruber, Harhoff & Hoisl (2013) find that inventors trained as scientists span technological boundaries more readily than those trained as engineers, a reminder that the ability to bridge is a specific, unevenly distributed skill. The bridge, in other words, is not the gap -- it's the people and ties that reach across it. Assemble distance without the means to integrate it and you've built a chasm, not a bridge.

Objection! Isn't this just "novelty"?

The first thing a careful reader should worry about: is "distance between fields" really distinct from plain "novelty," or am I relabeling the same variance result?

It's a fair challenge, and the honest answer is that the two are related but not identical. Novelty (as in the companion post) is about making any unusual combination; the distance literature isolates one specific flavor -- how far apart the combined fields are -- and finds that this dimension has its own signature. The decomposition matters: Yegros-Yegros et al. (2015) split interdisciplinarity into variety, balance, and disparity, and it's disparity -- distance -- that behaves worst. Ke (2023), studying how biomedical papers get cited by patents, finds the same split: spanning more fields (variety) and spanning them evenly (balance) help, but spanning distant fields (disparity) offsets the gain on the extensive margin while raising impact intensity conditional on landing. Distance is doing distinct work, and it is the part that both fattens the tail and thins the middle. So this isn't a synonym for novelty; it's the specific sub-component that the "bridge distant fields" slogan is actually about.

Objection! Maybe the gatekeepers are right

The deeper objection: if most distant bridges fail, maybe funders and referees are correctly steering scarce money toward safer, higher-expected-value work. Maybe the penalty is just good risk management.

I think this is half right and half wrong, and the line between them is the whole argument. It's right that a single distant bet is a bad bet -- a lone researcher staking one project on a far-flung bridge faces mostly the failure tail. It's wrong as a verdict on the portfolio. The breakthroughs that move fields disproportionately come from these bridges (Uzzi; Kneeland; Esposito), and their value is enormous and slow to show up (Zhang et al.). A funder holding a hundred bets is exactly the agent who should tolerate the variance -- and is exactly the agent currently judging each bet by its median outcome and three-year citations. The mismatch isn't that distance is good and gatekeepers are dumb; it's that distance is a portfolio play being evaluated with single-bet, short-horizon tools. That's a fixable design flaw, not a law of nature.

Where I'd hedge hardest is causality. Almost all of this evidence is correlational: distant work turns out to be more variable and worse-rewarded, but we rarely get to randomize distance. The inverted-U could partly reflect who attempts distant bridges rather than the distance itself. The cleaner quasi-experimental results in the broader novelty literature nudge incentives toward risk rather than distance directly. So I hold the shape of the relationship with more confidence than any specific causal magnitude.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That breakthroughs are over-represented at the borders between distant fields is well supported. That the average distant bridge underperforms and most fail -- the inverted-U of distance -- is one of the more reliably replicated patterns in the innovation literature, though it is largely correlational. That interdisciplinary work is systematically under-rewarded by funding, publishing, citation, and career metrics is convergent across several large studies. Stack them and the paradox dissolves: cross-field bridging is a low-hit-rate, fat-tailed, slow-paying bet, simultaneously the source of the biggest wins and a loser on average -- and our evaluation machinery, built for averages and short horizons, reliably picks the wrong tail to optimize against.

The practical upshots are concrete. Reach a moderate distance, not the maximum -- the sweet spot of the inverted-U beats the far edge. Anchor the reach in a conventional base -- the Uzzi recipe, conventional core plus distant dash, not distance for its own sake. Bridge with teams and ties, not lone heroics -- supply the absorptive capacity the gap demands. And if you fund research, hold distance in a portfolio and judge it on long horizons, because the entire case for the bet is a tail you cannot see in a three-year window. The slogan "bridge distant fields" isn't wrong. It's just dangerously incomplete -- it names the jackpot and hides the pile of losing stubs, and most people who follow it end up holding stubs.

What we don't know

  • Is the tail worth the price? No one has really computed the risk-adjusted social return to distant bridging -- whether the rare jackpots actually pay for the many failures, and for which funders. The portfolio argument is a logic, not a measured number.
  • How far is too far? The inverted-U says there's an optimum, but where it sits -- and whether it shifts by field, era, or the availability of bridge-builders -- is largely uncharted. "Moderate distance" is easy to say and hard to operationalize ex ante.
  • Causality. We mostly observe that distant work is more variable and less rewarded; we rarely get to assign distance. A clean natural experiment that pushes otherwise-similar projects to span more or less distance would sharpen all of this.
  • Does the AI/large-team era change the bridging cost? If models lower the cost of absorbing a distant field, the inverted-U's peak could move outward -- making far bridges cheaper to cross and the historical penalty partly an artifact of how expensive integration used to be.
  • Citations aren't welfare. Almost every outcome here is a citation or patent count. The breakthroughs that matter most for human welfare need not be the ones that show up brightest in the tail of a citation distribution.

There is a story we all know about what happens when a big chain store opens on the edge of town. The local hardware store, the family grocer, the independent pharmacy — they can't match the prices, the customers drift away, and within a few years the shutters come down on Main Street. It is the plot of a hundred newspaper features and at least one Tom Hanks movie. When the store in question is foreign — a Walmart in Mexico, a Carrefour in Brazil — the same story gets retold as a parable about globalization hollowing out a country from the inside.

That story is not wrong. The shops really do close. But it describes one ledger while ignoring a bigger one. If you actually go and measure what the arrival of a modern retailer does to the people who live there, the largest number by far shows up somewhere the Main Street story never looks: on the shopping receipt. When economists David Atkin, Benjamin Faber, and Marco González-Navarro tracked the entry of foreign supermarkets across Mexico using detailed household spending data, they found that the average household was made better off by the equivalent of 6.2 percent of its income — almost all of it through a lower cost of living. That is not a rounding error. For a poor family, a 6.2 percent raise is the kind of number that governments engineer entire tax reforms to deliver.

This is the claim of the post: the dominant welfare effect of modern retail competition runs through the cost of living, it is large, and it reaches the poor. The shuttered shops are real, but they are a concentrated cost sitting on top of a much broader consumer gain — and once you put the two on the same scale, the consumer side wins handily. I'll build the argument in pieces. First, that the cost-of-living gains are large and show up across very different countries and methods. Then, where the savings actually come from (a more surprising answer than you'd guess). Then the genuinely hard question — whether the gains reach the poor, or mostly the rich. Then the strongest objection, the one about the shops and the jobs, which I'll concede and then bound. The honest caveats are real, and I'll give them their own room.

The grocery bill falls — and by a lot

Start with the cleanest possible evidence, because retail is one of the rare corners of economics where you can run an actual experiment. Matías Busso and Sebastián Galiani did exactly that in the Dominican Republic. Working with a cash-transfer program, they randomly assigned the entry of new retailers into 72 local markets — 61 firms parachuted into some neighborhoods and not others, with the coin flips controlled by the researchers. Six months later, prices in the markets that got more competition had fallen by 2 to 6 percent, self-reported quality had risen, and — exactly as a textbook would predict — prices fell furthest where the most entrants showed up. Because the customers here were poor program beneficiaries, this is about as direct a test as exists of whether competition lowers the cost of living for the poor. It does.

The Mexican supermarket study gives the same answer with a different method and a much richer welfare measure. Atkin and coauthors combined the staggered roll-out of foreign stores across localities with a model that decomposes the total gain into its sources. The headline 6.2 percent breaks down in a way worth sitting with: +5.5 percentage points from households shopping at the new foreign stores, +1.6 points from incumbent domestic stores cutting their own prices in response — and −0.7 points lost to the exit of pre-existing retailers. The closures, in other words, are right there in the accounting. They subtract. They just subtract a small amount from a much larger sum.

Step across to a rich country and the magnitude, if anything, grows. In a classic study, Jerry Hausman and Ephraim Leibtag estimated what Walmart's supercenters did to American food shoppers. On identical grocery items, supercenters ran 15 to 25 percent cheaper than traditional supermarkets. Putting together the direct savings from shopping there and the indirect pressure on competitors, they valued the gain to consumers at roughly 25 percent of food spending — a quarter of the grocery bill. And the households shopping hardest at these no-frills outlets were the lower-income ones, a fact I'll return to.

The pattern holds in settings you might not expect competition to reach. When chain pharmacies entered Indian drug markets — a place where you'd worry that cut-throat pricing means dangerous, counterfeit medicine — Daniel Bennett and Wesley Yin found the opposite: incumbent shops responded with a 5 percent improvement in drug quality and a 2 percent drop in prices. Cheaper and better at once, because chains can build the kind of reputation and supply chain that a single mom-and-pop counter cannot. And the effect didn't depend on how rich the customers were. Even gas stations fit the mold: studying the universe of Mexican fuel retailers, Lucas Davis, Shaun McRae, and Enrique Seira found that a new station within three minutes' drive pulled down prices — while a new station owned by the same company as the incumbent did nothing, a neat placebo confirming it's competition, not just construction, doing the work.

Four countries, four methods — a randomized experiment, a structural welfare decomposition, a demand-system estimate, and a series of natural experiments — all pointing the same way. When the confidence ladder asks how sure we should be that retail competition lowers the cost of living consumers face, this is the rare case where the answer is near the top rung: it's well established. How large the gain is varies, but "several percent of income" or "a fifth-to-a-quarter of the grocery bill" is the right order of magnitude.

Where the savings hide

Here is the part that surprised me. Ask most people why a new store makes shopping cheaper and they'll say: it forces the old stores to drop their prices. Competition disciplines the incumbent. That's the mechanism in the textbook and in the politician's speech.

The data are lukewarm on it. The most carefully identified study of the question, by Peter Arcidiacono, Paul Ellickson, Carl Mela, and John Singleton, matched the exact opening dates and locations of Walmart Supercenters to weekly grocery scanner data. When a Supercenter opened within a mile of an incumbent supermarket, that incumbent's revenue dropped sharply — by about 16 percent — as shoppers voted with their feet. But the incumbent's prices? Essentially unchanged. No measurable cut, product by product, brand by brand. The old store bled customers without ever moving a price tag.

How can shopping get cheaper if the old stores don't cut prices? Because the savings come mostly from switching stores, not from repricing. Picture it with round numbers. Say the new store is 20 percent cheaper on the goods you buy, and over a year you move half your basket to it. You've cut your grocery bill by about 10 percent — even if your old store charges exactly what it always did. The gain is real; it just lives in your decision to walk through a different door. That's why, in the Mexican decomposition, the lion's share of the welfare gain (5.5 of the 6.2 points) came from shopping at the new stores, and only a sliver from incumbents cutting prices.

So does the competitive price response exist at all? Honestly, the evidence is mixed. Hausman and Leibtag estimated an indirect effect of around 5 percent — incumbents cutting prices even for people who never set foot in a Walmart. Atkin's Mexican incumbents shaved prices enough to contribute that 1.6-point slice. But the cleanest single design, Arcidiacono's, finds close to nothing. My read: incumbents sometimes trim prices and sometimes don't, but the textbook discipline channel is never the main event. The main event is that consumers get a better option and take it.

And "better" isn't only about the price sticker. A recurring finding is that a large chunk of the gain is variety and quality — more products on more shelves. Christian Broda and David Weinstein showed that the explosion of available product varieties in the United States was worth about 2.6 percent of GDP, and that ignoring it made the standard price index overstate inflation by 28 percent over three decades. Jessie Handbury and David Weinstein found that once you properly account for the wider selection in big cities, food prices actually fall with city size rather than rise. In the Indian pharmacy case it was quality; in studies of imported goods, Gloria Sheu found quality improvements were the single largest source of gains. The receipt understates the win, because some of the win is a shelf that now stocks something it didn't before.

Does the checkout dividend reach the poor?

If the story stopped there it would be too clean, and the honest part of this literature is a real disagreement about distribution. Cheaper shopping is a gift — but a gift to whom?

The Mexican study delivers an uncomfortable answer: the gains were regressive. Positive for every income group, yes — but the richest households gained about 50 percent more than the poorest. The reason is mechanical and a little deflating: the new foreign supermarkets were a relatively upscale option, and well-off households shifted more than half their shopping to them, while the poorest shifted less than 15 percent. You can only save at the fancy new store if you shop at the fancy new store.

This isn't a one-off. Xavier Jaravel documented something similar and subtler inside the US retail sector: from 2004 to 2015, annual inflation ran about 0.66 percentage points higher for the bottom income quintile than the top — wider still, near 0.9 points, once you count product variety. His explanation is a kind of innovation inequality. As rising incomes swelled demand at the top, firms launched more new products aimed at affluent shoppers, and the resulting competition pushed those prices down. The poor got less of the new-product churn and so less of the disinflation. In the same vein, Jessie Handbury finds that high-income households extract about 40 percent more value per dollar from rich cities than poor households do — not because they're charged less, but because the shelves are stocked for them.

And yet — here the literature turns on itself in an illuminating way — a different body of work finds retail and trade gains to be sharply pro-poor. Pablo Fajgelbaum and Amit Khandelwal, in an influential study measuring the unequal gains from trade, conclude that "trade typically favors the poor, who concentrate spending in more traded sectors." Poor households spend a larger share of their budget on food, clothing, and basic manufactures — exactly the goods that cheap imports and efficient retail make cheaper. By that logic, falling prices for tradable staples are a poor person's windfall. Studying India's trade liberalization directly, Beyza Ural Marchand found gains at every income level but a pattern that was, on average, pro-poor.

So which is it? The resolution is that the poor's basket is unusually exposed to traded-goods prices, and exposure cuts both ways. The same feature that hands them a windfall when those prices fall hands them a beating when those prices rise. Javier Cravino and Andrei Levchenko showed the dark side cleanly: after Mexico's 1994 peso collapse sent import prices soaring, the cost of living for the poorest tenth of households rose 1.48 to 1.62 times more than for the richest tenth. Poorer households also substitute differently — Raphael Auer and coauthors, using a Swiss currency shock, find they have higher price elasticities, another reason the same shock lands unevenly.

Put it together and the contradiction dissolves into a rule: the poor almost always gain in absolute terms, but whether they gain more or less than the rich depends on two things — who adopts the new format, and what's in each group's basket. Where the new option is a down-market discounter the poor flock to (Walmart in America), the gains are pro-poor. Where it's an up-market supermarket the affluent adopt first (foreign chains in Mexico), the same arrival is regressive. There is no universal sign here, and any post that gave you one would be lying. What survives is the floor: it is hard to find a setting where the arrival of retail competition makes poor consumers worse off at the checkout. It mostly makes them better off; the fight is over how the surplus splits.

Objection! What about the shops it kills?

Now the hardest objection, and the one that animates the whole Main Street story: even if consumers win, what about the businesses that die and the people they employed?

Concede it up front, because it's true and the magnitudes are not small. Panle Jia, in a structural study of the discount industry, estimated that the presence of a chain store makes roughly half of a local market's small discount stores unprofitable, and that Walmart's expansion through the 1980s and 1990s explained 40 to 50 percent of the net decline in small discount retailers. Arcidiacono's 16 percent revenue hit to nearby supermarkets is the same wound at closer range. And Atkin's Mexican data show it in human terms: in the traditional retail sector, profits, employment, and the labor incomes of shopkeepers and their workers all fell. The shutters are not a myth.

But three things bound the damage, and together they're why the consumer ledger still wins.

First, the losses are local and concentrated, not economy-wide. The most striking number in the Mexican study is one I haven't mentioned yet: despite all the disruption to traditional retail, the authors found no effect on average municipality-level income or employment. Workers and spending reallocated; the town as a whole was not poorer. The pain was real and fell hard on a specific group — but it did not aggregate into a shrunken local economy. (This is the same shape we saw in an earlier post on automation: the durable losses land on identifiable incumbents even when the wider market absorbs the shock.)

Second, proximity isn't always predation. Studying discount-variety entry in Norway, Charlotte Evensen, Frode Steen, and Simen Ulsaker found a genuinely non-linear pattern: a new discounter close to an incumbent grocer often raised that grocer's sales, because the cluster pulled in more shoppers; only entry at a middle distance competed business away. Co-location can be a rising tide before it's a threat.

Third, the survivors often get better. When Walmart entered Mexico, Leonardo Iacovone and coauthors found it reshuffled supplier relationships toward higher-quality producers and pushed those suppliers to innovate and raise productivity — a competitive cull that left the surviving supply chain stronger. Florin Maican and Matilda Orth found Swedish incumbents' own productivity rose after big-box entry. Run the logic in reverse and you see the cost of the alternative: Fabiano Schivardi and Eliana Viviano showed that where Italian regions threw up entry barriers to protect incumbent retailers, the protected firms enjoyed fatter margins and lower productivity — and consumers paid higher prices for it. Shielding the shop is not free; the bill goes to the shopper.

What about the quality of the jobs that remain — isn't big-box retail a race to the bottom on pay? Here too the recent evidence is kinder than the reputation. Ellora Derenoncourt and David Weil studied the wave of voluntary minimum-wage hikes that large US retailers — including the biggest of all — adopted between 2014 and 2023, and found they raised pay and cut turnover across more than three million jobs. Scale, it turns out, can push wages up as well as down.

None of this erases the displaced shopkeeper. It means the right way to score the change is consumer gains minus concentrated incumbent losses — and every time someone has added it up, the sum is comfortably positive.

Bottom line

I come down on the consumer side, and not tentatively. The proposition that modern retail competition lowers the cost of living is about as well established as applied microeconomics gets — an actual randomized experiment, structural welfare estimates, and clean natural experiments across rich and poor countries all agree, and they agree the gain is large: several percent of income, a quarter of the grocery bill, 2.6 percent of GDP from variety alone. That those gains reach poor households in absolute terms is nearly as solid. The losses to incumbent shops are genuine and deserve their own moral weight, but they are concentrated and, in the one study that checks, they don't drag down the local economy as a whole. Net, the receipt beats Main Street.

Now the bounding, because two things keep me honest. The first is distribution: I cannot tell you that the poor gain more than the rich, because they often don't — when the new store is upmarket, the well-off capture most of the surplus, and product innovation has a built-in tilt toward affluent shoppers. "Almost everyone's groceries get cheaper" is the defensible claim; "the poor gain most" is not. The second is that most of this evidence is medium-run and partial-equilibrium — it holds the rest of the economy fixed and watches a few years, not decades. We are good at measuring the price of a basket the year after a store opens. We are worse at tracing a laid-off shopkeeper across the following decade, or at seeing what happens to prices once a chain has finished consolidating and the competition it once brought has thinned back out.

What we don't know

A few questions I'd genuinely like answered before pushing the claim further. The long-run incidence on displaced workers: Atkin's "no aggregate effect" is reassuring at the municipality level over a few years, but says little about whether a 55-year-old grocer ever recovers her old earnings. Whether the incumbent non-response generalizes: the finding that old stores don't cut prices is clean for US groceries — is it true elsewhere, or a quirk of one tightly-fought sector? The endgame of consolidation: competition that lowers prices on the way in could raise them on the way to dominance; we have far more entry studies than studies of what a mature local monopoly does. And the platform era: e-commerce is the newest competitive shock, and so far it looks consumer-friendly and even pro-remote — the "Alibaba effect" in China delivered welfare gains about 30 percent larger for the smallest, most isolated cities, and e-commerce in Japan cut variety-adjusted prices while speeding price convergence across cities. But platforms accumulate market power in ways a single supercenter never could, and the same friction that lets retailers raise prices and quietly absorb part of a government food benefit rather than passing it to shoppers could let a dominant platform keep part of the consumer surplus for itself. The story of the last forty years is that competition handed shoppers a quiet, enormous raise. Whether the next forty keep delivering it is, I think, an open question — and one worth watching the receipts to answer.

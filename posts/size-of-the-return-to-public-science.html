In 1991 the economist Edwin Mansfield published a survey that asked R&D managers at dozens of large American firms a pointed question: what share of your recent products and processes could not have been developed — or at least not without a substantial delay — in the absence of recent academic research? Across seven manufacturing industries the answer came to about 11% of new products and 9% of new processes, and to nearly a third of both in pharmaceuticals. From those answers Mansfield backed out a social rate of return to the academic research involved of around 28%.

He was not alone in finding big numbers. Two years earlier, Adam Jaffe had shown that U.S. states with more university research had more corporate patenting, with the effect concentrated in drugs and electronics. And James Adams had traced the productivity growth of American manufacturing back to the slow accumulation of academic science — with lags so long, on the order of twenty years, that the cause was almost out of sight of its effect.

For a long time, the right reaction to these numbers was suspicion. Each of them was the kind of estimate a careful skeptic distrusts on sight. So here is the claim I want to defend: across four very different ways of measuring it — state-level correlations, firm surveys, productivity accounting, and modern natural experiments — the estimated return to publicly funded science keeps coming out large and positive, and it has not collapsed as the methods got harder. What is still genuinely up for grabs is the precise number. Not its sign, and not its order of magnitude.

One note on scope. By "public science" I mean publicly funded research — government money flowing to universities and national labs — not the R&D that firms pay for out of their own pockets. The question is what society gets back from the science it buys, channeled through to the inventions that private firms ultimately patent. (The companion question of who pockets those returns — overwhelmingly firms that never held the grant — I've taken up separately; here I'm after the size of the prize, not its address.)

The numbers you were right to distrust

Start with why a sober reader in, say, 2005 should have discounted the figures above.

Mansfield's 28% came from asking executives to recall, in effect, counterfactual histories of their own products. People are not reliable narrators of what they could have invented in a world that didn't happen, and managers have every reason to flatter the science base that trains their hires. The estimate is also explicitly an average social return on the worldwide stock of academic research, built from a chain of rough assumptions; Mansfield himself reported that more conservative versions of the calculation pulled it down toward 10%.

Jaffe's state-level correlation — an estimated elasticity of corporate patenting with respect to university research on the order of 0.2, and larger for broader measures of innovation — has the textbook reverse-causality problem. Firms and universities cluster in the same places for the same reasons. A state thick with electronics firms grows electronics-heavy universities; the patents and the professors rise together without the second necessarily causing the first.

And Adams's productivity accounting rests on assigning a twenty-year lag between an academic finding and its industrial payoff. Push the lag around and the measured contribution moves with it; with effects that delayed, almost any time series can be made to fit.

The unifying problem is that all three designs are associational, and the most obvious confound pushes them in the same direction: public money flows toward fields that are scientifically hot, and scientifically hot fields are exactly where private invention was headed anyway. That bias inflates the estimated return. So the honest prior, circa 2005, was that these large numbers were at least partly selection dressed up as a causal effect. Stronger evidence would be nice — and, helpfully, it was coming.

The experiments that should have killed the old numbers

The fix is a source of variation in public funding that is not a vote of confidence in a field's commercial promise. Three studies, in three very different settings, found one.

The cleanest is Azoulay, Graff Zivin, Li, and Sampat (2019), in the Review of Economic Studies. They exploit the rigidities of NIH peer review: applications are funded by score within institute-specific paylines, and quirks in how proposals get sorted across study sections hand some research areas an idiosyncratic windfall and others an arbitrary shortfall — variation unrelated to commercial promise. The result is a clean causal estimate: a $10 million increase in NIH funding to a research area yields roughly 2.3 to 2.7 additional private-sector patents. Tellingly, the induced patents are overwhelmingly the firms', not the funded academics'; the effect shows up whether or not the patents even cite NIH-funded work.

Myers and Lanahan (2022), in the American Economic Review, use a different lever — state policies that top up federal Department of Energy grants — and measure spillovers without relying on a citation trail. Their headline is the one to hold onto: for every patent a grant recipient produces, about three more are produced by others, and the recipients capture only 25–50% of the value their funded research ultimately generates. Most of the payoff lands on someone else's books, often in a different technological field.

The third is the most surprising, because it reaches back almost a century. Gross and Sampat (2023), also in the AER, treat the U.S. World War II research effort — the crash program run through the Office of Scientific Research and Development — as a natural experiment in where public R&D got concentrated. Doubling a technology cluster's share of OSRD funding raised its patenting by roughly 20% by 1960 and 30% by 1970, with effects on the location of inventive activity that persisted for decades. Their conclusion is blunt: wartime public R&D "permanently changed the trajectory of US innovation." (I read these particular magnitudes as solid but not airtight — they come from the paper's authors and reputable summaries rather than my own reading of every table.)

Here is the move that makes this more than a list of large numbers. Recall that the old designs should have been biased upward by selection. If cleaner identification had merely confirmed a smaller effect, that would be the expected, deflating result. Instead, the natural experiments land in the same neighborhood as the suspicious old correlations. When you strip out the confound that should have been inflating the estimate and the estimate stays large, the most parsimonious explanation is that the effect was mostly real all along. The evidence that public science pays off handsomely is, I'd say, fairly convincing — not because any one study is decisive, but because three credible natural experiments, spanning basic biomedical science, applied energy research, and a 1940s mobilization, converge with two decades of messier work pointing the same way. An earlier pharmaceutical study by Toole (2012) had already found public basic research driving new-drug entry once you allowed for the long lags; it fits the pattern.

It's a spillover, which is why it's so hard to see

The size of the return and the difficulty of measuring it turn out to be the same fact, viewed from two angles. The return is large because the knowledge spills widely; it is hard to see because the knowledge spills widely.

Take the appropriation share seriously for a moment. If the people who do publicly funded research capture only a quarter to a half of the value it eventually creates — Myers and Lanahan's estimate — then any evaluation that scores a program by its grantees' own output is looking at, at most, half the ledger. A program returning a true 20% to society could show up as 5–10% on the grantee's books. The "invisible" majority isn't lost; it's sitting in the patent portfolios of firms three states away.

Two further pieces of evidence tell us those firms are everywhere. Cohen, Nelson, and Walsh (2002), drawing on the Carnegie Mellon Survey of nearly 1,500 industrial R&D units, found that public research feeds private R&D pervasively — and that it travels mainly through open channels. Published papers, conferences, informal conversation, and consulting all matter more, for most industries, than patents or licenses. Public research both suggests new projects and helps complete existing ones, in roughly equal measure. That is precisely why citation-based measures undercount the link: most of the influence never leaves a paper trail a patent examiner would catch.

And the footprint is enormous. Fleming, Greene, Li, Marx, and Yao (2019), writing in Science, traced citation chains from patents to research articles to those articles' funding acknowledgments, and found that almost a third of all U.S. patents rely on federally funded research — close to 60% in fields like chemistry and metallurgy — a share that has risen sharply since the 1970s. Patents with that lineage are, on average, more highly cited and more often renewed.

All of which is just the modern, quantified face of the oldest idea in the economics of innovation. Richard Nelson (1959) argued that basic research produces knowledge its funder can't fence in, so the market underprovides it. The recent literature keeps measuring the size of that wedge: Griliches (1992) summarized a generation of work by concluding that social rates of return to R&D "remain significantly above private rates"; Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013), in Econometrica, put the gross social return to R&D at at least twice the private return; Zacchia (2020) traces spillovers through scientist networks and lands near the same place. Public science is the slice of that wedge the market is least able to capture — and so the slice where the gap between social and private value is widest. That the social return exceeds the private one is, across these methods, well established.

Objection! You're adding up apples and oranges

The strongest counterargument is that I've quietly stacked the deck. The estimates I've called "convergent" measure different things. Mansfield's 28% is an average return on the entire global stock of academic research; Azoulay's number is a marginal effect identified off idiosyncratic NIH paylines; Gross and Sampat measure a wartime mobilization. These are not the same object, and you can't average them into a single rate of return. Worse, there's a publication-bias worry: striking, large estimates are easier to publish, so a literature can look more unanimous than the underlying reality.

I concede most of this. The convergence is not on a point estimate — it would be a mistake to write "the return to public science is 28%" on the strength of it. The studies differ in what they fund (basic versus applied), when (1940s versus now), and what margin they identify (average versus marginal). And there's a genuine composition warning lurking underneath: Babina and coauthors (2023), in the QJE, show that when federal funding to a field is cut, university patenting can actually rise — but the extra patents are lower-quality and privately assigned, while the basic publications and the startups fall. Count raw patents and you'll misread what funding does to the mix of innovation.

But notice what survives the objection. None of the well-identified estimates collapses to zero, or even to "small." They disagree about magnitude within a band — somewhere between "substantial" and "very large" — not about sign. A skeptic who believed the true return to public science was negligible has to explain why four independent methods, three of them clean natural experiments, all overshoot in the same direction by a lot. The robust object here is not a number; it is a shape: large, positive, social, and systematically undercounted by anything you can measure at the grantee.

What does it clear?

It's worth grounding this in the only comparison that matters for a budget: the hurdle. Public investment is usually asked to beat a real return of a few percent — call it the ~7% that's conventional for long-horizon public capital. Even Mansfield's conservative 10% reading clears that bar; his central 28% clears it several times over; and the modern dollar-per-dollar estimates — Azoulay's finding that $1 of NIH funding generates between $0.70 and $2.13 in lifetime drug sales alone, before any consumer surplus or health benefit — say the same thing in a different currency. You do not need the optimistic end of any of these ranges for public science to look like a good deal. You need only the pessimistic end, and it still clears.

The honest caveat comes from the people who've thought hardest about measuring this. Salter and Martin (2001), reviewing the whole literature, argue that the economic benefits of basic research flow through at least six channels — new knowledge, trained graduates, new instruments and methods, professional networks, problem-solving capacity, and new firms — and that "no simple model of the economic benefits from basic research is possible." A single return-on-investment number is the wrong target. The benefits are too various, too indirect, and too long-delayed to collapse into one rate without throwing most of them away.

Bottom line

I read the evidence this way. That publicly funded science generates a large, positive social return through spillovers to private invention — and that this finding is robust to how you measure it — is about as well-supported as conclusions in this corner of economics get. The reason isn't any single dazzling study; it's that wildly different methods, run by different people in different decades on different programs, triangulate the same answer, and the cleanest designs confirm the suspect old correlations instead of dissolving them. That the social return comfortably exceeds the private one I'd put at the same level of confidence.

The bounds are about size, not sign. I hold the direction and order of magnitude far more firmly than any specific figure: Mansfield's 28% is an average resting on heroic assumptions; Adams's twenty-year lag is a modeling choice; Jaffe's elasticity is a correlation; even the clean modern estimates are marginal effects on particular programs that may not generalize to the next dollar. And Babina's composition result is a standing reminder that what you fund, and what you count, can flip the naive conclusion. So: fairly convincing to well-established on "the return to public science is large, social, and undercounted"; deliberately humble on any precise return to a marginal public-science dollar today. The instinct to distrust the big old numbers was correct. The lesson of the last decade is that distrust, properly armed with natural experiments, mostly vindicated them.

What we don't know

  • Marginal versus average, at today's levels. Nearly every clean estimate is marginal and local; the comforting averages are not. The number a budget fight actually turns on — the return to the next billion dollars at current funding — is the one we have the least direct evidence on.
  • How far it generalizes. The sharp identification is NIH biomedical, DOE energy, and 1940s OSRD. Whether the same magnitudes hold for NSF basic science, climate R&D, or the social sciences is largely untested, and the spillover share surely varies by field.
  • The counterfactual for displaced private research. If public funding partly crowds out private R&D, the net social return is smaller than the gross; Azoulay's evidence of additionality is reassuring but not the last word.
  • Whether the convergence partly reflects shared blind spots. Patents and citations are the common currency of most of these studies, and they share known biases. Convergence across methods that lean on the same proxy is less reassuring than convergence across genuinely independent measures.
  • The composition of what funding buys. Babina and coauthors show funding shifts the open-versus-appropriable, basic-versus-applied mix. We still lack a clean welfare accounting that nets the lost basic science against the gained private patents — which is exactly the calculation a funding cut requires.

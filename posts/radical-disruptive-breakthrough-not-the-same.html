In December 2015, Clayton Christensen did something a little unusual for the man who had coined one of the most successful pieces of business jargon of the previous twenty years. He published an article in the Harvard Business Review, with coauthors Michael Raynor and Rory McDonald, asking everyone to please stop using his word wrong. The word was "disruptive." Christensen, Raynor & McDonald (2015) complained that "many use 'disruptive innovation' to describe any situation in which an industry is shaken up and previously successful incumbents stumble" -- and that "that's much too broad a usage." Uber, the canonical 2010s disruptor, was in their reading not actually a disruptive innovation, because it didn't start in a low-end or new-market foothold the incumbents were happy to cede. A term he had defined precisely in The Innovator's Dilemma (1997) had escaped into the wild and come to mean, roughly, "a big deal."

Here is the thing Christensen's lament hints at but doesn't quite say. "Disruptive" is not the only word doing this job. We also call exceptional innovations "radical," "breakthrough," and "discontinuous," and we use all four more or less interchangeably to mean "an unusually important innovation." The comfortable assumption is that they are loose synonyms -- four hats on the same head. They are not. Underneath the labels sit two genuinely different properties of an invention, and the words quietly disagree about which one they point at. Once you see the two properties separately, a lot of confident claims about "breakthroughs" start to look like claims about different things.

The claim

The words for exceptional innovation -- radical, disruptive, breakthrough, discontinuous -- are not synonyms. They bundle together two distinct properties: how novel an invention is (how far it departs from what came before) and how much impact it has (how much it reshapes markets or later technology). Because novelty and impact are distinct, and because each label and metric weights them differently, the one you pick changes which inventions, and which firms, count as exceptional -- and what you'll conclude they predict.

Why should anyone outside a faculty seminar care? Three reasons. If the labels aren't synonyms, then a literature full of studies that each claim to "measure breakthroughs" may be measuring different animals, so their findings don't stack up into knowledge. A manager told to "be more disruptive" is being told to do one of several different things. And a funding agency that ranks proposals by "radicalness" is quietly ranking them on whichever hidden axis its chosen metric happens to weight -- and could fund a different portfolio simply by relabeling its goal.

The words don't agree

Start with the most direct evidence that this is a real problem and not a pedant's invention. Verhoeven, Kovács, and coauthors (2025), writing in Industrial and Corporate Change, ran a bibliometric analysis of 4,407 articles that use these labels and then read 60 of the field's seminal contributions closely. Two findings stand out. First, the papers flying different flags -- "radical" here, "disruptive" there -- turn out to draw on shared theoretical foundations and cover the same thematic ground; the labels mark dialects, not distinct fields. Second, and more damning, the definitions themselves are a mess: they are often not provided at all, they are inconsistent within a single label (two "radical innovation" papers need not mean the same thing), and they are not distinctive across labels (a "breakthrough" definition and a "radical" definition are frequently indistinguishable). This is not one grumpy reviewer's impression. An independent systematic review by Datta & Srivastava (2023), covering 86 articles from 1990 to 2022, reached the same verdict: "breakthrough" is routinely defined synonymously with radical, disruptive, and discontinuous. When two separate teams comb the literature and both report the same definitional soup, the soup is real. I'd put this part of the claim high on the confidence ladder: it is well established that the labels are used loosely and non-distinctively.

The interesting twist is that this confusion is not new, and the field has periodically tried to fix it -- which tells you the single-label habit is sticky. Thirty-five years ago, Henderson & Clark (1990) pointed out that "radical versus incremental" was already too coarse. They proposed a 2x2: an innovation can change the components of a product, the architecture that links those components, both, or neither -- giving incremental, modular, architectural, and radical innovation as four distinct cells, not a single radical-to-incremental dial. A decade and a half later, Dahlin & Behrens (2005) tried to pin down "radical" itself, and -- crucially for what follows -- their definition needed three criteria: a radical invention is (1) novel, (2) unique, and (3) impactful on later technology. The first two can be judged before the market sees the invention; the third only after. In other words, the single word "radical" was already smuggling in two separate ideas -- how new it is, and how much it ends up mattering.

Two things hiding in one word

That smuggling is the heart of the matter. Verhoeven, Kovács, and coauthors find that when definitions of these labels are offered, they converge on two underlying dimensions -- novelty and impact -- but disagree about how each label maps onto them. So the productive move is to stop arguing about the words and measure the two dimensions separately. The moment you do, you discover they are not the same dimension wearing two names.

The cleanest way to see it: ask whether the most novel inventions are also the most impactful. If novelty and impact were really one property, the answer would be a boring "yes." It isn't. Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016), building patent-based novelty indicators on millions of USPTO patents, found that technological novelty mostly raises the variance of impact and the chance of landing in the extreme positive tail -- not the average. Novel inventions are not reliably better; they are more dispersed. (That risk angle is a whole story of its own, which a companion post takes up; here the point is narrower.) Keijl, Gilsing, Knoben & Duysters (2016), studying every biopharmaceutical patent from 1976 to 2006, found the relationship between recombination -- mixing knowledge from different domains, a standard novelty proxy -- and impact is an inverted U. The highest-impact inventions came from intermediate recombination (a blend of local, adjacent, and distant knowledge), not from the most distant, most novel leaps. Most novel is, on average, not most impactful.

The strongest version comes from Kaplan & Vakili (2015) in the Strategic Management Journal. They used topic modeling -- a text technique that spots when a patent originates a genuinely new idea -- to separate cognitive novelty from economic value, and then asked whether the two have the same source. They don't. Patents that originated new topics tended to come from local search (digging deep in a narrow area), while economic value came from broad recombination. The drivers of "new" and the drivers of "valuable" pull in different directions. Being novel and being impactful are distinct achievements that need not, and often do not, coincide.

A toy example makes the two-dimensional space concrete. Put novelty on one axis and impact on the other, each low or high:

                     LOW impact            HIGH impact
   HIGH novelty   a strange device that    the rare jackpot --
                  flops: "radical" but     novel AND important;
                  not a "breakthrough"     every label agrees

   LOW novelty    incremental tweak --     a modest design that
                  nobody labels it         topples incumbents from
                                           below: "disruptive"
                                           (Christensen) but not
                                           "radical"

Look at the two off-diagonal cells. A genuinely new recombination that never finds a use is high-novelty, low-impact -- it earns "radical" but not "breakthrough." A plain, unoriginal product that quietly displaces incumbents from the low end of the market is low-novelty, high-impact -- it is "disruptive" in Christensen's strict sense but no one would call it "radical." A single word cannot hold both of those inventions at once. That is why the labels fight: they are each grabbing a different corner of a two-dimensional space and insisting it's the whole map. On this -- that novelty and impact are distinct axes -- I'd say the best evidence is fairly convincing: several independent designs, with different data and methods, keep finding the two come apart.

Even "novelty" isn't one thing

It gets one layer worse, in an interesting way. "Novelty" -- the axis I just treated as single -- is itself plural. Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers distinguish Novelty in Recombination (combining existing things in new ways) from Novelty in Knowledge Origins (drawing on genuinely new technological or scientific building blocks), and show the measures are correlated but each carries different information. Strumsky & Lobo (2015) go further, using patent technology codes to identify four distinct sources of novelty -- refining an existing technology, combining new with old, recombining existing ones, and creating a capability from scratch -- and report that plain reuse has recently overtaken recombination as the main engine of new invention. Jung & Lee (2016) add yet another axis they call "originality" of the knowledge a firm searches.

These different flavors of novelty don't predict the same outcomes, which is the whole point. Schoenmakers & Duysters (2010) examined 157 radical patents pulled from a pool of more than 300,000 and found -- against the romantic image of the radical invention springing from nowhere -- that radical inventions are built more on existing knowledge than ordinary ones, just recombined across more domains. Arts & Veugelers (2015) showed, in 26 years of biotech patents, that making new combinations of familiar components raises both the breakthrough rate and average usefulness while lowering the failure rate -- a very different risk profile from reaching for unfamiliar, distant knowledge. And Veugelers & Wang (2019) found that novelty of scientific origin -- papers making new combinations of prior science -- is sizably more likely to have broad technological impact, especially the top 1% most novel science. So "novel" can mean recombine-the-familiar (lower risk, higher hit rate), or reach-for-the- distant (higher variance), or stand-on-new-science (broad downstream reach) -- and these behave differently. The umbrella term hides the very distinctions that do the predictive work. I read this as moderately well supported: consistent across studies, though each flavor rests largely on a single design.

The metric makes the finding

If the words are slippery, surely the numbers are safe? This is where it gets unnerving, because the formal metrics inherit the same two-dimensional confusion -- and the metric you choose can flip the empirical result.

The sharpest demonstration is brand new. Yang (2025) took two of the most popular quantitative measures of exceptional innovation and ran them head-to-head on more than six million USPTO patents from 1980 to 2017. One is a text-based breakthrough index (call it KI), which flags a patent as a breakthrough when it is textually novel relative to past patents and textually similar to future ones -- novelty times impact, baked in. The other is the citation-based disruption index (the CD index, introduced by Funk & Owen-Smith (2017)), which asks whether an invention's followers ignore what it built on -- whether it destabilizes the prior art rather than consolidating it. Both are respectable; both are routinely described as measuring "breakthrough" or "disruptive" innovation.

At the top level, the news is reassuring: KI and CD are highly correlated, and both light up for the same kind of patent -- original, drawing on a narrow, focused knowledge base. But the divergences are systematic, not noise, and they hit exactly the questions people ask of these measures:

  • Trend. The CD index has declined steadily over the decades. The KI index has not -- it fluctuates with the business cycle instead. Ask "is innovation getting less disruptive over time?" and your answer depends entirely on which metric you grabbed. (Whether the CD decline is real or a citation artifact is a live and separate debate -- see the related post below; here it just illustrates that the metrics disagree.)
  • What it predicts. KI is positively related to a patent's future citation impact. CD has a U-shaped relation to impact -- the highest-CD patents are not the most cited. The two metrics make opposite predictions about the value of a "breakthrough."
  • Who makes them. Small, remote teams score higher on KI but lower on CD. So the question "do small teams produce more breakthroughs?" has the answer "yes" or "no" depending only on the column you read.

A second, older study makes the same point from the construct-validity side. Govindarajan & Kopalle (2006) built a careful survey scale for "disruptiveness," fielded it to senior executives at 199 business units across 38 Fortune 500 firms, and showed statistically that disruptiveness is a distinct construct from radicalness -- they don't collapse into one factor. The measurement literature has been telling us, politely, for two decades that these are different things.

Here's a back-of-the-envelope to feel why a high correlation is cold comfort. "Breakthrough" lives in the extreme tail -- the top few percent -- and correlations that look strong in the middle get weak in the tails. Suppose two breakthrough scores correlate at 0.7, which is high for this literature, and you take each one's top 5% of patents. The math of overlapping tails implies the two "top 5%" lists share only on the order of a third of their members. So a funder that switched its screen from disruption (CD) to breakthrough (KI) would re-pick a large chunk of its "exceptional" portfolio -- and, going by Yang's team-size result, would systematically swap toward small, remote teams. The two metrics agree about the broad ranking and disagree about precisely the cases the whole exercise is meant to find. I'd mark this claim -- the metric changes the answer -- as supported but resting heavily on one head-to-head study (Yang) plus the discriminant-validity evidence; I'd love to see the horse race rerun on other metric pairs and other outcomes.

Objection: isn't this just semantics?

The strongest pushback runs like this: This is academic hair-splitting. Everyone knows the words loosely mean "a big deal," the formal metrics are highly correlated anyway, and in practice it all comes out in the wash. It deserves a real answer, because the first two halves of it are true.

Concede the correlation: Yang really does find KI and CD strongly related, and for a coarse "is this patent roughly exceptional?" sort, picking either is fine. Concede the loose usage: in conversation, "breakthrough" as a synonym for "important" is harmless.

But the divergences are not random scatter you can average away -- they are structured. They go the same direction every time (CD down over time, KI flat; small teams up on KI, down on CD; opposite impact curves). Structured disagreement is exactly what survives aggregation and contaminates conclusions. The moment your question is about a trend, about who innovates, or about what an invention predicts -- which is to say, almost any interesting question -- the choice of label or metric flips the answer. The correlation rescues you only if all you ever wanted was a rough ranking. Christensen's Uber complaint is just the boardroom version of the same failure: call the wrong thing "disruptive" and you draw the wrong strategic lesson from it.

And there's an honest complication that cuts deeper than semantics, because it shows the two dimensions can actively point in opposite directions. Nemet & Johnson (2012) tested the cherished idea that important inventions draw on knowledge from distant technological domains. Using US patents from 1976 to 2006, they found the opposite: citing external, distant prior art was a weaker predictor of an invention's future impact than citing technologically close prior art -- and the effect was strongest among the most-cited patents. If you equate novelty (distant origins) with impact, this result is a paradox. If you keep them as two separate axes, it's simply telling you that on the impact axis, distant novelty can be a liability. The conflation doesn't just blur the picture; it can invert it.

Bottom line

I read the evidence as supporting a graded conclusion. That the labels -- radical, disruptive, breakthrough, discontinuous -- are used loosely and are not distinctively defined is, at this point, about as settled as a claim about a literature gets: two independent systematic reviews and the originator of "disruptive" all say so. That novelty and impact are two genuinely different dimensions, rather than one property under several names, is fairly convincing -- it shows up across measurement studies, the inverted-U of recombination and impact, decoupled antecedents of novelty and value, and a 35-year history of taxonomies that keep needing more than one axis. That the specific metric you choose can flip an empirical finding is supported but newer, leaning on one careful head-to-head plus discriminant-validity work; it would be good to see it replicated across more metric pairs.

So the practical rule is modest and, I think, hard to argue with: don't say "breakthrough" (or radical, or disruptive) and expect it to carry an argument by itself. Say which dimension you mean -- how novel, or how impactful -- and, if you're putting a number on it, say which metric and accept that a different metric might rank the inventions differently. The labels are fine as conversational shorthand. They are not fine as measurements, and they are not interchangeable when something rides on the answer.

What I would not claim: that there is one correct taxonomy everyone should adopt, or that the words should be banned. They carry useful connotations -- "disruptive" rightly evokes market displacement, "radical" a technological leap. The error is treating those connotations as one thing.

What we don't know

  • Will a consensus taxonomy stick? Verhoeven, Kovács, and coauthors propose treating novelty and impact as separate axes and mapping out the trajectories between them. Whether the field actually adopts a shared scheme -- or whether text-and-embedding measures keep proliferating, each with its own implicit definition -- is genuinely open.
  • Do the distinctions matter for the outcomes managers care about? Most of this evidence uses patent citations as "impact." Whether the same novelty-versus-impact splits show up in commercial outcomes -- profit, market share, survival -- is much less studied, and citations are an imperfect stand-in for value.
  • Which metric is "right" for which question? Knowing that KI and CD diverge is not the same as knowing which to trust when. We lack a map from "the question you're asking" to "the measure you should use," and building one would do more good than inventing a fifteenth index.
  • How much of the CD decline is real? The steady fall in the disruption index over time is either a profound fact about innovation or an artifact of how citations have inflated. That debate is unsettled, and it matters for anyone reading trend claims about "disruption."

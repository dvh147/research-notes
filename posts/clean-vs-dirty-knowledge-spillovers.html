Imagine you run an innovation agency, and you can do exactly one thing today: nudge a single research dollar out of an internal-combustion-engine lab and into a battery lab. Now set the climate completely aside — pretend, for the length of this thought experiment, that carbon is free. Is moving that dollar still a good idea?

Almost every argument for subsidizing clean technology never gets to ask this question, because almost every argument rests entirely on the carbon externality: burning fossil fuels dumps a cost on everyone that the polluter doesn't pay, the market ignores that cost, so we tax carbon — or, when we can't, we subsidize the clean alternatives that let us emit less. On that logic, if carbon were somehow free, the whole case for favoring clean R&D would evaporate.

This post is about a second, quieter reason to favor clean R&D — one that has nothing to do with carbon. The idea is that a dollar spent inventing clean technology throws off more useful knowledge for everyone else to build on than a dollar spent inventing the dirty technology it would replace. If that's true, clean innovation is under-supplied by private markets for the same reason all knowledge is under-supplied — inventors can't capture everything they teach the rest of us — but more so for clean. Green R&D support would then be partly a free lunch: it would buy economic growth, not only lower emissions. And the case would survive even in that imaginary world where carbon is free.

So here is the claim I want to weigh: clean knowledge spills over more than dirty knowledge, which gives an innovation-policy reason to subsidize clean R&D that stands on its own, separate from the climate case. I read the evidence as genuinely supportive — but resting on a thinner base than the confident version of the argument lets on, and I'll try to be precise about how much we actually know.

Why the gap is what matters, not the level

Start with why a difference between clean and dirty spillovers — rather than the sheer size of either — is the thing that should move policy.

In a reasonably competitive economy, firms push R&D into whatever field pays them best, until the private return on the last dollar is about the same everywhere. (If batteries paid a firm more than engines at the margin, it would move money from engines to batteries until that gap closed.) The implication, which Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen put at the center of their paper, is sharp: shifting the composition of R&D with a subsidy can only improve welfare if the external returns — the part that spills to others and that no firm prices in — differ across fields. If clean and dirty spilled over equally, reshuffling R&D between them would be a wash.

That external return is real and, in general, large. The workhorse estimate comes from Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013), who untangle the two things a firm's R&D does to other firms — a positive knowledge spillover and a negative "business-stealing" effect on product-market rivals — and find that the knowledge spillovers dominate, so that the gross social return to R&D is at least twice the private return. The tool they and almost everyone else lean on is the patent citation: when patent B cites patent A, it is leaving a paper trail that it built on A's knowledge. Jaffe, Trajtenberg, and Henderson (1993) put that tool on the map, showing that citations cluster geographically near the cited invention more than chance would predict — evidence that knowledge really does flow along those links, and that it is stickier close to home (a fact I'll come back to).

So spillovers are big, and citations are a usable ruler for them. The live question is narrower: does a clean invention leave a longer, richer trail than a dirty one?

The core finding: clean patents are cited about 43% more

The most direct attempt to answer that is the Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen study, which compares knowledge spillovers from clean, dirty, and other emerging technologies in two domains — energy production and transportation — using patent citations. Their wrinkle is to follow the whole downstream citation tree rather than just first-generation cites: a clever invention is one whose children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren in the citation network are many and important. They borrow Google's PageRank algorithm — the same idea used to rank web pages by the links pointing at them — to score each patent's place in that tree.

Their headline: conditional on a wide range of confounders, clean patents receive on average 43% more citations than dirty patents, and across their various spillover measures they "consistently find up to 40% higher levels of spillovers from clean technologies." Two further results matter for how seriously to take this. First, the magnitude is not a rounding error in the world of technology: clean spillovers come out "comparable in scale to those observed in the IT sector" — i.e., clean tech looks less like a mature industry and more like a young, fertile, general-purpose field. Second, when they switch from counting citations to using firm financial data, the market value tied to clean spillovers is also greater — a second ruler pointing the same way.

Why would clean spill over more? Their explanation is that clean technologies are, at this stage, radically novel, while dirty inventions are comparatively incremental — another tweak to a combustion engine that has been refined for a century versus a genuinely new way to store or convert energy. Radically new knowledge opens more downstream doors, so more later inventors walk through them. That's a satisfying mechanism, and it does double duty: it predicts the spillover gap could shrink as clean technologies themselves mature, which is worth holding onto.

How confident should we be in the 43%? This is a careful working paper with extensive robustness checks, and it is corroborated in direction by the market-value cross-check. But it is one study, the cleanest estimates come from a single closely-linked research group, and — as I'll discuss below — patent citations are an imperfect ruler, especially for young technologies. I'd call this the best evidence we have, and fairly convincing on direction, but not yet the kind of replicated, multi-team result that earns unqualified language.

It shows up in how innovation actually moves

A spillover number measured off citation trees would be more convincing if the same pattern showed up in firms' real behavior. It does.

The richest evidence is from the auto industry. Aghion, Dechezleprêtre, Hémous, Martin, and Van Reenen (2016) built a firm-level panel of clean (electric, hybrid, hydrogen) and dirty (internal-combustion) patents across 80 countries over several decades, and showed that firms innovate more in clean and less in dirty when they face higher tax-inclusive fuel prices — the direction of innovation bends to the carbon-relevant price. Crucially for our claim, they also find strong path dependence: a firm's propensity to invent clean rises both with its own past clean inventions and with the aggregate stock of clean knowledge around it. That second channel is a spillover, and it means clean innovation feeds future clean innovation. Where spillovers are strong, history compounds.

This is the directed-technical-change picture sketched a few years earlier by Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn, and Hémous (2012), whose model has clean and dirty inputs competing and innovation flowing toward whichever is more profitable to improve; in it, temporary, well-aimed taxes and subsidies can permanently redirect technical change toward clean precisely because of that compounding; Acemoglu, Aghion, Barrage, and Hémous (2023) have since restated the same case for today's clean transition. The induced-innovation reflex is older still: Popp (2002) showed with US patents from 1970–1994 that higher energy prices reliably call forth more energy-efficient innovation. Innovation goes where the incentives point — and the spillover structure decides how far each push echoes.

And when the push comes from public money aimed at energy, the echo is loud. Myers and Lanahan (2022) studied US Department of Energy grants to small firms and, using state-level variation in matching policies for identification, estimate that for every patent a grant recipient produces, about three more are produced by others who benefit from the spillovers — much of it in technological areas quite different from the one funded. That 3-to-1 ratio is a concrete, design-based reading of how generative clean-adjacent R&D is. (Hold onto one more number from that paper: 60% of those spillovers stay within the United States. It's about to matter.)

"Compared to what?" — and why the growth-versus-climate worry seems not to bite

Here is the strongest objection to everything above, and it deserves its own section.

"Clean spills over 43% more than dirty" is the wrong comparison for a policymaker. No agency reallocates R&D from engines to batteries one dollar at a time. When you subsidize clean, the money you pull in comes from the whole portfolio of things society might have invented — software, materials, pharmaceuticals, machine tools — not from dirty technology specifically. The honest benchmark is: does a clean R&D dollar spill over more than the average, untargeted R&D dollar? If clean beats dirty but loses to semiconductors, then steering R&D toward clean might cut carbon while quietly lowering total knowledge spillovers — a real growth-versus-climate tradeoff.

Martin and Verhoeven (2023) take exactly this benchmark, estimating field-specific spillover returns and then simulating targeted versus untargeted R&D support. Their answer is reassuring for the claim, with an important asterisk. Supporting clean turns out to be a "win-win," with global returns about one-eighth higher than an untargeted policy, and they "identify no explicit innovation policy tradeoff in tackling the twin challenges of economic growth and climate change." So the worry — that going green means leaving growth on the table — does not appear to bite: clean clears not just the low bar (dirty) but the fair bar (the average dollar).

Notice, though, how much smaller "+one-eighth versus the average" is than "+43% versus dirty." Both can be true — dirty is a low bar, the portfolio average is a high one — and the gap between the two numbers is a useful corrective against over-selling. The knowledge case for clean R&D is real but, against the right benchmark, modest.

The leak: clean spillovers cross borders

The same paper adds a twist that changes who should pay. Only a modest share of clean spillover returns stays inside the borders of the country whose taxpayers funded the research; the rest leaks abroad. That lines up with Myers and Lanahan's 60%-stays-home figure for US energy R&D, and with the old Jaffe–Trajtenberg–Henderson finding that spillovers are localized but national borders are porous and grow more so over time.

The policy consequence is sharp. A global planner, seeing large clean spillovers, would subsidize clean R&D heavily. But each national government captures only part of the benefit and pays all of the cost, so each under-invests — a free-rider problem layered on top of the ordinary knowledge externality. Martin and Verhoeven estimate that supranational coordination could raise returns by roughly 25% for the EU and more than 60% globally, and they note the EU benefits substantially from US clean innovation spillovers — which reframes a subsidy program like the US Inflation Reduction Act not purely as a competitive threat to European industry but partly as a gift of spillovers to everyone downstream. This is one study leaning on simulations, so I hold the exact numbers loosely; the direction — national policy under-provides clean R&D more than global accounting alone implies — follows from well-established facts about where citations land.

Objection! Clean and dirty aren't separate knowledge bases

A second objection cuts at the framing. I've been talking as if clean and dirty were two separate reservoirs of knowledge. They aren't. Jee and Srivastav (2024) map the clean–dirty citation network directly and find that fewer than one in ten clean patents cite a dirty patent directly, but nearly two-thirds are linked indirectly, often through bridging fields like control systems, data processing, and heat-and-waste management. Knowledge built for engines can resurface, several citation-steps later, inside a battery system.

Does this sink the claim? No — and it actually sharpens it. The clean/dirty comparison is about how much each generates for others, not about whether the two pools touch. If anything, the low rate of direct clean-from-dirty borrowing is the flip side of the path dependence Aghion and co-authors measured: when clean draws little directly from the dirty stock, a head start in dirty doesn't automatically carry over, and the direction of technology can lock in. Weak cross-pollination is part of why a temporary nudge toward clean can have lasting effects. The two literatures are telling the same story from opposite ends.

Objection! Citations are a noisy ruler

The deeper worry is the ruler itself. Patent citations are added partly by inventors and partly by patent examiners; Alcácer and Gittelman (2006) document that on the average patent about two-thirds of citations are inserted by examiners, which adds noise to any inference about what inventors actually learned from. And clean technologies are, on average, younger than dirty ones, so they've had less time to accumulate citations — naively, that biases against finding a clean advantage, but the corrections for it are exactly where a citation-truncation skeptic should push.

I take this seriously, and it's the main reason I won't put the headline number in strong language. But two things keep the claim standing. First, the clean advantage shows up even after conditioning on the usual confounders, and the youth-of-clean bias runs the "wrong" way (against the result), so the raw finding is if anything conservative. Second, the market-value cross-check in the original study doesn't rely on citation counts at all and points the same direction. Convergent evidence from a second, independent ruler is what turns "an artifact of citation-counting" from a fatal objection into a caveat.

What it means for policy: two failures, two instruments

Put the pieces together and the policy logic is clean (sorry). There are two distinct market failures around dirty technology. One is the carbon externality — a price problem, whose textbook fix is a carbon price. The other is a knowledge externality that is larger for clean than for the average alternative — a quantity-of-innovation problem, whose fix is R&D support aimed at clean. Two failures call for two instruments, and using only one leaves welfare on the table. That is precisely what the quantitative models conclude: both Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn, and Hémous (2012) and Acemoglu, Akcigit, Hanley, and Kerr's estimated "Transition to Clean Technology" model find the optimal path leans on both research subsidies and carbon taxes, and that relying on carbon prices alone — or delaying — carries significant welfare costs.

Is the R&D-subsidy lever real, or just a modeling convenience? It's real. Dechezleprêtre, Einiö, Martin, Nguyen, and Van Reenen (2023) use a sharp regression-discontinuity in R&D tax-relief eligibility to show that tax incentives causally raise firms' patenting and spill over to their technological neighbors — subsidies both work and themselves generate the spillovers we're trying to buy more of. And the broader point that composition matters, not just the total, is exactly what Akcigit, Hanley, and Serrano-Velarde (2021) find for basic versus applied research: uniform subsidies misallocate by over-funding the lower-spillover type. Clean versus dirty is another instance of the same lesson.

How big should the clean subsidy be? Here's a deliberately crude back-of-the-envelope to fix scale, not to be quoted as a parameter. Anchor the social return on a dirty R&D dollar at $1.50 — modest relative to Bloom and co-authors' "at least twice the private return." If clean spills over on the order of 40% more, the clean dollar returns roughly $2.10 to society. Reallocating one dollar from dirty to clean then adds about $0.60 of social value before counting any climate benefit at all — and against the fairer "average dollar" benchmark, the per-dollar bonus is smaller, closer to Martin and Verhoeven's one-eighth. The honest takeaway isn't a number; it's that the knowledge case adds a real, positive increment on top of the carbon case, large enough to matter and small enough that it complements rather than replaces carbon pricing. (For calibration, targeted R&D subsidy programs have been estimated to carry social returns in the 30–50% range — real, but not bottomless.)

Bottom line

I come down on the side of the claim, with bounded confidence. The proposition that clean knowledge spills over more than dirty knowledge is supported by the most direct study we have (a 43% citation premium, echoed by a market-value premium), by a believable mechanism (clean is radically novel, dirty is incremental), and by the way real innovation bends toward clean and compounds through spillovers. The further, policy-relevant proposition — that clean even beats the average R&D dollar, so there's no growth-versus-climate tradeoff — has one careful study behind it and survives the sharpest objections, but it is one study, and the margin against the right benchmark is modest, not dramatic. From the theory side, the conclusion that this justifies a clean R&D subsidy distinct from carbon pricing is on very solid ground; the right size of that subsidy is genuinely uncertain.

If you want the one-sentence version: the climate case for clean R&D is the headline, but there's a second, carbon-independent reason hiding underneath it — clean ideas are unusually generative — and it points the same way, which is part of why economists who study this keep landing on "price carbon and subsidize clean research," not one or the other.

What we don't know

  • Is the 43% robust out of sample and out of one research group? The cleanest clean-versus-dirty spillover estimates come from a tightly linked set of authors using patent citations. Independent teams, other countries, and non-citation measures (licensing, worker flows, product-level diffusion) would tell us whether the premium is a fact about clean technology or a fact about citation data.
  • Does the gap shrink as clean matures? If the advantage comes from radical novelty, today's frontier clean fields should lose their edge as they become as incremental as engines once they're mature. We don't yet have a good time series on that.
  • How large is the leak, exactly, and what does it imply for instrument design? The cross-border share is estimated in one simulation-based study. Better numbers would tell us how much of the action should be international coordination versus national subsidy.
  • What's the right magnitude? Every optimal-subsidy figure here is model-dependent. The qualitative case ("subsidize clean research on top of pricing carbon") is robust; the quantitative one is not.

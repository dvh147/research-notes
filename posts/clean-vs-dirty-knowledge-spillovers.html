Revised 19 November 2026, after checking every number against the full texts and adding newer studies. The main changes: the headline now comes from the latest version of the core paper; a market-value check I had called independent turned out to rest on citations too; independent studies now find a green premium; and the cross-border leak is sharper than I first said.

Imagine you run an innovation agency, and you can do exactly one thing today: nudge a single research dollar out of an internal-combustion-engine lab and into a battery lab. Now set the climate aside: pretend carbon is free. Is moving that dollar still a good idea?

Most arguments for subsidizing clean technology never ask, because they rest on the carbon externality: burning fossil fuels imposes a cost the polluter doesn't pay, so we tax carbon or subsidize the clean alternatives. If carbon were free, that case would evaporate.

This post is about a second, quieter reason that has nothing to do with carbon: a dollar spent inventing clean technology may throw off more useful knowledge for others to build on than a dollar spent on the dirty technology it would replace. If so, clean innovation is under-supplied for the same reason all knowledge is — inventors can't capture everything they teach the rest of us — but more so, and green R&D support buys some growth as well as lower emissions.

So here is the claim I want to weigh: clean knowledge spills over more than dirty knowledge, which gives an innovation-policy reason to subsidize clean R&D on top of pricing carbon. Having read the papers in full, I find the first half better supported than I first thought and the second half more conditional: clean beats the average research dollar but not the best one, and most of its spillovers land in other countries.

Why the gap is what matters, not the level

In a reasonably competitive economy, firms push R&D into whatever field pays them best, until the private return on the last dollar is about the same everywhere. Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen put the implication at the center of their paper: shifting the composition of R&D can only raise welfare if the external returns — the part that spills to others and that no firm prices in — differ across fields. If clean and dirty spilled over equally, reshuffling R&D between them would be a wash.

Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013) follow US firms from 1980 to 2001 and place each one in technology space, by the fields it patents in, and in product-market space, by the industries it sells in. Using changes in federal and state R&D tax credits to shift firms' R&D, they separate the knowledge a firm picks up from technological neighbours from the sales it loses to rivals, and find that the knowledge spillovers dominate, so that the gross social return to R&D is at least twice the private return — about 55% against 21% in their main estimates.

To trace spillovers between particular technologies, most studies use a different tool: the patent citation, a paper trail suggesting that the citing patent built on the cited one. Jaffe, Trajtenberg, and Henderson (1993) put that tool on the map, showing that citations come disproportionately from the same country, state, and city as the patents they cite, beyond what the existing geography of research would predict.

So does a clean invention leave a longer, richer trail than a dirty one?

The core finding: clean inventions are cited a lot more

The most direct answer comes from Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen; I use the latest version of their working paper, from May 2022. They take more than a million inventions patented between 1950 and 2012 in two fields where clean and dirty can be told apart — electricity production (renewables against fossil-fuel plants) and transport (electric, hybrid, and hydrogen cars against combustion engines) — and compare how often clean and dirty inventions filed at the same patent office in the same year are cited later, holding fixed signs of each invention's quality such as the number of countries it was filed in and whether it was granted. Beyond counting citations, they score each invention with a PageRank-style index — the idea Google used to rank web pages — that also credits citations from inventions that are themselves heavily cited.

The authors sum up their result as "up to 60% higher levels of spillovers" from clean technologies, and their text puts the citation gap at 60–68%. Their main table implies more: converted exactly, its coefficient means clean inventions receive close to 90% more citations. Text and tables in this working paper don't always agree, so I would not lean on any single figure; "about two-thirds more, possibly much more" is a fair reading. (Earlier versions, still widely cited, reported 43% in 2014 and up to 40% in 2017.) The gap holds when they drop citations added by patent examiners rather than inventors, remove self-citations, and compare inventions by the same inventor. Estimated cohort by cohort, it has grown since the early 1980s, when it was slightly negative.

This is a comparison across inventions, not an experiment: clean inventions are built upon more than dirty ones of similar observable quality from the same office and year.

Why would clean spill over more? The authors' explanation is that clean technologies are radically new, while dirty inventions are incremental tweaks to engines and power plants refined for a century. Two of their own results support it. When they control for how old an invention's technology field is, the clean premium in the PageRank score roughly halves. And when they compare clean directly with other young fields, it is not clearly different from IT, artificial intelligence, wireless, biotechnology, nanotechnology, robotics, or 3D printing; only aerospace clearly trails it. The authors concede the point in their conclusion: the case for targeted support "could equally be justified" for emerging areas such as artificial intelligence, nanotechnologies, or IT. On this evidence, the premium belongs to young, radical fields, of which clean is one.

Other teams find a green premium too

All of the above comes from one closely linked group of authors. Three studies by other researchers now speak to the question. None compares clean with dirty directly; each compares green inventions with non-green ones in general, the tougher benchmark discussed below.

Barbieri, Marzucchi, and Rizzo (2020) take about a million patent families filed at both the European and US patent offices between 1980 and 2012 and compare green with non-green families in the same narrow technology class: the green ones receive about 32% more citations within five years, still about 20% more after controlling for how complex and novel they are, and they are cited from a wider spread of fields. Buzzacchi, Croce, De Marco, and Ughetto (2026) take all 3.8 million applications to the European Patent Office from 1981 to 2020, about 8% of them tagged as climate-related, and compare each with non-green applications filed in the same year and technology field. The green ones receive about 24% more citations — about 22% more when citations of citations are counted too — and are cited from a slightly wider range of fields. The raw averages show almost no gap; it appears once filing year and field are held fixed.

A third study narrows the lens to firms. Lupi, Rentocchini, and Rizzo (2026), using US patenting firms from 1976 to 2019, find that inventions by green start-ups collect more citations than those of other start-ups — about 20% more within three years and 8% more within seven — a premium concentrated in green technologies that fades as the firms mature.

How much does this add? A good deal on robustness to who is counting: different teams, offices, periods, and controls all find green inventions cited roughly 20–30% more than comparable non-green ones. Little on robustness to the ruler: all of them use patent citations. I'd call the green premium fairly convincing — consistent across several careful correlational studies, though none is a natural experiment. The larger clean-versus-dirty gap rests on one research group, but it is what you would expect if dirty technologies sit at or below the average, as that group finds.

It shows up in how innovation actually moves

A premium read off citations is more convincing if it shows up in behavior. It does.

First, the direction of invention follows incentives and the existing stock of clean knowledge. Aghion, Dechezleprêtre, Hémous, Martin, and Van Reenen (2016) follow about 3,400 car-industry patent holders' clean (electric, hybrid, hydrogen) and dirty (combustion-engine) patents. Firms patent, and presumably sell, in different countries, so each faces its own mix of tax-inclusive fuel prices; the authors fix that mix from where each firm patented in 1965–85 and track its patenting as prices in 25 countries move over 1986–2005. They find that firms invent more clean and less dirty technology when the fuel prices they face are higher — a 10% higher price goes with about 10% more clean patents and 6% fewer dirty ones — and that a firm's clean invention is associated with both its own past clean inventions and the clean knowledge of other firms. That second link looks like a spillover: clean innovation feeding future clean innovation. Popp (2002) found the same reflex in US patents from 1970 to 1994: higher energy prices call forth more energy-efficient inventions.

This is the directed-technical-change picture that Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn, and Hémous (2012) formalized: clean and dirty inputs compete, innovation flows toward whichever is more profitable to improve, and, when the two inputs are sufficiently substitutable, temporary taxes and subsidies can redirect technical change toward clean for good, because once clean is far enough ahead, research keeps flowing to it without help.

Second, energy R&D throws off large spillovers even when they are measured without citations. Myers and Lanahan (2022) use the fact that some states top up federal Department of Energy grants to small firms and others don't, so how much money a firm gets depends partly on where it happens to be. With a way of detecting spillovers that does not rely on citations, they estimate that for every patent a grant recipient produces, about three more are produced by others who benefit from spillovers, many in technologies quite different from the one funded, with foreign patentees producing about 41% of all the extra patents. But the grants come from every part of the department, fossil-energy and nuclear offices included, so this shows that energy R&D spills over a lot, not that clean spills over more than dirty.

"Compared to what?"

Here is the strongest objection. "Clean beats dirty" is the wrong comparison for a policymaker: a clean subsidy draws money and researchers from the whole portfolio — software, materials, pharmaceuticals — not from dirty technology specifically. The honest benchmark is the average research dollar. If clean beats dirty but loses to semiconductors, steering R&D toward clean could cut carbon while lowering total spillovers: a real growth-versus-climate tradeoff.

The independent studies above already take this benchmark, and so do two more. Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen compare every group with all other inventions and find that dirty energy technologies spill over less than the average invention, while clean ones spill over more, alongside other young fields. Martin and Verhoeven (2023) value each patent's spillovers by the value of the later inventions that build on it, directly or through chains of citations, and then compute the return to a dollar of R&D support in each field. Support targeted at clean returns about 135% worldwide, against 120% for support spread across fields in proportion to their size — an edge of about one-eighth, which ranks clean second of six broad fields — and the authors "identify no explicit innovation policy tradeoff" between growth and climate. The two studies share an author, Ralf Martin, and the same citation ruler, so they are not independent confirmations.

So the tradeoff worry doesn't bite against the average dollar. But clean is not the best place for a research dollar: in Martin and Verhoeven, electrical engineering — telecommunications, computers, audio-visual technology — returns about 168%, and in Dechezleprêtre and co-authors, clean does no better than IT, artificial intelligence, or wireless. A planner who cared only about growth would not put every marginal dollar into clean. The knowledge case for clean is that it beats the average, not that it beats everything.

Popp and Newell (2012) point to a hidden cost. They link US firms' patents to their finances and find that alternative-energy patents are cited more often, and by a wider range of technologies, than the same firms' other patents — another sign of larger spillovers — but also that a firm that patents more in alternative energy patents less in other fields. They find no such crowding out across industries; within firms, part of the clean dollar is a moved dollar.

The leak: most clean spillovers cross borders

Martin and Verhoeven add a twist that changes who should pay. Because they know where each citing invention was made, they can ask how much of a country's clean spillover value stays at home. On average, about 23% does — no less than for innovation in general — and the rest lands abroad. Counting only domestic returns, clean support returns barely more than the average field, about 30 cents per dollar against 28: its advantage grows in the EU, shrinks in Japan and China, and vanishes in the United States.

Independent evidence points the same way. In Buzzacchi and co-authors' European data, green patents are cited by inventors about 12% farther away than comparable non-green patents, and a share of their citations about 3.6 percentage points smaller comes from the same country (measured on citations made by inventors in Europe). Newer work by Edet, Martin, de Rochambeau, Tran, and Verhoeven (2026) finds the same pattern for developing economies: green R&D by their inventors returns about 93% against 61% for all technologies when spillovers anywhere are counted, but only about one percentage point more when counting just the spillovers that reach other developing-economy inventors.

The first version of this post said this "lines up with" Myers and Lanahan's figure that 60% of the spillovers from US energy grants stay in the United States. That figure counts the grant recipients' own patents; among the spillover patents alone, by my reading of their tables, a bit under half go to US patentees — closer to, though still above, Martin and Verhoeven's roughly 32% home share for the United States. The two measure different things in different ways, and agree that a large part of the benefit leaves the country that paid.

The policy consequence is sharp. A global planner, seeing large clean spillovers, would subsidize clean R&D heavily. A national government captures only part of the benefit and pays all the cost, so it under-invests — a free-rider problem on top of the ordinary knowledge externality. Martin and Verhoeven simulate coordinated policies and find that coordination raises the returns to clean R&D support by about 25% within the EU, about 40% across the OECD, and more than 60% worldwide; the worldwide gain comes mostly from directing support to Chinese clean innovation, which would receive over 80% of subsidies under a fully global policy. They also find that the EU benefits strongly from US clean innovation, which casts the US Inflation Reduction Act as partly a gift of spillovers to Europe. These are simulations from one study, so I hold the exact numbers loosely; the direction follows from where citations land.

Objection! Clean and dirty aren't separate knowledge bases

I've been treating clean and dirty as two separate reservoirs of knowledge. They aren't. Jee and Srivastav (2024) trace citation paths between them and find that fewer than one in ten clean patents cite a dirty patent directly, but nearly two-thirds are linked indirectly, often through bridging fields like control systems, data processing, and the management of heat and waste.

That sharpens the claim rather than sinking it. The comparison is about how much each kind of knowledge generates for others, not whether the two pools touch. And the low rate of direct borrowing is, as Jee and Srivastav note, the flip side of path dependence: a head start in dirty doesn't carry over to clean, so the direction of technology can lock in, which is why a temporary nudge toward clean can last.

Objection! Citations are a noisy ruler

The deeper worry is the ruler itself. Patent citations are added partly by inventors and partly by examiners; Alcácer and Gittelman (2006), using a change in US reporting that shows who added each citation, find that examiners insert about two-thirds of the citations on the average patent. The clean premium survives dropping examiner citations, which answers the narrowest version of this worry.

The first version of this post leaned on two further defenses that don't survive the full texts. It said the citation premium was "echoed" by a market-value premium measured without citations. In the version that claim came from, the market-value test was itself built from citations — it asked whether firms whose patents cite clean patents are worth more — and in the latest version, which relates firms' stock-market value to their clean and dirty patents, the authors find no difference in the average private value of clean and dirty inventions. It also said that because clean patents are younger (22 years old on average, against 27 for dirty ones), the bias from having had less time to be cited runs against the result. The authors themselves say the direction of that bias is not obvious: younger fields may also offer more room for breakthroughs.

Measuring "green" is a second weak spot. Which patents count as green depends on classification codes that change between database releases: Barbieri, Hötte, and Persoon (2025) find that reclassification and wider coverage add about 9% and 10% to the count of green patents, and that the newly visible ones are more novel and more cited. Santarlasci, Rungi, and Zinilli (2025) read patent texts and conclude that only about 18.5% of patents classified as green by their codes are genuinely green. Neither overturns the premium, but both mean its exact size depends on definitions as well as on economics.

So the clean premium is well documented in citations and not yet shown in any other currency. The one study here that measures spillovers without citations, Myers and Lanahan's, finds that citation links would have missed about half of them — but it does not split clean from dirty.

What it means for policy: two failures, two instruments

There are two distinct market failures around dirty technology. One is the carbon externality — a price problem, whose textbook fix is a carbon price. The other is a knowledge externality that is larger for clean than for dirty and somewhat larger than for the average alternative — a quantity-of-innovation problem, whose fix is R&D support aimed at clean. Two failures call for two instruments. The best-known models also land on two instruments, but by another route. In Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn, and Hémous (2012) and in Acemoglu, Akcigit, Hanley, and Kerr's model of the US energy sector, estimated on firm data, clean and dirty research work alike; the subsidy exists to pull innovation away from carbon, and both find a carbon tax alone costly. Without climate damages, the second model's planner would not subsidize clean research at all. The carbon-independent case rests on the empirical premium, and Dechezleprêtre, Martin, and Mohnen draw it themselves: their results "justify research subsidies targeted specifically at clean technologies even if carbon is adequately priced."

Is the R&D-subsidy lever real? Dechezleprêtre, Einiö, Martin, Nguyen, and Van Reenen (2023) compare UK firms just below and just above a size threshold for more generous R&D tax relief and find that the relief raises firms' patenting and spills over to technologically close firms. Composition matters too: Akcigit, Hanley, and Serrano-Velarde (2021), estimating a growth model on French firms, find that uniform research subsidies oversubsidize applied research relative to basic research. One theoretical warning: Karydas (2017) shows in a growth model that, once innovations take time to diffuse, the wide applicability of green innovations does not by itself justify higher research subsidies. More citations are not automatically more welfare.

How big is the knowledge bonus? A crude calculation fixes the scale. Bloom and co-authors' main estimates put the private return to a dollar of R&D at about 21 cents and the social return at about 55 cents, so roughly 34 cents spill over to others. Suppose that is what a dirty research dollar throws off, and that a clean dollar's spillovers are 60% larger: about 54 cents. Moving a dollar from dirty to clean then adds roughly 20 cents of social return before counting any climate benefit. Against the average dollar the bonus is smaller — Martin and Verhoeven's 135% against 120% puts it at about 15 cents per dollar of support, counted worldwide — and for a single country counting only its own returns it is close to zero. (For calibration, targeted R&D subsidies in Finland have been estimated to earn social returns of 30–50%.) The knowledge case adds a real increment on top of the carbon case: large enough to matter, too small to replace a carbon price.

Bottom line

I come down on the side of the claim, with bounded confidence. Three independent studies find green inventions cited roughly 20–30% more than comparable non-green ones, and the one study that compares clean with dirty directly finds a gap of about two-thirds or more. The weak points: all of it is measured in citations, and the premium seems to belong to young fields in general rather than to clean ones in particular.

That clean beats the average research dollar — so there is no growth-versus-climate tradeoff against untargeted support — now has independent backing, but the margin is modest, and clean does not beat the best fields. And because most clean spillovers cross borders, the knowledge case for clean R&D subsidies is strongest for the world acting together and weak for one country acting alone; for the United States, counting only domestic returns, it disappears.

The one-sentence version: clean ideas do seem unusually generative, which gives a carbon-independent reason to subsidize clean research alongside a carbon price — but mostly a reason for countries to do it together.

What we don't know

  • Does the premium show up outside citations? Licensing deals, inventors moving between fields, or the diffusion of products would tell us whether the premium is a fact about clean technology or about citation data.
  • Will it shrink as clean matures? The signals conflict: the premium roughly halves once a field's age is controlled for, and it fades as green start-ups mature, yet the clean–dirty gap has grown since the early 1980s, while in European data the green premium peaked in the 2000s.
  • How large is the leak, country by country? The 23% home share is one study's average across countries; country-by-country estimates would show how much of the case belongs to international coordination.
  • What is the right subsidy? Every optimal-subsidy figure here is model-dependent. The qualitative case — subsidize clean research on top of pricing carbon — is robust; the quantitative one is not.

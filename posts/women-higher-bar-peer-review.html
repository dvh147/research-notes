Here is a fact that sounds like a compliment and isn't. In economics, papers written by women are better written than papers written by men. Not vaguely, impressionistically better — measurably so. Run the published articles of four leading economics journals through a readability formula and the female-authored ones come out 1–6% more readable than otherwise-equivalent papers by men.

Your first instinct is probably mine: good for them. But sit with it for a second. Why would the average woman in economics write more clearly than the average man, across thousands of papers? Women and men do not arrive in graduate school with different innate prose. So if their published writing differs that systematically, something happened to it on the way to publication. And the most economical explanation — the one that also predicts the other things we see — is uncomfortable: women's papers are better written because they have to be. They are being held to a higher bar.

That is the claim of this post, and I want to state it carefully, because the careful version is the only one the evidence supports. In economics, the best evidence suggests women clear a higher bar to get published: their papers spend longer under review and must meet a higher quality threshold — and the cleaner, more-cited papers that result are the residue of that tougher gate, not a perk. The qualifier "in economics" is doing real work, and I'll spend a whole section defending it, because across science as a whole this is not what peer review looks like.

Why this is worth caring about

Economics has a stubborn gender problem that the field has spent a decade failing to fix. By the mid-2000s women were about 35% of economics PhD students and 30% of assistant professors, and — this is the depressing part — those numbers have barely moved since. At the top of the ladder it is starker: women are something like 8–13% of full professors in research-oriented departments. Shelly Lundberg and Jenna Stearns call it "stalled progress," and the stall is not at the classroom door, where women enter economics in healthy numbers, but somewhere in the long climb after.

Publication is the rung that decides the rest of the climb. In academic economics, your published papers — where, how many, how cited — are very nearly the whole story for tenure, promotion, and pay. So if the gate to publication is set higher for women, that's not just an unfairness at one stage. It compounds. It is the kind of friction that could, on its own, account for a meaningful chunk of a stalled pipeline. And it would mean the profession is throwing away good work — a misallocation, not just an injustice.

So the stakes are high enough to want the evidence to be good. Let me walk through it in pieces: the readability fingerprint, the bar at the journal, the time tax, and then the harder questions — why a higher bar would exist, and whether we should believe it's really a bar at all.

The readability fingerprint

Start with the cleanest piece of evidence, because it's also the strangest. Erin Hengel's Publishing While Female (2022, Economic Journal) takes thousands of articles from four top journals and scores their readability. Three findings stack into a fingerprint.

First, the gap itself: female-authored papers are 1–6% more readable than comparable male-authored papers. Small in any one paper, systematic across the corpus.

Second — and this is the tell — the gap widens during peer review. Compare a paper's submitted draft to its published version, and the female–male readability gap is roughly twice as large at the end as at the beginning. Whatever is making women's papers cleaner is happening inside the review process, not before it. If women simply wrote more clearly by disposition, the gap would be there at submission and stay flat. Instead it grows under the referees' hands.

Third, women's writing keeps improving over their careers while men's does not. That is exactly what you'd expect if women face a standard they have to learn to clear, and men face a looser one they've already met. It is not what you'd expect from fixed, innate style.

Hengel writes down a small model of an author deciding how much to polish, and asks which world is consistent with all three facts at once. The answer that fits is one where referees (or editors, or the way papers get assigned to referees) apply a tougher standard to women. The readability "advantage," in this reading, is a scar.

I read this evidence as fairly convincing on its own terms — the within-paper and within-career dynamics are hard to explain any other way — but it is one author's careful work on one outcome (readability) in one field. So the question is whether an entirely different method, looking at an entirely different outcome, points the same way.

A higher bar at the journal

It does. The most important corroboration comes from David Card, Stefano DellaVigna, Patricia Funk, and Nagore Iriberri's Are Referees and Editors in Economics Gender Neutral? (2020, QJE), which had the rare luxury of the internal submission and refereeing records of four leading journals.

Their first finding is a clean null, and it matters: the gender of the referee has no effect on how female- versus male-authored papers are assessed. Male referees are not the villains here — at least not differentially. So whatever is going on is not "men refereeing women harshly."

Their second finding is the clever one. Hold the referees' evaluations fixed — compare male- and female-authored papers that referees scored the same — and then look at how those papers are cited years later. Female-authored papers go on to receive about 25% more citations than observably similar male-authored papers with the same referee scores. Think about what that means. If, conditional on clearing the referees, women's papers turn out to be substantially better (by the market's later judgment), then the referees were implicitly requiring more underlying quality from women to award the same score. That is a higher bar, measured from the other side: the papers that make it through are over-qualified.

The editors mostly follow the referees. Card and coauthors estimate that female-authored papers are about 1.7 percentage points less likely to get a revise-and-resubmit than a citation-maximizing editor would grant — a real, if modest, penalty relative to the optimum. (Interestingly, at the desk-reject stage editors lean the other way, treating women a bit more favorably — though still above the citation-maximizing bar.) The picture is not cartoon villainy. It is a system that, in the aggregate, asks women to be a little better to get the same verdict.

A third econ study triangulates the same point from gatekeeping of a different kind. Laura Hospido and Carlos Sanz, in Gender Gaps in the Evaluation of Research (2021), study more than 9,000 submissions to major economics conferences. All-female papers are 3.3 percentage points (about 6.8%) less likely to be accepted than all-male papers, even after controlling for the number of authors, field, citations, publication record, affiliations, and the referees themselves. And the gap has fingerprints: it is driven by male referees, it appears only for lesser-known authors, and it is largest in masculine-coded fields — especially finance.

Three different settings — journal readability, journal refereeing, conference acceptance — three different methods, one direction. That convergence is why I'm willing to use fairly confident language for economics specifically.

The time tax

A higher bar is not free, and the bill arrives as time. In Hengel's data, female-authored papers spend about six months longer under review than male-authored ones — men's papers move through in roughly 18 and a half months on average, women's in just over two years. Six months may not sound like much until you remember what it's made of: extra rounds of revision, more time referees spend scrutinizing, more time authors spend responding. And every month spent re-polishing an old paper to clear the bar is a month not spent starting a new one. Run the rough arithmetic: if the path to tenure runs through, say, six to eight published papers, a six-month surcharge on each is three to four years of cumulative delay spread across a career — invisible in any single case, very hard to miss on a CV by the tenure clock. Hengel calls it a "time tax," and in a profession where output is the currency of promotion, a recurring tax on women's time is a recurring drag on their careers.

Here I have to flag an honest tension, because calibrated honesty is the whole point. Card and coauthors find no gender difference in editorial delays at their four journals. Is that a contradiction? Not quite — the two studies measure different clocks. Card et al. time the editor's decision; Hengel times the whole journey, including the revise-and-resubmit rounds where the polishing actually happens. The time tax lives in the revisions, not in how fast an editor hits "send." But the tension is real enough that I'd put the time channel a notch below the readability and citation results in confidence.

Why would a higher bar exist?

Now the hard part. "Women are held to a higher standard" is a finding; it is not yet a mechanism. Where would such a standard come from, and is it bias?

The cleanest way to think about it predates the gender work entirely. Glenn Ellison's Evolving Standards for Academic Publishing (2002, JPE) models a paper as having two kinds of quality: call it q, the importance of the ideas, and r, everything else — polish, robustness, bulletproof exposition. Economics journals, he argued, have drifted toward demanding ever more r, which is why papers keep getting longer and more revised. Now bolt one assumption onto Ellison's machine — that referees demand more r from women — and Hengel's three facts fall straight out. Here is the toy version:

A journal accepts a paper if it scores at least 12, where ideas earn up to 10 (q) and polish earns up to 10 (r). A man submits great ideas, ordinary polish: q = 7, r = 5, total 12. Accepted. A woman submits the identical paper — q = 7, r = 5 — but the referee, consciously or not, won't wave a woman's paper through at r = 5; she has to reach r = 7. So she revises, and revises again: another round, another few months. When her paper finally appears, it scores r = 7 — visibly more polished than the man's. The higher bar didn't merely slow her down; it stamped her published paper with higher measured quality.

That is the engine. A bar applied to r mechanically produces longer review, more revision, and cleaner published papers — Hengel's fingerprint, and Card et al.'s "more citations for the same referee score," from one assumption.

But why would referees demand more r from women? The broader evaluation literature offers a candidate that isn't "men are sexist." Tristan Botelho and Mabel Abraham, in Pursuing Quality (2017, ASQ), show that gender double standards bite hardest precisely when evaluators face uncertainty and high search costs — many candidates, little information, a quick judgment. That maps eerily onto Hospido and Sanz's result that the conference penalty hits lesser-known authors: when a referee can't easily place you, a stereotype fills the gap, and the stereotype sets a higher proof burden for women. It's less "taste-based hatred" than statistical discrimination under uncertainty — which is cold comfort, since the effect on the author is the same.

Two more econ results suggest the lens through which women's work gets read really is different. Friederike Mengel, Jan Sauermann, and Ulf Zölitz, exploiting random assignment of students to instructors (2019, JEEA), find women get systematically lower teaching evaluations despite identical student grades and study hours — worst for junior women and in mathematical courses. And Markus Eberhardt, Giovanni Facchini, and Valeria Rueda, reading 12,000 reference letters from the economics job market (2023, EJ), find women are more often praised as hard-working "grindstones" and less often for raw ability — a subtle reweighting that predicts worse placements. Same person, different yardstick.

Objection! Maybe women just write better

The obvious rebuttal: maybe there's no bar at all, and women simply write more carefully — more conscientious, more risk-averse, whatever the just-so story. If the readability gap is pre-existing style, it's not evidence of a higher standard.

Two things answer this. First, recall that the gap widens during review and women improve over their careers while men don't. Pre-existing style can't widen inside the review process; an externally imposed standard can. Second, when you actually measure raw writing style under conditions that strip out the review process, the gender differences are tiny. Serge Horbach and coauthors analyzed blinded funding applications (2022) and found only minimal male–female differences in readability, sentiment, and concreteness — far too small to explain funding gaps. The implication cuts cleanly: if raw style barely differs, then the published readability gap in economics is largely manufactured by what happens between submission and print. That's the bar, not the writer. (It also warns us off the lazy flip-side — "women are just better writers" — which the blinded data don't support either.)

Objection! Then double-blind review would fix it

If the problem is that referees know the author is a woman, hiding the name should erase it. It mostly doesn't — and that is genuinely informative.

The foundational test is Rebecca Blank's randomized experiment at the AER (1991): papers were randomly assigned to double-blind or single-blind review. Double-blind made referees tougher and acceptance rates lower overall — but the effect was not significantly different for women. A modern field study by Timothy Pleskac and coauthors comes to the same deflating place: comparing blinded and unblinded conference review (2025, Management Science), they find double-blind is no cure-all, and if anything male first authors edged slightly higher under double-blind. The famous counter-case — a 2008 ecology study finding more female-authored papers after a journal went double-blind — was quickly contested on the grounds that female authorship rose at single-blind journals over the same years too.

Does "blinding doesn't fix it" refute the higher bar? No — it relocates it. A name is not the only thing a referee reads. Topic choice, methods, citation patterns, who you thank in the acknowledgments, the very polish of the prose: plenty survives anonymity and can carry a gender signal. And crucially, the bar in economics seems to operate through revise-and-resubmit standards — how much more you're asked to do before acceptance — which blinding barely touches. The objection is a good one, and the honest response is that anonymity is a weak lever, not that the bar isn't there.

Objection! Across science, peer review isn't biased against women

This is the objection I take most seriously, because it's true, and it's why my title says "in economics." The largest study we have — Flaminio Squazzoni and colleagues' analysis of 145 journals (2021, Science Advances), covering about 1.7 million authors and 740,000 referees across many fields — finds that peer review does not penalize women. If anything, all-women and mixed-gender author teams were slightly more likely to be published. Decomposing referee selection, referee recommendations, and editor decisions, they find no smoking gun.

I won't explain that away. The right conclusion is that a higher bar for women is not a universal law of refereeing. Across science as a whole, the peer-review gate is roughly fair on the outcome that study measures (accept/reject). So why would economics be different? A few reasons make it plausible rather than special pleading: economics is unusually slow and revision-heavy (so an r-bar has room to operate), unusually obsessed with a hierarchy of journals and with citations, and unusually male-dominated. And note that Squazzoni measures outcomes — recommend, accept — while the econ studies catch the bar in places a pure accept/reject rate would miss: in readability, in citations-conditional-on-score, in months of revision. It is entirely coherent for the accept/reject rate to look fair while the standard behind it is not.

Still, this is the boundary of the claim, and I want it to stay there. If you take one thing from this section: don't generalize Hengel and Card to "science is biased against women in review." The big multi-field evidence says otherwise.

Bottom line

Within economics, I read the evidence as fairly convincing that women clear a higher bar to publish. Three independent designs — Hengel's readability dynamics, Card et al.'s citations-conditional-on-referee-score, Hospido and Sanz's conference acceptances — point the same way, and a coherent mechanism (a higher r-threshold, worst under uncertainty, applied through a subtly different evaluative lens) ties them together. The signature consequence — that women's published papers are measurably cleaner and more cited — is not a perk but the visible scar of the gate, and the time tax that comes with it is a real, recurring drag on women's careers.

Now the bounds. This is a statement about economics, and the best cross-field evidence says it does not generalize to science at large. The acceptance-side penalties, where we can measure them, are modest — a couple of percentage points — not enormous. And "higher bar" is a description of the standard, not a proof of its cause: I cannot tell you how much is taste-based bias, how much is statistical discrimination under uncertainty, and how much is women rationally over-investing in polish because they anticipate the bar. What I'm confident of is the narrower, more useful thing: in economics, the gate is tilted, the tilt is costly, and the cleaner papers it produces are evidence of the problem, not consolation for it.

What we don't know

  • Taste, statistics, or self-defense? Whether the bar is animus, rational-but-unfair inference under uncertainty, or women pre-emptively polishing more because they expect tougher treatment. The three have very different policy implications and we can't yet separate them.
  • Is it shrinking? The same Card team found that selection of Econometric Society fellows flipped from a historical penalty for women to a large premium for highly qualified women in the last decade. Recognition, at least at the very top, may be over-correcting even as the publication bar persists — and nobody has reconciled those two trends.
  • How much of the citation premium is the bar? Some of women's extra citations could reflect choosing harder or more important problems, not just clearing a higher gate. Untangling "higher bar" from "different portfolio" is open.
  • Does it appear in finance, management, the other slow and hierarchical fields? The mechanism predicts it should, but the clean within-field evidence is mostly economics so far.
  • What actually fixes it? Blinding is weak. The most promising lever in the broader literature is structural: evaluating candidates jointly rather than one at a time makes evaluators weight performance over stereotype (Bohnet, van Geen, and Bazerman, 2016). Whether that survives contact with real refereeing is untested.

A patent is a bargain. The state hands an inventor up to twenty years of the right to exclude everyone else from making or selling her invention. In return, she has to do one thing: file a public document that explains how the invention works — in principle, well enough that a skilled person could rebuild it once the patent expires. Exclusion is the price the public pays; disclosure is what it buys. Eighteenth-century courts called it the quid pro quo, and it is still the official reason patent systems exist. The point was never to enrich inventors. It was to pull useful knowledge out of their heads and into the public record, where the next inventor can stand on it.

There has always been a skeptic in the room. Patents, she says, are written by lawyers to satisfy lawyers — dense "patentese" engineered to claim as much as possible while teaching as little as possible. And the surveys are on her side. When you ask R&D managers how they learn what competitors are up to, reading patents lands near the bottom of the list, well below hiring people away, reverse-engineering products, and reading the scientific literature. If almost nobody reads patents for the engineering, the disclosure half of the deal looks like a polite fiction — and we are paying for it with two decades of monopoly.

So which is it? Start with one number. In 2000, the United States quietly changed when a patent application becomes public. Before, most applications stayed secret until the patent issued, often three or four years after filing. After the American Inventors Protection Act (AIPA), the default was publication eighteen months after filing, whether or not the patent had been granted yet. Nothing about the strength of the patents changed — same term, same right to exclude. Only the timing of the teaching moved earlier. And follow-on invention — later inventions that build on an earlier one — measurably responded.

That is the trick that cracks this question open. You cannot learn much by asking whether "patents" help or hurt innovation, because a patent does two opposite things at once: it teaches (good for the next inventor) and it excludes (bad for the next inventor). But a handful of natural experiments change only the disclosure and hold the monopoly fixed. When you line them up, they tell a consistent story, and it is the one the skeptic doubts. The teaching function is real. Earlier, more reachable, and more readable patent disclosure causes more follow-on invention; locking disclosure away causes less. My read of the evidence is that this is fairly convincing — not airtight, because almost everything is measured in patent citations, and because the channel turns out to run through specialists rather than through inventors reading patents like textbooks. That last point is the caveat the title is carrying: patents teach when you can read and reach them, and mostly through middlemen.

Four moves let researchers isolate the teaching channel. You can publish disclosure sooner, make it reachable, make it readable, or — cleanest of all — switch it off and watch what breaks.

Publish sooner

The AIPA is the workhorse here, because "secret until granted" versus "public at eighteen months" is close to a clean on/off switch for disclosure timing. The most ambitious study is Hegde, Herkenhoff, and Zhu (2023), in the Journal of Political Economy. Their identification is clever: for hundreds of thousands of US patents, they find the "twin" European patent protecting the same invention. European applications were always published at eighteen months, so they were untreated by the AIPA; the US twins had their disclosure accelerated by roughly a year and a half. Comparing twins strips out the value of the underlying invention and leaves the effect of disclosure timing.

Post-AIPA, the US twins receive about 15% more follow-on citations over ten years than their European counterparts, and the first citations arrive a quarter faster. Crucially, self-citations don't rise — so this isn't firms patting themselves on the back; it's other inventors finding the work sooner. The design is built to be conservative (both twins are disclosed at eighteen months somewhere, so it understates the effect of disclosing at all), which makes the positive result more impressive, not less.

Faster disclosure also cuts wasteful duplication, and here a toy example helps. Picture two labs independently chasing the same improvement to a battery cathode. If neither can see the other's filing for four years, both may burn the full four years — and one of those efforts is redundant. Publish at eighteen months and the trailing lab learns it has been scooped a year and a half sooner; it stops, or pivots to something genuinely new. Hegde and coauthors find exactly this fingerprint: after the AIPA, technological overlap falls among near-duplicate patents and rises among distant-but-related ones, and applications are about 5% less likely to be abandoned. A separate study, Lueck, Balsmeier, Seliger, and Fleming (2020) in Management Science, builds a direct measure of "blocked" future applications and reaches the same conclusion: the AIPA reduced duplication in both the US and European systems.

How much is this worth? Hegde and coauthors estimate that publicly listed firms whose patents faced longer grant delays — and so got a bigger disclosure acceleration from the reform — raised their R&D spending by about 4%. They note this is roughly what you would get by lifting the federal R&D tax credit from 20% to 26%. The difference is that the disclosure rule is, fiscally, free: it is a change to a filing-cabinet policy, not a check from the Treasury. I'd treat the precise 4% as suggestive rather than nailed down — it leans on a weaker, firm-exposure design than the citation results do — but the order of magnitude is striking.

One honest complication comes from Baruffaldi and Simeth (2020), who study the same AIPA reform with a regression-discontinuity design around the cutoff date. They also find that earlier disclosure raised forward citations — by something like 12% for a one-year acceleration — but with a twist that recurs throughout this literature: the diffusion did not spread out geographically, and the extra attention flowed disproportionately to patent-attorney search firms. Their conclusion is that the gains from speedier disclosure are "confined to the existing attention scope and absorptive capacity" — a firm's ability to recognize and use outside knowledge — of the people already equipped to look. Disclosure is a lever, but its return depends on who is positioned to pull it.

Make it reachable

Timing is one access margin; physical and linguistic reach is another. Before the internet, if you wanted to read a patent you had to travel to Washington — or to one of the regional Patent and Trademark Depository Libraries, which held the full back-file of patents on paper and microfilm along with the tools to search it. Furman, Nagler, and Watzinger (2021), in AEJ: Economic Policy, use the staggered opening of these libraries from 1975 to 1997 as an experiment in access. When a library opens, local patenting rises by roughly 17% (their headline range is 8 to 20%) relative to matched control regions — and the effect is sharply local, fading to nothing beyond about fifteen miles, which is what you'd expect if the mechanism is the cost of physically getting to the documents.

Three details make this convincing as disclosure rather than something else. The boost is concentrated in chemistry, where patents are unusually informative; after a library opens, local inventors start citing more geographically and technologically distant prior art, exactly as if they are now reaching ideas they couldn't reach before; and the extra patents are no lower quality, carrying tens of millions of dollars a year in private value. Best of all, the effect disappears in the mid-1990s — precisely when the web made every patent searchable from any desk, rendering the library's special access worthless. A treatment whose effect vanishes the moment its mechanism is made redundant is a treatment you can believe in.

The internet handles distance; it does not handle language. Most of the world's patents are not in English, and a US inventor can't learn from a Chinese patent she can't read. Büttner, Firat, and Raiteri (2022) exploit a sudden fix: in September 2013, Google Patents added China's patents to its database and ran them through machine translation. Using patents that Google translated only later as a control group, they find that becoming readable in English raised citations from US inventors by about 7 to 10%. The cleanest check: drop US inventors who have Chinese surnames — people who could already read the originals — and the effect gets stronger (in physics, it roughly doubles). So the channel really is legibility, not co-ethnic networks. There's also a revealing exception: in pharmaceuticals and other "human necessities," the effect flips negative. In fields where invention is less cumulative and more winner-take-all, a readable foreign patent is mostly a competitor to design around, not a teacher to learn from — a useful reminder that disclosure and exclusion are always both in the room.

Make it readable

If reachability matters, so should the quality of the writing — and disclosure quality is, at last, becoming measurable. Kong, Dulleck, Jaffe, Sun, and Vajjala (2023) run sixty-odd computational-linguistics measures over patent applications and find that university patents are about 0.4 standard deviations more readable than corporate ones — consistent with the idea that universities have more reason to actually teach what they invented, while firms have reason to obscure. Reassuringly, their machine-scored readability lines up with which patents the USPTO rejects for failing to disclose adequately. This is the youngest and least causal strand of the four — it tells us disclosure quality varies and can be quantified, not yet that nudging it upward raises follow-on invention — so I hold it more loosely. But it points at the same margin: how legible the teaching is, not just whether it exists.

Switch it off

The sharpest test of whether disclosure teaches is to suppress it and see if invention falls. The US government does exactly that, through the Invention Secrecy Act: it can slap a secrecy order on an application it deems a national-security risk, withholding the patent and forbidding disclosure. That gives researchers the mirror image of the experiments above.

Gross (2023), in Management Science, studies the largest episode: during World War II the USPTO locked more than 11,000 applications under secrecy orders, then released them in a batch at war's end. Firms whose inventions were frozen pulled their patenting out of the affected technology classes, and the retreat persisted into the 1960s. The most direct evidence that secrecy concealed real knowledge is linguistic: the distinctive technical vocabulary of the secret patents only starts showing up in later patents — and even in the text of Google Books — after the 1945 release. The teaching was genuinely switched off, and you can watch it switch back on.

The cleanest of all is de Rassenfosse, Pellegrino, and Raiteri (2024). They match each secret patent's underlying prior invention to similar non-secret inventions and track follow-on. A secrecy order cuts the focal invention's follow-on citations by about 40% while it is in force — and, remarkably, by about 45% even after the order is lifted. They call it a "lost generation" of inventions: the inventions that would have built on the hidden one never get built, and they don't come back when the secret is finally released, because by then the moment has passed and attention has moved on. The effect is largest among inventors geographically distant from the original — precisely the people who could only have learned about it from the document, not from the local grapevine. That distance gradient is the single most disclosure-specific result in this whole literature: it is hard to explain by anything other than reading.

A caveat worth stating plainly: a secrecy order bundles "no disclosure" with "no patent rights" and "no commercialization," so these studies can't perfectly isolate the teaching channel from everything else secrecy does. The distance gradient and the word-diffusion test are the authors' best attempts to pin it on disclosure specifically, and I find them persuasive — but this is identification by accumulated circumstantial evidence, not a single knockout design.

Objection! "But nobody reads patents"

Now the skeptic's turn, because her evidence is real. Lisa Larrimore Ouellette's 2012 survey of nanotechnology researchers found that while most had read patents, only about 38% of those readers thought the patents were reproducible — that is, actually enabling. The classic appropriability surveys — Levin and coauthors' Yale survey in the 1980s, and Cohen, Nelson, and Walsh's Carnegie Mellon survey around 2000 — consistently find that R&D managers rank patent disclosures low as a way to learn rivals' technology, and that firms patent mainly to block competitors and bargain, not to teach. Edwin Mansfield documented decades ago that technology leaks to rivals within about a year regardless of patents. And the legal scholar Sean Seymore has argued that patents routinely fail to teach because the law demands no working example and tolerates "prophetic" examples — inventions described but never actually built.

There's a methodological worry on top of the behavioral one. Nearly every supporting study above measures diffusion with forward citations, and citations are a famously noisy proxy. Roach and Cohen (2013) show that patent citations miss the private, contract-based knowledge flows and are distorted by firms' citing strategies; worse, a large share of the citations on a patent are inserted by the examiner, not the inventor, so a citation is weak evidence that anyone read the cited document at all.

I take all of this seriously, and it is why the claim is "fairly convincing" rather than "established." But it does not topple the result, for two reasons. First, the strongest evidence here is not a citation count sitting still — it is a behavioral response to a disclosure shock. When the only thing that changes is the timing, reachability, or language of disclosure, and follow-on invention moves in lockstep, the examiner-citation and citing-strategy critiques largely wash out: those biases inflate or deflate the level of citations, not the response to opening a library or translating a patent. Second, "scientists don't read patents" is not the same as "patents don't teach." Disclosure can diffuse through intermediaries — the patent attorneys and freedom-to-operate search firms who very much do read patents, and who, recall, captured most of the AIPA's gains — and through firms reading patents precisely to design around them or to avoid duplicating them. And the secrecy experiments need no one to curl up with a patent: they just show that when the knowledge is sealed, the downstream inventions don't appear. The teaching can be real even if the classroom is mostly full of lawyers.

It's not the same as exclusion

It's worth being precise about what this does and doesn't say, because a companion claim points the other way. In a previous post I argued that patents can slow follow-on innovation — but mainly when they lock up access to a needed input. This post is the same coin's other face: the very same documents, by teaching, can speed follow-on. Both are true; the net effect of a patent on cumulative innovation is a race between its exclusion drag and its disclosure boost.

Which wins? It depends on the technology, and we can't yet write down a general exchange rate. But one data point is suggestive. Sampat and Williams (2019) found that patents on human genes had essentially no measurable effect on follow-on innovation — the exclusion drag, in that case, was roughly zero. If exclusion often does little while disclosure does something, then for a meaningful class of inventions the teaching face may be the more active one. That is a conjecture, not a finding — flagging it as such — but it reframes the policy question. We spend enormous energy arguing about whether patents are too strong. We spend almost none asking whether they teach well.

Bottom line

The disclosure half of the patent bargain is not a fiction. Across six independent natural experiments — accelerating disclosure (the AIPA studies), making it reachable (patent libraries, machine translation), and suppressing it (wartime and modern secrecy orders) — follow-on invention moves the way the teaching theory predicts, and the mirror-image secrecy results are clean enough that I'd put real weight on them. The accessibility of disclosure is therefore a genuine innovation-policy lever, and an unusually cheap one: publish sooner, fund translation and search tools, demand more readable specifications, and you can buy follow-on invention without touching the politically fraught question of how strong patents should be.

The bounds matter. The evidence is overwhelmingly about patent counts and citations, not the welfare those inventions deliver, and citations are a proxy I'd rather not lean on as hard as this literature must. The teaching works through specialists and intermediaries, and its payoff is gated by absorptive capacity — give a patent library to a region with no inventors and nothing happens. And the library effect already evaporated once the web arrived, a reminder that some of these levers have a shelf life. So: real, economically meaningful, and policy-relevant — but a complement to a well-functioning innovation system, not a magic wand.

What we don't know

  • The exchange rate between teaching and exclusion. We have good evidence on each face of the patent separately and almost none on which dominates, for which technologies. That ratio is what a patent system should actually be optimized around, and we can't yet compute it.
  • Does readability cause follow-on? We can now measure disclosure quality; we have not yet run the experiment that nudges it up and watches invention respond. A field experiment — or a USPTO rule change on specification standards — would be worth a great deal.
  • Anything beyond citations. The whole literature would be far more convincing with outcomes closer to real knowledge use: replication, product launches, engineers' own accounts of what they learned and where.
  • The AI wildcard. Speculatively — and this goes past the evidence — large language models could do for the readability margin what machine translation did for the language margin, turning opaque "patentese" into something any engineer can interrogate in plain English. If the machine-translation result is any guide, making the existing stock of disclosure suddenly legible could be a larger lever than anything we do to new patents. That is a hypothesis, not a finding, but it is the one I'd most like to see tested.

In 1925 the philosopher Alfred North Whitehead offered a one-line theory of where the modern world came from. "The greatest invention of the nineteenth century," he wrote in Science and the Modern World, "was the invention of the method of invention." His point was that the century's railways and dynamos mattered less than the thing underneath them: a repeatable process — the industrial research lab, the trained scientist, the experimental method — for producing inventions on purpose. Once you have a method of invention, you don't just get one new thing; you get a machine for getting new things.

A lot of the smartest writing about artificial intelligence is quietly asking whether AI is the twenty-first century's version of that move. The everyday conversation is about products and jobs: a chatbot that writes your email, a model that might take your job. But the more consequential question for long-run growth is whether AI changes the production of ideas itself — whether it is, in the economists' phrase, a general-purpose method of invention.

Why care? Because by the best measurement we have, ideas are getting harder to find. In a much-cited 2020 paper in the American Economic Review, Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb show that research productivity — ideas produced per researcher — has been falling steadily across domains, at something like 5% a year. Keeping Moore's Law on track now takes about 18 times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s. If finding ideas keeps getting more expensive, then a technology that makes finding them cheaper is not just another gadget. It is the most valuable thing AI could possibly be.

So here is the claim I want to argue, with calibrated confidence: modern AI is becoming a general-purpose method of invention — a research tool that lowers the cost of search and prediction across many fields — but the proof is uneven. The evidence that it cuts the cost of cognitive work is strong; the evidence that it shifts the direction of discovery is real and growing; the evidence that it raises the aggregate rate of discovery is still thin, and the single most famous estimate of it just collapsed. Let me walk through the pieces.

What "a method of invention" means — and is AI one?

The economists' term of art for Whitehead's idea is a general-purpose technology (GPT). In a foundational 1995 paper, Bresnahan and Trajtenberg defined a GPT by three traits: it's pervasive (used as an input across many sectors), it's dynamic (it keeps getting better on its own), and — the crucial one — it generates innovational complementarities, meaning it makes downstream R&D more productive. The steam engine, electricity, and the computer are the canonical examples: each didn't just do a job, it changed how other jobs got done, including the job of inventing.

The headline argument that AI belongs on that list comes from Cockburn, Henderson and Stern (2019), in the influential NBER volume The Economics of Artificial Intelligence. Their claim is sharper than "AI is a GPT." They argue that deep learning specifically is a general-purpose invention in the method of invention — a GPT whose main effect is on the research process. As evidence, they build a database of AI publications and patents from 1990 to 2015 and document a sharp pivot after about 2009 toward learning-oriented AI showing up as a tool inside other fields' research, not just as its own subject. (They trace the "method of invention" phrase, via Zvi Griliches, all the way back to Whitehead.)

Is this just a nice story, or does AI actually have the breadth a GPT needs? Here the best evidence is a clever 2023 paper in Research Policy by Goldfarb, Taska and Teodoridis. Instead of arguing about definitions, they measure breadth directly: they read the text of millions of online job ads and rank 21 emerging technologies by how widely each is being demanded across industries and occupations — a GPT "fingerprint." Machine learning scores near the top, and the ranking has predictive power years ahead of patent-based measures. So the GPT label is fairly convincing, not just rhetorical: by a direct measure of pervasiveness, machine learning behaves like the real thing.

What does "method of invention" actually buy you, mechanically? The cleanest way to see it is search. Picture inventing as rummaging for needles in a haystack — the metaphor Agrawal, McHale and Oettl (2019) use in the same NBER volume. Suppose a chemist has 1,000 candidate molecules and wants useful pairs. There are about half a million possible pairings, and say only one in ten thousand — roughly 50 of them — actually works. Test them blindly and you run half a million experiments. Now give the chemist a prediction model that's decent (not perfect) at guessing which pairings are promising, so that the 50 winners are concentrated in the 50,000 the model flags. She finds most of the needles for a tenth of the searching. AI's core trick — cheap, scalable prediction — is exactly a way to shrink the haystack. Plugged into the standard "idea production function," better needle-finding raises the output of ideas for a given amount of research labor. That's the whole thesis in one image.

The cost of thinking is falling

Before we get to laboratories, start with the more mundane and best-documented fact: AI sharply lowers the cost of well-defined cognitive tasks. This is the part of the story where the evidence is strong, because several randomized or quasi-randomized studies agree.

  • In customer support, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025, Quarterly Journal of Economics) studied 5,000-plus agents at a firm rolling out an AI assistant. Productivity — issues resolved per hour — rose about 15% on average. The striking part is the distribution: gains of around 30% for the least-experienced agents and roughly zero for the most experienced. The AI worked by capturing what the best agents did and handing it to everyone else.
  • In professional writing, Noy and Zhang (2023, Science) ran a randomized experiment with 453 college-educated workers. Time on task fell about 40% and rated quality rose about 18%, and again the effect compressed the skill distribution — weaker writers caught up.
  • In software, a randomized trial of GitHub Copilot by Peng and coauthors (2023) found programmers completed a coding task about 56% faster. (Treat this one more gingerly: it's a preprint, the task was narrow, and two of the authors work for the vendor.)
  • And in management consulting, Dell'Acqua and a large team (2023; published in Organization Science, 2025) randomized 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants using GPT-4. On tasks inside the model's competence, they did about 12% more work, 25% faster, at >40% higher quality.

Two qualifications keep this honest. First, the same BCG study named the catch — the "jagged technological frontier." On a task chosen to sit outside the model's competence, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer: confident, fluent, wrong. AI lowers the cost of cognition unevenly, and where the boundary lies is not obvious from the outside. Second, every one of these is a task, not a discovery. Resolving a support ticket faster is not the same as inventing a new material. The leap from "AI helps people do bounded cognitive tasks" to "AI helps people invent" is exactly the leap the rest of this post has to earn.

Into the lab: the rate and direction of discovery

So does the task-level magic reach actual research? Here the evidence is thinner but pointed, and it says something specific: AI seems to be moving the direction of discovery more clearly than its rate.

Start with a pre-AI-boom natural experiment that tells us how research tools behave in general. Furman and Teodoridis (2020, Organization Science) used the surprise arrival of cheap motion-sensing hardware (the hacked Microsoft Kinect) as a tool that automated a class of research tasks in computer science and electrical engineering. It raised the rate of idea production and changed its type — and it disproportionately helped researchers who lacked access to expensive lab equipment. That is the template: a new method of invention doesn't just speed things up, it changes what gets worked on and who can work on it.

Now AI specifically. The seed for this whole topic, Bianchini, Müller and Pelletier (2022) — whose title is, helpfully, "Artificial intelligence in science: an emerging general method of invention" — tracks deep learning's spread through science itself. Using text analysis across arXiv and Web of Science, they document exponential growth in the adoption of deep learning as a research tool across essentially all scientific fields and all over the world, with the technology moving from a niche interdisciplinary curiosity into the everyday method of individual disciplines. So the diffusion across science is not in doubt. What that diffusion produces is the open question.

The single best study on that question is brand new: Hill and Stein (2026, NBER), on AlphaFold — the deep-learning system from DeepMind that predicts protein structures, and whose underlying model Jumper and coauthors published in Nature in 2021. Hill and Stein compare research on proteins that lacked a known structure before AlphaFold to research on proteins that already had one, around the 2021 release. Their findings are a model of calibration. Basic research on the previously-unmapped proteins rose 15 to 40% — AI redirected science toward the territory it had just illuminated. But the rate of experimental structure determination "remained almost unchanged," and they find no effect yet on applied, early-stage drug development. In other words: clear evidence that AI changed the direction of inquiry, and so far no evidence that it sped up the translation into applications. The best evidence suggests AI is bending the direction of discovery; on the rate, the verdict is "not yet visible."

Firms are betting on it — and the bet is new products

Outside the lab, do firms behave as if AI changes what they invent? The cleanest evidence is Babina, Fedyk, He and Hodson (2024, Journal of Financial Economics). They build a firm-level measure of AI investment from the résumés of employees (who has AI skills?), and find that AI-investing firms grow faster in sales, employment, and market value. Crucially, they trace where the growth comes from: product innovation — new products and trademarks — rather than cost-cutting on existing ones. AI shows up as a tool for inventing what to sell, not merely for making it cheaper. They shore up causality by instrumenting with firms' exposure to local universities' supply of AI graduates.

That result comes with a warning attached, which I'll return to: the growth "concentrates among larger firms and is associated with higher industry concentration." The method of invention, at least so far, is being wielded disproportionately by incumbents. Smaller-bore studies point the same way on the product-innovation channel — Wang and Wu (2025, Management Science), studying 1,800 Chinese startups, find AI capability raises product innovation, especially paired with disciplined experimentation — and Boussioux and coauthors (2024, Organization Science) find human-guided AI can match or beat a human crowd at generating creative business solutions. The direction-of-innovation evidence is consistent across settings, even if no single study is decisive.

Objection! The most famous result just collapsed

If you followed this topic in 2024, you may be waiting for me to cite the paper that seemed to settle it: a young MIT economist's randomized study of a materials-science lab adopting an AI discovery tool, reporting enormous gains — on the order of 44% more new materials and 39% more patents. It was endorsed by senior economists and headed for the Quarterly Journal of Economics.

I'm not citing it as evidence, because it isn't any. In May 2025, MIT publicly announced that it had "no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data and… no confidence in the veracity of the research," asked for the paper to be withdrawn from both the preprint server and the QJE, and confirmed the author was no longer at the university. The whole spectacular result evaporated.

I dwell on this because it is the most important methodological fact in the area. This is a fast-moving field with enormous commercial and academic incentives to overclaim, thin peer review relative to the hype cycle, and a hunger — mine included — for a clean number that says "AI invents." The honest response is to discount flashy single-study magnitudes hard, especially the ones that confirm what we want to hear. The credible evidence above is more modest precisely because it survived contact with comparison groups and skeptical referees.

The retraction is not the only reason for caution. Three deeper counter-forces bound the optimism. Displacement: the same AI that augments inventors automates other people's tasks; Acemoglu and Restrepo (2022, Econometrica) attribute 50–70% of the rise in US wage inequality over four decades to automation displacing workers from routine tasks, and related work on AI exposure in job vacancies shows the displacement margin is real for AI too. A method of invention and a method of displacement can be the same tool. Bottlenecks: Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) point out that even if AI automates much of idea-production, growth can stay "constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve" — a Baumol bottleneck in which the un-automated steps become the binding constraint. Concentration: the theory in Ide and Talamàs (2025, Journal of Political Economy) is a useful corrective to the cheerful "AI democratizes" reading of the task studies. They show that autonomous AI — agents that work on their own — tends to benefit the most knowledgeable people, while only copilot-style AI benefits the least knowledgeable. Which world we get depends on how the technology is deployed, and Babina's concentration result is a hint that the autonomous, incumbent-favoring version is not hypothetical.

How much could this matter?

Let me do one back-of-the-envelope, flagged as speculation. Bloom and coauthors put the decline in research productivity at roughly 5% a year. Suppose AI raises the effective productivity of research labor by, say, 15% — a deliberately conservative figure, well under the task-level numbers, because only part of research is automatable cognitive work. That one-time boost would claw back about three years of the burden-of-knowledge slide. Meaningful — but notice two things. It's a level shift, not a permanent change in the growth rate, unless AI itself keeps improving fast enough to deliver such gains again and again. And it assumes the gains reach the frontier of discovery rather than stopping at the customer-support desk. The following is more speculative than what came before: if AI keeps improving and the gains do reach real research, you could tell a story where it offsets a decent chunk of the idea-scarcity problem for a couple of decades. But that is a hope resting on a theoretical growth model, not a measured fact, and I'd hold it loosely.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. That AI lowers the cost of bounded cognitive work is well established. That AI is genuinely general-purpose — broad enough to qualify as a method of invention rather than a niche tool — is fairly convincing. That it is starting to shift the direction of research and of commercial innovation is supported by the best available evidence, though each strand rests on one or two clean studies rather than a mature literature. That it is raising the aggregate rate of discovery is, honestly, not yet demonstrated — the diffusion is everywhere, the productivity at the task level is real, but the through-line to faster discovery is still mostly inference, and the one paper that claimed to nail it was retracted. So: AI is becoming a general-purpose method of invention, but slowly, unevenly, with the payoff visible in what we investigate before how much we discover — and with a real chance that the gains concentrate in a few big players rather than democratizing invention.

If I had to bet, I'd bet Whitehead's framing is the right one and that a decade from now the "method of invention" story will look obviously correct. But the gap between that bet and what the data currently prove is exactly the space where hype lives, and the retraction is a reminder of what happens to people who forget it.

What we don't know

  • Rate, cleanly identified. We badly need more studies like Hill and Stein's — credible comparison groups, real research output — across more fields. One AlphaFold is not a literature.
  • Does it reach the frontier, or stall at routine work? The task RCTs measure support tickets and memos. Whether the same gains show up in genuinely novel discovery is the central open question.
  • Democratize or concentrate? The task studies say AI lifts the least skilled; the firm and theory evidence says it may concentrate gains in incumbents. These can't both be the whole story, and which dominates is a first-order question for policy.
  • Level or growth. Is AI a one-time boost to research productivity or something that keeps compounding? The difference is the difference between a nice decade and a different century.
  • What would change my mind. Several well-identified studies showing no change in research output where AI tools diffused would push me back toward "useful tool, not a method of invention." So would evidence that the measured task gains don't survive once AI's confident errors are properly priced in.

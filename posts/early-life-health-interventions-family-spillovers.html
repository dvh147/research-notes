There is a line, in most rich countries, at 1,500 grams.

A baby born weighing 1,499 grams is labeled "very low birth weight" (VLBW) and, almost mechanically, receives a burst of extra medical attention — closer monitoring, more time in the neonatal intensive care unit, a longer list of interventions. A baby born at 1,501 grams, all but identical, gets somewhat less. The label, not the underlying health of the child, does much of the deciding right at the cutoff.

That arbitrary line is one of the cleaner natural experiments in economics. Because the two groups of babies are essentially the same on either side of 1,500 grams, the difference in what happens to them later is a credible estimate of what the extra care does. Douglas Almond, Joseph Doyle, Amanda Kowalski, and Heidi Williams used exactly this design in a 2010 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, and a small literature has since followed the same line across several countries.

This post is about one claim those studies, taken together, support: the returns to treating a fragile newborn are bigger than the standard accounting suggests — because the benefits last for years, and because they don't stop with the child who was treated. When we ask whether neonatal spending is "worth it," we usually count the survival of the treated baby. That turns out to be the narrowest possible ledger.

Does the extra care actually help?

Start with the treated baby, and with mortality, the outcome that is hardest to argue about.

Almond and coauthors used the U.S. census of births from 1983–2002 and compared one-year mortality on either side of the 1,500-gram threshold. They found that newborns just below the line — the ones who get tagged VLBW and treated more aggressively — have about a one-percentage-point lower one-year mortality rate than newborns just above it. That is a large number in context: mortality just above the cutoff averages about 5.5 percent, so the extra care is associated with something like a one-fifth reduction in the death rate right at the margin. And it runs against the underlying gradient — heavier babies are normally healthier — so the discontinuity is hard to explain by anything other than the treatment.

What does that cost? Hospital charges jump by roughly $4,000 as you cross the threshold from above, against mean costs of about $40,000. Put the mortality reduction and the spending together and you get a price per statistical life saved of about $550,000 (in 2006 dollars). Whatever number you favor for the value of a statistical life — standard regulatory figures run into the millions — half a million dollars per life is cheap. On the narrow ledger, treating these babies already looks like a good deal.

So the first link in the chain is solid: at this margin, more medical care really does save lives, and at a low price.

And the benefits outlast the NICU

A skeptic could grant all that and still worry that we are saving sicker children into harder lives — that the survivors carry lasting damage. The evidence points the other way.

Prashant Bharadwaj, Katrine Løken, and Christopher Neilson took the same VLBW-cutoff design to administrative data from Chile and Norway, linking birth records to school records, in a 2013 paper in the American Economic Review. Children who got the extra care at birth not only had lower mortality — they had higher test scores and grades years later, by 0.15 to 0.22 standard deviations. The early intervention shows up as human capital in a third-grade classroom and beyond.

This fits a much larger body of work on what economists call the fetal origins hypothesis — the finding that conditions very early in life cast long shadows. In their review Childhood Circumstances and Adult Outcomes: Act II (2018), Douglas Almond, Janet Currie, and Valentina Duque summarize a now-large literature with a striking bottom line: "even relatively mild shocks in early life can have substantial negative impacts" decades later. The corollary is that help early in life can have outsized positive impacts. Sarah Miller and Laura Wherry, studying U.S. Medicaid expansions to pregnant women in a 2019 paper, find that the cohorts who gained prenatal coverage grew up to have fewer chronic conditions and higher high-school graduation rates. Tania Barham, following a maternal-and-child-health program in Matlab, Bangladesh, finds cognitive gains of 0.39 standard deviations in late childhood. Different settings, different interventions, same direction: early-life health spending shows up much later as schooling and adult health.

The evidence on these first two links — that the care saves lives, and that the survivors do better, not worse — is about as good as observational economics gets: clean designs, replicated across countries.

The benefits don't stop with the child

Here is where it gets more surprising, and where the seed for this post comes in.

Meltem Daysal, Marianne Simonsen, Mircea Trandafir, and Sanni Breining ran the VLBW-cutoff design on Danish administrative data, but they did something the earlier papers had not: they followed the treated child's family. Their 2022 paper in the Review of Economics and Statistics confirms the now-familiar focal-child result — better health, higher test scores — and then reports two spillovers:

  • Siblings of a treated child score higher on 9th-grade language and math tests and are more likely to be enrolled in high school by age 19.
  • Mothers of a treated child have better mental health.

In other words, an intervention aimed squarely at one fragile newborn left the brothers and sisters doing better in school and the mother doing better psychologically — none of whom were the patient.

This is one study, in one country, and I'll come back to how much weight it can bear. But it is not free-floating. A separate paper by Melanie Guldi, Amelia Hawkins, Jeffrey Hemmeter, and Lucie Schmidt uses a different birth-weight cutoff — eligibility for Supplemental Security Income at 1,200 grams — and finds, in a 2024 study, that the extra support changes how parents parent. (Notably, they find no effect on maternal depression or measured child outcomes — a useful reminder, which I'll return to, that "family processes" and "measured mental health" are not the same thing.)

Why would treating one child help the others?

A spillover you can't explain is a spillover you shouldn't fully trust, so it's worth asking how a treatment to one child reaches the rest of the household. Three channels, in rough order of how well I think they're established.

A calmer mother. A very sick newborn is a source of enormous family stress, and a healthier focal child plausibly lifts that weight — which is exactly the mechanism Daysal and coauthors emphasize for the maternal-mental-health result. That maternal mental health is causally upstream of children's outcomes is supported elsewhere: Dilek Sevim, Victoria Baranov, Sonia Bhalotra and coauthors find in a 2024 study that a perinatal psychosocial intervention improved maternal mental health by 0.17–0.29 SD and weakened the link between a mother's depression and her child's skills. And the reverse runs too: Petra Persson and Maya Rossin-Slater show, in a 2018 AER paper, that maternal stress in pregnancy raises a child's later use of ADHD and anxiety medications. If stress flows from mother to child, relieving it should flow the same way — to the siblings.

Freed-up resources and parental attention. A child who recovers needs less, so parents have more time, money, and attention left for the others (and may deliberately rebalance — what Daysal et al. call parental compensating behavior).

Siblings simply transmit. Even outside health, there is now good causal evidence that siblings shape one another. Adam Altmejd and coauthors show in a 2021 QJE that younger siblings follow older ones into the same college and major — in Chile, Croatia, Sweden, and the United States. Krzysztof Karbownik and Umut Ożek find sibling spillovers in school achievement using a regression discontinuity design in Florida. Javaeria Qureshi finds that an older sister's schooling raises her younger siblings' literacy and numeracy in Pakistan — and draws the general moral directly: evaluations "that ignore these spillovers on younger siblings systematically underestimate total benefits." If a sibling's schooling moves the others, a sibling's health plausibly does too.

How much should we believe this?

The chain has two very different links, and I'd put very different confidence on them.

The direct links — that VLBW care lowers infant mortality cheaply, and that treated children do better in school — are about as well established as this kind of research gets. Multiple clean cutoff designs, replicated in the United States, Chile, and Norway, all point the same way, and they sit inside a fetal-origins literature with hundreds of consistent results. I'd call that close to settled.

The family-spillover link is more tentative, and honesty requires saying so. The headline sibling-and-mother result rests largely on one well-identified Danish study. Spillovers, when they exist, are smaller and noisier than direct effects, and the supporting evidence I've marshaled (the sibling-spillover and maternal-stress papers) is suggestive-by-analogy rather than a direct replication of the treat-a-newborn-help-the-sibling finding. And family spillovers are not always benign: Jennifer Heissel finds that a teen birth harms the mother's younger siblings — lower test scores, more grade repetition. Whatever transmits good outcomes through a family can transmit bad ones. The Guldi et al. null on maternal depression is a related caution: the family channel is real but its size depends on the intervention, the margin, and the setting.

There is also the standard regression-discontinuity caveat. These estimates are local to the roughly-1,500-gram margin, in well-resourced hospitals. They tell us about the marginal baby at the cutoff, not the average premature infant, and not about neonatal care in a low-income country where the constraints are entirely different.

So: high confidence that early-life medical care is a good investment for the child; moderate, and appropriately hedged, confidence that the family spillovers make it an even better one.

A back-of-the-envelope

Even with that hedge, the direction is what matters for policy. At about $550,000 per statistical life saved, treating these babies clears any reasonable value-of-life bar on the focal child alone. The spillovers don't have to be large to tilt the calculation further. Suppose a treated child has, on average, roughly one sibling, and that sibling captures even a third of the focal child's ~0.2 SD test-score gain. That alone raises the human-capital return to the intervention by tens of percent — before you count the mother's mental health, which standard cost-benefit analyses value at zero simply because they never look. The narrow ledger isn't just narrow; it's biased toward under-investing.

What we don't know

The open questions are mostly about the second link. We have, essentially, one clean estimate of the sibling-and-mother spillover from early-life medical care, in one country; we badly need replications elsewhere, and longer follow-ups to see whether the sibling test-score gains become gains in schooling, earnings, and the next generation's health. We don't have a clean decomposition of the mechanism — how much is the mother's relief, how much is freed-up money and time, how much is direct sibling interaction. We don't know whether the spillovers shrink, vanish, or even reverse for less marginal infants or in lower-resource health systems. And we know almost nothing about whether the same logic extends to other early-life health interventions — vaccinations, nutrition programs, prenatal care — though there's no obvious reason it wouldn't.

What we can say is this. When a hospital decides how hard to fight for a 1,499-gram newborn, the stakes are wider than that child's chart. The benefits, when they come, seem to ripple outward — to a brother's report card, to a sister's chance of finishing high school, to a mother's peace of mind. We should at least try to count them.

There is a line, in most rich countries, at 1,500 grams.

A baby born weighing 1,499 grams is labeled "very low birth weight" (VLBW) and, almost mechanically, receives a burst of extra medical attention — closer monitoring, more time in the neonatal intensive care unit, a longer list of interventions. A baby born at 1,501 grams, all but identical, gets somewhat less. The label, not the underlying health of the child, does much of the deciding right at the cutoff.

That arbitrary line is one of the cleaner natural experiments in economics. Because the two groups of babies are essentially the same on either side of 1,500 grams, the difference in what happens to them later is a credible estimate of what the extra care does. Douglas Almond, Joseph Doyle, Amanda Kowalski, and Heidi Williams used exactly this design in a 2010 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, and a small literature has since followed the same line across several countries.

This post is about one claim those studies, taken together, support: the returns to treating a fragile newborn are bigger than the standard accounting suggests — because the benefits don't stop with the child who was treated. When we ask whether neonatal spending is "worth it," we usually count the survival of the treated baby. That turns out to be the narrowest possible ledger.

Does the extra care actually help?

Start with the treated baby, and with mortality, the outcome that is hardest to argue about.

Almond and coauthors compare one-year mortality just below and just above the 1,500-gram line, in the census of U.S. births from 1983 to 2002. Newborns just below — the ones tagged VLBW and treated more aggressively — have about a one-percentage-point lower one-year mortality rate than newborns just above. That is large in context: mortality just above the cutoff averages 5.5 percent, so the extra care is associated with something like a one-fifth reduction in deaths right at the margin. And it runs against the underlying gradient — heavier babies are normally healthier — so the discontinuity is hard to explain by anything other than the treatment.

What does it cost? Here they switch data sources, to hospital discharge records for five states from 1991 to 2006: charges jump by roughly $4,000 as you cross the threshold from above, against mean costs of about $40,000. Put the mortality reduction and the spending together and you get about $550,000 per statistical life saved, in 2006 dollars. Against standard regulatory values of a statistical life, which run into the millions, half a million dollars is cheap.

One caveat belongs with that number, because the paper states it and it is load-bearing. The $550,000 holds "under an assumption that observed medical spending fully captures the impact of the 'very low birth weight' designation on mortality." If crossing the line changes anything else that matters — attention, follow-up, how hard staff fight — the spending is not the whole treatment, and the true cost per life saved is higher than the headline. The assumption is untestable with these data.

Still, the first link holds: at this margin, more medical care really does save lives, at a price that clears any plausible bar.

And the benefits outlast the NICU

A skeptic could grant that and still worry that we are saving sicker children into harder lives — that the survivors carry lasting damage. The evidence points the other way, though less uniformly than I first wrote.

Prashant Bharadwaj, Katrine Løken, and Christopher Neilson took the same VLBW-cutoff design to administrative data from Chile and Norway, linking birth records to school records, in a 2013 paper in the American Economic Review. Children who got the extra care at birth had both lower mortality and higher test scores years later. On the magnitudes I have to be careful: the copy of that paper I could obtain is its online appendix, not the article, so I am relying on Daysal et al.'s published account of it, which reports math test-score effects of 0.152 standard deviations in Chile and 0.476 in Norway. (An earlier version of this post gave the range as 0.15 to 0.22 SD. The upper figure appears to have come from a different paper.)

This fits a much larger body of work on what economists call the fetal origins hypothesis — the finding that conditions very early in life cast long shadows. In their review Childhood Circumstances and Adult Outcomes: Act II (2018), Douglas Almond, Janet Currie, and Valentina Duque summarize a now-large literature with a striking bottom line: "relatively mild shocks in early life can have substantial negative impacts" decades later. The corollary is that help early in life can have outsized positive impacts. Sarah Miller and Laura Wherry, comparing cohorts whose mothers did and did not gain prenatal Medicaid coverage under the expansions of the 1980s, find in a 2019 paper that the covered cohorts grew up with fewer chronic conditions, fewer diabetes- and obesity-related hospitalizations, and higher high-school graduation rates. Tania Barham, following a maternal-and-child-health program in Matlab, Bangladesh, reports cognitive gains of 0.39 standard deviations in late childhood — a figure I flag as unverified, because the file I could obtain for that paper is also an appendix rather than the article.

There is one honest complication. The Danish study we're about to meet finds the test-score gains but no effect on childhood disability by age 10, and no effect on whether the treated child continues in education past the compulsory level. A quantile version of the design shows why: the gains don't appear at the bottom of the test-score distribution, so the children being helped were never the ones on the margin of dropping out. "Better outcomes" here means a better score, not a longer education.

The benefits don't stop with the child

Here is where it gets more surprising, and where the seed for this post comes in.

Meltem Daysal, Marianne Simonsen, Mircea Trandafir, and Sanni Breining ran the VLBW-cutoff design on Danish administrative data, but did something the earlier papers had not: they followed the treated child's family. Their 2022 paper in the Review of Economics and Statistics is worth describing precisely, because the design does a lot of work. They take focal children born 1982–1993 weighing between 1,300 and 1,700 grams, and keep only those with a gestational age of at least 32 weeks — below that, Danish medical guidelines prescribe the extra treatment regardless of birth weight, so there is no discontinuity to exploit. That leaves 2,156 focal children and 3,311 siblings. Because they test many outcomes across several family members, they bundle variables into indices and adjust p-values for multiple testing.

They confirm the familiar focal-child results, and then report two spillovers:

  • Siblings of a treated child score 0.375 standard deviations higher on a ninth-grade test index — 0.386 in language and 0.255 in math. To scale that: it is enough to close the language gap between children of immigrants and non-immigrants in Denmark, and it shrinks the test-score gap between children from the top and bottom tenth of the income distribution by 33 to 69 percent.
  • Mothers are 0.347 standard deviations less likely to fill an antidepressant prescription. The gain is short-lived, fading as the focal child ages, and there is no equivalent effect for fathers.

In other words, an intervention aimed squarely at one fragile newborn left the brothers and sisters scoring higher and the mother less likely to be medicated for depression — none of whom were the patient.

Two things that first version of this post got wrong here. The sibling gains, like the focal child's, do not show up as more schooling: "siblings' test score gains do not translate into a higher likelihood of pursuing education beyond the compulsory level." What does change is which track they take — siblings of treated children are more likely to be in an academic rather than a vocational one. And unlike the focal children, the sibling gains do appear across the whole test-score distribution.

This is one study, in one country, and I'll come back to how much weight it can bear. A related paper by Melanie Guldi, Amelia Hawkins, Jeffrey Hemmeter, and Lucie Schmidt uses a different birth-weight cutoff — eligibility for Supplemental Security Income at 1,200 grams — and reports in a 2024 study that the extra support changes family processes without moving maternal depression or measured child outcomes. I could not obtain that paper's full text either, so I report it as the authors' abstract describes it and lean on it for nothing.

Why would treating one child help the others?

A spillover you can't explain is a spillover you shouldn't fully trust, so it's worth asking how a treatment to one child reaches the rest of the household.

A calmer mother. A very sick newborn is a source of enormous family stress, and a healthier focal child plausibly lifts that weight. Daysal and coauthors go further than asserting this: they show the maternal mental-health gain is not only about the child surviving. It persists among the groups whose survival didn't improve — twins, and girls — which points to general health improvement, not just averted death. That maternal mental health is causally upstream of children's outcomes gets support elsewhere: Dilek Sevim, Victoria Baranov, Sonia Bhalotra and coauthors find in a 2024 study that a perinatal psychosocial intervention improved maternal mental health by 0.17–0.29 SD and narrowed the "depression gap" linking a mother's mental health to her child's skills — though in that trial the child's own cognitive development barely moved, and the socioemotional gains were transitory. Running the other way, Petra Persson and Maya Rossin-Slater show in a 2018 AER paper that a sharp prenatal shock — the death of a maternal relative during pregnancy — raises the child's use of ADHD medication in childhood and of anti-anxiety and depression medication in adulthood.

Reallocated attention, not a bigger pot. I originally wrote that a recovering child frees up time and money for the others. Daysal and coauthors looked, and that isn't what happens: there is no discontinuity in either parent's employment, days worked, or income, in the short run or the long run. Their conclusion is blunt — early-life medical interventions have "no impact on parental behavior that affects total household resources." What moves is the allocation: they find sibling gains are larger for siblings who were themselves healthier at birth, a pattern consistent with parents redirecting effort toward the children best placed to use it. The household doesn't get richer; it gets rearranged. (Denmark, with 52 weeks of parental leave, subsidised childcare and free health care, is also about the hardest place in the world to find an income channel — which is a caution about exporting this particular null.)

Siblings simply transmit. Even outside health, there is evidence that siblings shape one another. Adam Altmejd and coauthors show in a 2021 QJE that younger siblings follow older ones into the same college and major, in Chile, Croatia, Sweden, and the United States. Krzysztof Karbownik and Umut Ożek find sibling spillovers in school achievement using a regression discontinuity design in Florida. Javaeria Qureshi finds that an older sister's schooling raises her younger siblings' literacy and numeracy in Pakistan — and draws the general moral directly: evaluations "that ignore these spillovers on younger siblings systematically underestimate total benefits." I should be straight that of these three, only the Qureshi paper is one I could read in full; the other two I have only as abstracts, so treat them as pointers rather than as evidence I have checked.

How much should we believe this?

The chain has three links, and they deserve different confidence.

The mortality link is about as well established as this kind of research gets, and every number in it checks out against the original tables. Multiple clean cutoff designs, replicated in the United States, Chile, Norway, and Denmark, all point the same way.

The persistence link — that survivors do better, not worse — is solid in direction and softer in the details than I first claimed. The test-score gains are real and replicated. But in the one dataset where somebody looked, they did not become more schooling, they did not reduce disability, and they did not reach the bottom of the distribution. Two of the studies I cited for long-run benefits are ones whose full text I could not obtain.

The family-spillover link rests largely on one well-identified Danish study. It is a good study — a credible discontinuity, falsification checks at other birth-weight cutoffs, corrections for multiple testing — and its effect sizes are larger than I would have guessed. But 2,156 focal children is not a lot, the maternal gain fades, and the corroborating sibling-spillover literature I gestured at is suggestive-by-analogy rather than replication. Family spillovers are also not always benign: Jennifer Heissel finds that a teen birth harms the mother's younger sisters — test scores down 3.8 percentage points, grade repetition up 7.6. Whatever transmits good outcomes through a family can transmit bad ones.

There is also the standard regression-discontinuity caveat. These estimates are local to the roughly-1,500-gram margin, in well-resourced hospitals in rich countries. They tell us about the marginal baby at the cutoff, not the average premature infant, and not about neonatal care where the constraints are entirely different.

So: high confidence that early-life medical care is a good investment for the child; moderate, and appropriately hedged, confidence that family spillovers make it a better one than the standard ledger records.

A back-of-the-envelope

At about $550,000 per statistical life saved, treating these babies clears any reasonable value-of-life bar on the focal child alone. The spillovers don't have to be large to tilt the calculation further — and on the Danish estimates they aren't small. A treated child in that sample has, on average, roughly one and a half siblings in the analysis, each gaining on the order of 0.375 SD in test scores. Even discounting heavily for the fact that this is one country and one study, an effect of that size on a second child is not a rounding error against a 0.2–0.3 SD gain for the first. Add a mother measurably less likely to be on antidepressants in the years after the birth — which standard cost-benefit analyses value at zero, because they never look — and the narrow ledger isn't just narrow. It is biased toward under-investing.

What we don't know

The open questions are mostly about the second and third links. We have essentially one clean estimate of the sibling-and-mother spillover from early-life medical care, in one country, and we badly need replications — and longer follow-ups, to see whether sibling test-score gains that visibly do not become extra years of schooling eventually become earnings, health, or anything at all. We don't have a clean decomposition of the mechanism: Daysal and coauthors can rule out the household simply having more money, but "improved intrafamily interaction" is a residual, not a measured channel. We don't know whether spillovers shrink, vanish, or reverse for less marginal infants or in lower-resource health systems — and the one place we can be fairly sure the Danish result won't transfer cleanly is the part that depends on a very generous welfare state. And we know almost nothing about whether the same logic extends to other early-life interventions — vaccination, nutrition, prenatal care — though there's no obvious reason it wouldn't.

What we can say is this. When a hospital decides how hard to fight for a 1,499-gram newborn, the stakes are wider than that child's chart. The benefits, when they come, seem to ripple outward — to a brother's report card, to a sister's place in an academic track, to a mother's peace of mind. We should at least try to count them.

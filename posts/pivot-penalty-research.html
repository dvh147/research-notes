2026-06-02 · 15 min read · economics-of-science innovation

In early 2020, the entire scientific world tried to make the same pivot at once. Immunologists, economists, materials scientists, sociologists — an enormous number of researchers dropped what they were doing and turned to COVID-19. It was, arguably, the most justified mass redirection of scientific effort in living memory, and it came with a built-in reward: for a while, anything with "COVID" in the title got read, cited, and fast-tracked into good journals.

So here is an uncomfortable result. The researchers who pivoted hardest toward COVID — the ones who moved furthest from their own prior work — tended to produce their least impactful papers, even with the COVID tailwind at their backs. The premium for working on a pandemic was real, and it was almost exactly cancelled out by a penalty for working far from home.

That penalty is the subject of a 2025 Nature paper by Ryan Hill, Yian Yin, Carolyn Stein, Xizhao Wang, Dashun Wang, and Benjamin Jones, "The pivot penalty in research". Across 25.8 million papers and 1.7 million patents, they document a simple and discouraging regularity: the further a piece of research sits from its authors' previous work, the less likely it is to land. The penalty shows up in almost every field, in patents as well as papers, within a single person's own body of work — and it has been growing for fifty years.

This cuts against a lot of what we like to believe. We lionize the bold career change, the outsider with fresh eyes, the interdisciplinary leap. So it's worth getting the claim exactly right, because the data support a more specific — and more interesting — version of it than "stay in your lane." The penalty is not really a penalty on novelty. Novel, unexpected combinations of ideas are, if anything, the raw material of high-impact science. The penalty is on novelty that has been cut loose from a deep base of conventional expertise. Pivoting tends to buy you the first and spend the second. That trade, on average, is a bad one.

How far did you wander?

The first thing the authors needed was a ruler. How do you measure how far a paper sits from its author's past?

Their answer is intuitive. Look at the journals a paper cites, and compare that mix to the mix of journals the same author was citing over the previous three years. If the overlap is high, the paper is "close to home"; if the paper draws on an almost entirely different slice of the literature, it's a big pivot. Formally it's a cosine distance — a standard way to score how similar two lists are — running from 0 (no pivot: same intellectual neighborhood as your recent work) to 1 (a complete pivot: a wholly new neighborhood). For patents, they swap journals for technology classes and do the same thing.

A toy version makes it concrete. Imagine a labor economist whose last three years of papers lean on the same dozen labor and applied-micro journals. A new paper that again leans on those journals scores a pivot near 0. A new paper that instead leans entirely on virology and epidemiology journals scores a pivot near 1. Most real papers fall somewhere in the middle — a little reach, not a leap.

Now line papers up by pivot distance and ask how often each becomes a "hit" — landing in the top 5% of citations in its field and year. By construction the baseline hit rate is 5%. What Hill and coauthors find is a clean, monotonic slide. Papers with the smallest pivots hit 7.4% of the time — about 48% above baseline. Papers with the largest pivots hit just 2.2% of the time — roughly 56% below baseline. Put differently: your lowest-pivot work is more than three times as likely to be a hit as your highest-pivot work.

A back-of-the-envelope sharpens what that means for a career. A top-5% hit is rare — about 1 paper in 20 at baseline. At the low-pivot end you hit roughly 1 in 14; at the high-pivot end, about 1 in 45. So going from working near home to working far from it roughly triples the number of papers you must write to land one big one.

Two features make this more than a curiosity about averages. First, it holds within a researcher. Comparing a given scientist's own papers to each other — which nets out talent, ambition, and career stage — their lowest-pivot papers are about 40% more likely than their personal average to be a hit, and their highest-pivot papers about 36% less likely. The penalty isn't just "weaker scientists pivot more." The same person does worse when they wander. Second, it is nearly everywhere: the relationship is negative in 93% of 154 scientific fields and 91% of 127 patent technology classes, and it survives splitting the data by career stage, team size, and funding status. It even shows up in dollars: the biggest-pivot patents are worth about 29% less on the market than the smallest-pivot ones.

I read this as about as solid as observational science-of-science findings get. The dataset is enormous, the pattern is monotonic rather than a knife-edge, it replicates across papers and patents, and the within-person comparison removes the most obvious confound. The evidence that a pivot penalty exists is, to my eye, pretty unambiguous.

It's not novelty that's punished

Here's where the story gets more interesting than "specialists win." If pivoting hurts, you might conclude that science punishes unusual, boundary-crossing ideas. The opposite is closer to the truth.

Go back to a foundational result: Brian Uzzi, Satyam Mukherjee, Michael Stringer, and Benjamin Jones's 2013 Science paper, "Atypical Combinations and Scientific Impact". Studying 17.9 million papers, they ask what the citation superstars have in common. It is not maximal novelty. The highest-impact papers overwhelmingly rest on an exceptionally conventional core — the expected, well-trodden combinations of prior work for their field — while injecting a minority of genuinely atypical combinations on top. Papers with that "conventional core, novel tail" signature are about twice as likely to be runaway hits. Novelty pays, but only when it is anchored.

Now connect that to the pivot penalty, because the same Nature paper measured exactly this. High-pivot papers, it turns out, are more likely to contain atypical, novel combinations — roughly half of them score high on tail novelty, versus about a third of low-pivot papers. So pivoters are not short on novelty. What they are short on is conventionality: only about 27–30% of the highest-pivot papers score high on conventional grounding, against 79% of the lowest-pivot papers.

That is the crux of the whole story in two numbers. When you pivot, you bring the novel ingredient but lose the conventional base — and Uzzi and coauthors already told us the base is what makes the novelty cash in. You arrive in the new field with interesting outsider combinations and without the deep, tacit command of what is already known, what has been tried, what the field considers obvious. The result reads as novel-but-unmoored, and it lands softly. This is also why I'd resist "stay in your lane" as the lesson. The lesson is closer to: bring new ideas into a field you already command, rather than carrying yourself into a field you don't.

Why leaving home is expensive — and getting more so

If the penalty were constant, we might shrug. The striking part is that it has been growing for half a century, in both papers and patents. Why would moving away from your expertise cost more in 2015 than in 1975?

The most economical explanation is Benjamin Jones's "burden of knowledge" (2009, Review of Economic Studies). As a field's stock of knowledge grows, each newcomer has to learn more before reaching the frontier where original contributions are possible. Jones shows the fingerprints of this across invention: people start contributing later, specialize more narrowly, and work in larger teams over time. The frontier keeps receding from the door. And if simply getting to a frontier takes more years of immersion than it used to, then re-mastering a second frontier — which is what a big pivot demands — gets steadily more expensive. The growing pivot penalty is what a deepening burden of knowledge looks like from the individual researcher's chair.

Two more strands fit snugly. Jacob Foster, Andrey Rzhetsky, and James Evans, in "Tradition and Innovation in Scientists' Research Strategies" (2015, American Sociological Review), studied the choices of biomedical chemists and found an "essential tension": innovative, risky bets are simultaneously more likely to be ignored and more likely to be high-impact — but the citation bonus, on average, doesn't compensate for the added risk. Since careers reward a steady stream of solid papers, most scientists rationally play it safe and stay near what they know. (Sergey Kolesnikov, Eriko Fukumoto, and Barry Bozeman document the same "risk-smoothing" play-it-safe publishing directly, 2018.) The pivot penalty is the price tag on the risky option that most researchers are quietly declining to pay.

And Erin Leahey, Christine Beckman, and Taryn Stanko's "Prominent but Less Productive" (2017, Administrative Science Quarterly) gives us an independent corroboration from ~900 scientists and 32,000 papers. Spanning disciplines, they find, raises a scientist's visibility (more citations when it works) but lowers their productivity (fewer papers) and widens the spread of outcomes — "high-risk, high-reward." That cocktail — lower mean output, fatter tails — is exactly what you'd expect if reaching across fields imposes a real cognitive and collaborative cost. The pivot penalty and the interdisciplinarity penalty look like the same animal seen from two angles.

There's a broader backdrop, too. Michael Park, Erin Leahey, and Russell Funk argued in 2023 that papers and patents have become less "disruptive" over time — more consolidating, less likely to upend what came before. I'd flag this one carefully: the "CD index" behind it has been shown to be biased by citation inflation (Petersen, Arroyave, and Pammolli, 2024), so I wouldn't lean on the precise magnitude of a disruption decline. But notice that the critique that wounds the disruption story does not wound the pivot penalty: Hill and coauthors don't use the CD index at all — they use within-field citation percentiles and, crucially, within-person comparisons. The pivot result stands on sturdier measurement than the consolidation result it sits beside.

Objection! Isn't this just fresh eyes being ignored?

The strongest pushback runs like this: maybe pivoters don't do worse work — maybe the field just doesn't recognize it yet. Outsiders bring unfamiliar framings; gatekeepers and citers are slow to appreciate what they can't easily categorize. On this view the pivot penalty is a measurement artifact of impatience, and the truly original cross-field work is sitting in a drawer waiting to be discovered.

This objection has real teeth, and the honest thing is to concede what's true in it. Novel work is systematically under-appreciated in the short run: Jian Wang, Reinhilde Veugelers, and Paula Stephan show a "bias against novelty" (2017, Research Policy) in which highly novel papers are more likely to be big hits eventually but are under-cited in standard short windows and more often ignored at first. Interdisciplinary work, similarly, reaches its citation peak later than disciplinary work (Zhang and coauthors, 2024). And spanning fields can pay off in ways a single paper's citation count misses: one 2025 analysis finds interdisciplinary engagement is associated with greater long-run career resilience and steadier output. Recognition lag is genuine, and some of the pivot penalty surely reflects it.

But three things in the Nature paper suggest the penalty is mostly real, not mostly mismeasurement. First, it barely shrinks when you hold the audience fixed: publishing your pivot in the same journal as your prior work — same editors, same readers, same reputational halo — removes only about a quarter of the penalty. Reputation and audience matter, but they're a minority of the story. Second, and most telling, the penalty appears before anyone is cited at all: high-pivot preprints are about 35% less likely to clear the bar from preprint to published journal article. That's a quality-and-fit signal that has nothing to do with citation impatience. Third, the COVID experiment points the same way — even riding a recognition premium, hard pivots underperformed. When the work is given every benefit of the doubt and still lands softer, "they just didn't get it" gets harder to sustain.

Where I'd hand the objection a partial win is on the tails. A lower average hit rate is fully compatible with pivots producing the occasional paradigm-shifting home run — and those rare home runs may be worth far more to society than the dozens of soft landings cost. The pivot penalty is a statement about the average and the odds, not a denial that fresh eyes ever crack a field open. They do. Just less often than our favorite stories imply.

So how do you pivot well?

If you're going to pivot anyway — and sometimes you must, because a field dies, or a problem like a pandemic demands it — what does the evidence suggest? Here I'm extrapolating from mechanism more than from a clean causal test, so treat this as the most speculative part of the post.

Three hints fall out of the same literature. Don't shed your base — extend it. The Uzzi recipe says impact comes from novel combinations layered on a conventional core, so the better move is to import a new idea into a field you command rather than relocate wholesale into one you don't. Explore, then commit. Lu Liu and coauthors find that career "hot streaks" (2021, Nature Communications) are typically preceded by a burst of exploration followed by concentrated exploitation — a period of wandering that then resolves into focus. Permanent wandering isn't the pattern that precedes greatness; a wander that lands somewhere and digs in is. Borrow a base. Since teams are far more likely than solo authors to inject atypical combinations (Uzzi and coauthors again), and small teams disrupt while large teams develop (Lingfei Wu, Dashun Wang, and James Evans, 2019, Nature), pivoting alongside a collaborator who already owns the new field's conventional base is a way to get the novelty without paying the full re-mastering cost yourself.

Bottom line

The pivot penalty is real, large, remarkably general, and growing. On the best current evidence, moving your research far from your own prior work cuts your odds of high impact by roughly half to two-thirds, the effect holds within individual researchers and across nearly every field and patents too, and it has steepened over five decades. The most coherent reading of why is not that science hates new ideas — it clearly rewards them — but that high-impact ideas need a deep conventional foundation to stand on, and a big pivot trades that foundation away for novelty it can no longer fully cash in.

Let me bound that. This is a claim about averages and probabilities, not destinies: the distribution has a tail, and bold pivots still produce the occasional field-defining hit. It is measured in citations and patent value, which capture a lot but not everything we care about — some slow-burning, cross-field work is undervalued by any short citation window. And it is, importantly, an equilibrium, not a law of physics. The penalty is the joint product of how much there is to know (the burden of knowledge) and how science rewards its people (steady productivity over risky range). Both are movable. Tools that lower the cost of getting up to speed in a new field — and large language models are the obvious candidate — could shrink it; reward systems that discourage diversification (Abramo and coauthors, 2024) could be deepening it. There's even a level-of-analysis twist: at the level of nations, diversifying a research portfolio raises impact (Herron and coauthors, 2016), even as it lowers it for the individual who does the diversifying. What's costly for the wanderer can be valuable for the system that contains many of them.

What we don't know

Three open questions, in rough order of how much they'd change the picture.

Who pivots, and why? The within-person comparison handles fixed traits, but not timing: people may pivot precisely when their main line runs dry, which would make pivots look worse than the act of pivoting really is. The authors lean on retractions as a "push" into new territory, which helps, but a clean exogenous shock to which direction a given scientist jumps would settle far more.

Is the citation yardstick the right one? If the social payoff to the rare pivot home run is large enough, a privately rational fear of pivoting could be collectively wasteful — science might be under-exploring. Measuring the value of pivots, not just their odds, is the harder and more important problem, and we don't have a clean answer.

Will AI move the equilibrium? If the pivot penalty is fundamentally a burden-of-knowledge problem, then anything that cheaply transfers expertise — a tutor that gets you to a new frontier in months instead of years — should bend the curve. Whether that actually shrinks the penalty, or just lets everyone pivot into the same crowded places, is exactly the kind of question the next decade of this data will get to answer.

For now, the practical takeaway is unromantic but well-supported: novelty is your friend, and your expertise is the ground you need to stand on to throw it. Pivots that keep one foot at home tend to beat pivots that leap with both.

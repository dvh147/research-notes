In April 2024, the Federal Trade Commission did something it had never done before: it voted, 3–2, to ban almost every non-compete agreement in the United States. The legal case was about wages — the FTC argued that clauses forbidding workers from joining a competitor hold pay down. But buried in the agency's own fact sheet was a more surprising set of numbers. Freed from non-competes, the FTC projected, Americans would start about 8,500 more businesses a year, and the economy would produce somewhere between 17,000 and 29,000 additional patents annually over the following decade. The claim wasn't just that non-competes are bad for paychecks. It was that they are bad for invention.

(The rule never took effect. A federal judge in Texas struck it down nationwide in Ryan LLC v. FTC in August 2024, holding the FTC lacked the authority; under a new administration the agency dropped its appeal in September 2025, and the question went back to the states, where it has always lived. So this is a live fight, not a settled one.)

There's an old piece of Silicon Valley folklore lurking behind those patent numbers. Why did the personal-computer and chip industries explode in California rather than around Boston's Route 128, which in 1975 looked like the stronger bet? The legal scholar Ronald Gilson gave a provocative answer in a 1999 article: California, almost uniquely, refuses to enforce non-competes, while Massachusetts enforced them. In California, an engineer with an idea her employer wouldn't fund could quit on Friday and incorporate on Monday; in Massachusetts she'd be looking at a lawsuit. On this telling, an obscure quirk of state contract law quietly set the velocity of an entire regional economy — by deciding whether knowledge could walk across the street.

It's a great story. Is it true? That's what this post is about. The claim I want to defend, with carefully calibrated confidence:

Enforceable non-competes reduce inventor mobility, and because so much know-how travels inside people's heads, they throttle the cross-firm flow of knowledge. The best evidence is that this slows innovation, on net, in the places and industries that enforce them. Non-competes do buy something real in return — they protect a firm's incentive to invest in its people and its research — but that benefit turns out to be the smaller one.

The argument is a chain with three links and one honest complication, so let me lay the pieces on the table before walking through them. Link one: do non-competes actually keep inventors from leaving? Link two: when an inventor leaves, does knowledge leave with her? Link three: does choking off that movement actually show up as less invention in a region? And the complication: if non-competes stop your rivals from poaching the people you trained, don't they make you more willing to train and research in the first place? Each link has to hold for the claim to stand. Let's take them in order.

Link one: do non-competes really lock people in?

Start with the cleanest natural experiment in the literature, and the paper that seeds this post. In 1985 Michigan passed an antitrust reform and, apparently by accident, repealed a decades-old statute that had made non-competes void — flipping the state overnight from not enforcing them to enforcing them. Marx, Strumsky, and Fleming (2009) treat that switch as a natural experiment, comparing the job mobility of Michigan's patent-holding inventors with inventors elsewhere before and after, and carefully controlling for the fortunes of the auto industry. Once non-competes became enforceable, inventor mobility fell by roughly 8% relative to the control states — and the drop was noticeably sharper for inventors with firm-specific skills and for specialists in narrow technical fields. Exactly the people whose knowledge is most valuable to a rival are the people non-competes pin down hardest.

That 8% is one state and one policy flip. The more comprehensive estimate comes from Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei (2023), who exploit the steady drip of enforceability changes across many states over several decades. Their summary statistic: an average-sized increase in a state's non-compete enforceability reduces inventor mobility by about 22%. The direction is never in doubt across these studies; only the size moves with how big a policy change you're describing.

And the bite extends past the people who actually sign. Starr, Frake, and Agarwal (2019) show that in state-industries where non-competes are common and enforceable, all workers — including those who never signed one — get fewer job offers, move less, and earn less. A thick web of non-competes raises the temperature of the whole local labor market, because recruiters learn not to bother poaching from firms that litigate. The flip side appears when a state bans them: when Oregon barred non-competes for hourly workers in 2008, Lipsitz and Starr (2022) find job-to-job mobility rose and hourly wages climbed 2–3% on average (and plausibly 14–21% for the workers actually bound).

I read link one as well established. Multiple credible designs — a clean natural experiment, a many-state panel, a state ban — all point the same way: enforceable non-competes measurably reduce mobility, most of all for the specialists who matter most.

Link two: knowledge walks out the door with people

Here's why a labor-market fact becomes an innovation fact. A great deal of valuable technical knowledge is tacit — it lives in an engineer's hands and judgment, not in any document a competitor could read. The main way that kind of knowledge crosses from one firm to another is that the engineer herself crosses.

The foundational evidence is Almeida and Kogut's (1999) study of semiconductor patents. They show that knowledge spillovers — measured by which later patents cite which earlier ones — are geographically localized, but not everywhere equally: localization is strongest in regions like Silicon Valley. And the thing that does the localizing is people. By tracking the inter-firm moves of major patent holders, they show that the local transfer of knowledge follows the engineers as they job-hop; in their phrase, the flow of knowledge is "embedded in regional labor networks." Knowledge stays local because workers stay local — but it spreads within the region because workers move between its firms.

How much does an individual move actually transfer? Singh and Agrawal (2011) give the sharpest number. When a firm hires an inventor, its use of that inventor's prior inventions (again, via citations) jumps by about 219%. But — and this is the honest part — they find roughly half of that is simply the recruit continuing to build on her own past ideas, and most of the rest is confined to her immediate new collaborators rather than diffusing across the whole organization. Mobility moves knowledge, but the spillover beyond the person who moved is narrower than the folklore implies. The knowledge doesn't broadcast; it seeps, person to person.

That picture is corroborated from several angles: Song, Almeida, and Wu (2003) find learning-by-hiring is strongest when the hired engineer's expertise is distant from what the new firm already knows, and Corredoira and Rosenkopf (2010) show the flow even runs backwards — the firm that loses an engineer later starts citing the firm that gained her, especially across distance. People are two-way pipes for ideas.

So link two is also well established in its core form: mobility is a real, important channel for the diffusion of tacit knowledge. The inference the claim needs — that less mobility therefore means less diffusion — is strong, though slightly more indirect, since firms have other, weaker channels (publications, suppliers, reverse-engineering) that don't depend on anyone changing jobs.

Link three: does the region actually invent less?

Now the payoff question. If non-competes pin down inventors, and pinned-down inventors don't spread their knowledge, do enforcing regions visibly innovate less? This is where the evidence is newer and a bit thinner — but it is increasingly consistent.

The capstone is the same Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei (2023) paper. Using state enforceability changes, they estimate that an average-sized increase in enforceability causes about a 13% decline in patenting, alongside the 22% drop in mobility we already met. Crucially, they trace the mechanism: the fall in mobility is what stifles knowledge diffusion, and the effect doesn't stay neatly inside state lines — because firms operate across states, tightening non-competes in one state dampens innovation at connected establishments elsewhere. Their unsettling conclusion is that conventional state-by-state estimates, if anything, understate the economy-wide damage.

Two earlier studies pointed the same direction from different starts. Samila and Sorenson (2011) ask what a region gets back from its venture capital, and find the answer depends on the legal regime: a given increase in local VC produces significantly more patents, more new firms, and more employment in states that restrict non-competes than in states that enforce them. The enforcement regime acts like a tax on the entrepreneurial recycling of talent and ideas. And Contigiani, Hsu, and Barankay (2018) study a cousin policy — the "inevitable disclosure doctrine," which lets employers block a departure by arguing the worker would inevitably reveal trade secrets — and find that when a state strengthens this employer-friendly protection, the affected inventors' own patenting falls. Their interpretation is pointed: tightening the leash is, paradoxically, against employers' own innovation interests.

There's even a geographic sting in the tail. Marx, Singh, and Fleming (2015), back at the Michigan experiment, show enforcement doesn't only freeze inventors in place — it drives some of them to emigrate to non-enforcing states, a brain drain concentrated among the most collaborative and most impactful inventors. An enforcing region can lose its best people to California twice over: it can't recycle them internally, and it can't keep them.

I'd put link three at the best evidence suggests, fairly convincingly — one strong, recent, direct causal estimate plus several converging designs, but fewer clean experiments than link one, and (as we're about to see) a real countervailing force to weigh.

Objection! Don't non-competes make firms invest more?

This is the strongest case for non-competes, and it deserves a fair hearing rather than a brush-off. The logic is hold-up. If I spend a fortune training you, or hand you the keys to my secret process, and you can stroll over to my competitor next quarter, then I'll under-invest — I'll train you less and tell you less. A non-compete solves that: protected from poaching, I'll happily invest, and more investment could mean more innovation. On this view non-competes aren't a drag on invention; they're what makes risky, long-horizon invention safe to fund.

The evidence that this channel is real is genuine. Conti (2014) finds that firms in states with stricter enforcement pursue riskier R&D — their inventions land more often in the tails, as either breakthroughs or flops, and in more novel technical areas — precisely because lower leakage makes high-variance bets more valuable to hold. Chen, Balasubramanian, and Forman (2024) find that when worker mobility gets easier, firms become less willing to adopt a costly new technology (machine learning) whose payoff depends on workers they might lose. So mobility is not a free lunch; somebody pays for the training and the bets, and non-competes shift who.

Here is why I still come down where I do. The two studies built specifically to weigh the trade-off both find the mobility side dominant. Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei confirm that stricter enforceability does raise firms' R&D spending — and find innovation falls anyway, because the diffusion loss outweighs the investment gain. And Liyan Shi's (2023) structural model in Econometrica, built to price exactly this tension, concludes that employers use non-competes to extract rent from future employers in a way that is socially excessive, so that the welfare-optimal policy is "quantitatively close to a ban." When the people who most carefully model the upside of non-competes still come out against them, that's telling. The appropriability benefit is real; it is simply the smaller quantity.

Here I'm a touch more speculative than the studies themselves: my read is that the investment channel is strongest for firm-and-job-specific training that has little value elsewhere, and weakest exactly where innovation policy cares most — general, recombinable, frontier knowledge, whose entire social value lies in its ability to travel. Non-competes are a blunt instrument that protects the first by also imprisoning the second.

Objection! Enforceability isn't the same as the contract — or its use

A subtler complication, and the one that should keep us from overclaiming the Silicon Valley legend. "Enforceability" is a property of state law; whether non-competes actually bind workers is a separate, messier question. Starr, Prescott, and Bishara (2021), surveying over 11,000 American workers, find that about 18% are currently bound by a non-compete and roughly 38% have signed one at some point — and, strikingly, that non-competes are nearly as common in states where courts won't enforce them. Many workers comply with a clause they could safely ignore, simply because they don't know it's void or don't want to risk a fight. So a court's willingness to enforce is only part of what actually chills mobility, which both widens the policy's reach and blurs any clean "enforce vs. don't" comparison.

The Silicon Valley story needs the same discipline. Cheyre, Klepper, and Veloso (2015) show that much of the famously high inventor mobility in Silicon Valley was driven by spinoffs — and that spinoffs spurred mobility even before the industry clustered there and even in settings without California's non-compete ban. The legal regime was one ingredient, not the whole recipe; Stanford, defense contracts, the founding of Fairchild, and the semiconductor industry's own structure were all in the pot. Non-competes are also far from the only institution that pins inventors down: Melero, Palomeras, and Wehrheim (2020) find that simply being granted one more patent makes an inventor about 23% less likely to change employers, as the patent turns her skills into employer-specific assets. And firms aren't passive: facing mobile workers, they shift toward patenting rather than secrecy and reorganize to collaborate more internally. The system has many valves; non-competes are one prominent one, not the master switch.

One last nuance worth holding, because it cuts against a lazy version of the claim. Starr, Balasubramanian, and Sakakibara (2018), studying 5.5 million new firms, find that enforcement reduces the number of within-industry spinouts but raises their quality — the spinouts that do form are larger, founded by higher earners, and more likely to survive. "Fewer startups" is not automatically "worse outcomes"; some of what non-competes screen out is noise. The honest claim is about the level of inventive activity and diffusion, not a blanket verdict that every deterred move was a loss.

A rough sense of the stakes

How much is at issue? Take the most direct estimate at face value: if a high-enforcement state dialed its regime down toward a low-enforcement one — an average-sized change — Johnson, Lipsitz, and Pei imply patenting there would rise on the order of 13%, and by more once cross-state spillovers are counted. Scale that intuition to the country and you land in the same neighborhood as the FTC's own projection of 17,000–29,000 extra patents a year — very roughly 2–3% of annual U.S. patents.

Treat those figures gently. The FTC was an advocate making a case, and outside analysts were skeptical of the precision (the 8,500-new-businesses figure in particular leans on extrapolation). But the order of magnitude is not crazy, and it converges with the academic estimates from the opposite direction. A few percent of national inventive output is not a rounding error; it is comfortably larger than what most of the R&D tax credits and subsidy tweaks economists argue about would plausibly deliver — and this one costs the government nothing to pull.

Bottom line

Here is how I read the whole chain. Enforceable non-competes reduce the mobility of inventors — that is well established. The knowledge those inventors carry diffuses largely through their mobility — also well established. Choke the mobility and you choke the diffusion, and the best and most direct evidence is that enforcing regions and industries innovate measurably less as a result — which I hold as fairly convincing, and strengthening, on the back of one strong recent causal estimate and several converging older ones. Non-competes buy a genuine benefit in exchange — they relieve the hold-up problem and make firms more willing to invest and to take big research bets — but the two studies built to weigh the trade-off both find that benefit is outweighed by the diffusion loss.

Now the bounds. The Silicon-Valley-versus-Route-128 legend is supported in direction but should not be oversold as a clean natural experiment — Silicon Valley had many parents, and law was only one. The policy lever is blunter than it looks, because non-competes chill mobility even where they're unenforceable. And "less invention" is a statement about the level and diffusion of inventive activity, not a promise that every deterred job-hop was a social loss; some screening is real. I'd summarize my own position as: lean strongly toward restricting non-competes for inventors and knowledge workers on innovation grounds — not "ban everything everywhere, QED."

What we don't know

  • The aggregate, general-equilibrium number. Most estimates are regional or firm-level. If every state restricted non-competes at once, would national invention rise by the sum of the local effects, or would competition for a fixed pool of inventors wash much of it out? The cross-state spillover evidence hints the total is larger, not smaller — but it's an inference, not a measurement.
  • The shape of the investment trade-off. We know firms invest and bet more under enforcement, and innovate less on net. We don't have a clean map of which industries or training types tip which way — where appropriability protection is worth the diffusion cost and where it isn't.
  • Mechanism versus magnitude on diffusion. That knowledge rides on mobility is settled; how much aggregate diffusion is actually lost when a marginal inventor is frozen in place — as opposed to leaking through other channels — is weakly pinned down.
  • The behavioral margin. Since non-competes bind even where void, the real policy question may be less "should courts enforce them" than "what stops workers from ignoring unenforceable ones." That's a question about information and norms that the enforcement literature barely touches.
  • Beyond patents. Nearly all of this is measured in patents and patent citations. Whether the same holds for software, design, process know-how, and the un-patented frontier is largely untested.

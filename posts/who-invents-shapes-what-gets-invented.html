Of all the U.S. biomedical patents filed between 1976 and 2010, the ones invented entirely by women were 35% more likely to focus on women's health than the ones invented entirely by men. That is the central finding of Koning, Samila, and Ferguson (2021), who read the text of every biomedical patent in that window and sorted them by whose body the invention was for. Same patent office, same decades, same broad field of medicine — change only the sex of the inventors, and the subject of the invention moves.

It is tempting to wave this away as obvious: of course women think about women's health. But sit with the implication, because it is not obvious at all. The question "what gets invented?" is usually answered with money — we invent things people will pay for. Koning and his coauthors are pointing at a second, stranger answer. Part of what gets invented depends on who happens to be in the room holding the pipette.

Here is the claim I want to defend, with calibrated confidence: who gets to invent shapes what gets invented. Inventor identity steers the direction of innovation — which problems get attacked — and not merely its volume. The cleanest case is gender and health: because women are a small minority of inventors, the problems women would disproportionately work on are systematically under-invented. Missing inventors, it turns out, mean missing inventions — and fairly predictable ones.

One scoping note. Economists distinguish the rate of innovation (how much we invent) from its direction (what we invent). This post is entirely about direction. And the evidence is strongest for one identity margin — gender — and one domain — health — so that is where I will spend most of it, widening out only where the data let me.

What we already knew: demand steers invention

Start with the explanation that needs no inventor's identity at all, because it is well established and it is the thing the identity story has to beat.

The direction of invention responds to demand. Make a market bigger or richer, and invention flows toward it. The canonical evidence is Acemoglu and Linn (2004), who used predictable shifts in U.S. demographics — the aging of the baby boom, say — to forecast which drug categories were about to gain customers, and found that a larger potential market size sharply raised the entry of new drugs and new molecular entities. The effect held up after controlling for the supply side of pharmaceutical research, which is what makes it convincing: the drugs followed the patients, not the other way around.

This is not a one-paper result. Dubois and coauthors (2015) put the elasticity of innovation to market size at about 0.23 — roughly, it takes about $2.5 billion in extra expected revenue to call forth one new chemical entity. Blume-Kohout and Sood (2013) found that when Medicare Part D expanded drug coverage for seniors, R&D rose in precisely the therapeutic classes seniors use most. And Costinot, Donaldson, Kyle, and Williams (2019), in a paper bluntly titled "The More We Die, the More We Sell?", show that a country's demographic profile predicts which diseases its people die from, which predicts which drugs its firms invent and export. Demand pulls direction. On this, the evidence is about as unambiguous as innovation economics gets.

So before crediting anything to inventors' identities, you have to clear demand out of the way. If women's-health patents come disproportionately from women, maybe that is just because the market for women's health was growing and women happened to be standing near it. Hold that objection; it is the right one, and I will come back to it.

Inventors invent for people like themselves

Now the supply side. The striking thing about the recent evidence is that who the inventor is predicts what they invent even after you do your best to hold demand fixed.

Koning et al.'s 35% is the headline, and three features make it more than a curiosity. It holds across research areas, not just in one obviously gendered niche. It holds across decades, from the 1970s to the 2000s. And it comes from a clean within-system comparison — all-female versus all-male teams filing in the same patent office. Their conclusion is worth quoting: "the inventor gender gap is partially responsible for thousands of missing female-focused inventions since 1976 ... who benefits from innovation depends on who gets to invent."

If the pattern were confined to biomedical patents, I would treat it as suggestive. But a second, independent line of work finds the same thing in an entirely different dataset, using sales rather than patent text. Einiö, Feng, and Jaravel, in a working paper aptly called "Social Push and the Direction of Innovation," link inventors to the products their industries sell and ask who buys those products. Inventors, they find, create goods disproportionately bought by people demographically like themselves — along gender, age, and income. Female inventors work in industries where the share of sales going to female-headed households is 2.2 percentage points higher, about 3.9% above the national baseline. The same shows up for age (young inventors tilt toward goods the young buy) and for income.

Two very different designs — text analysis of twentieth-century biomedical patents on one side, modern product-sales linkages on the other — converge on one conclusion: inventors aim at the world they know. That convergence is why I read the basic correlation as fairly convincing, not merely suggestive.

Is it identity, or demand in disguise?

Back to the objection I parked. Couldn't all of this still be demand? Women's-health markets did grow; women may sort into the fields serving them. Disentangling "who invents" from "what's profitable" is the whole ballgame, and two pieces of the evidence do it.

First, Einiö, Feng, and Jaravel find their effect within detailed industries, not just across them. Compare two inventors in the same narrow line of business — same customers available to both, same demand environment — and the one who is a woman still tilts toward the products women buy. Demand is held roughly fixed by construction; the inventor's identity still moves the output.

Second, and to me the most telling result here: the effect runs through power, not mere presence. In their companion paper, Koning, Samila, and Ferguson (2020) show that a 10-percentage-point increase in the female share of inventors in a research area yields about 1.2 percentage points more female-focused patents — but only when women lead the team. Areas that add women in supporting roles get no shift in direction at all. A pure demand story has no reason to care whether the women in a lab are principal investigators or assistants; a story about whose ideas actually get pursued cares enormously. The hinge is decision-making authority — exactly what you'd predict if the mechanism is "inventors chase the problems they personally find compelling, once they're the ones choosing."

Which raises the obvious question: why would identity shape invention at all? The likeliest answer is mundane and powerful — you invent for problems you can see and feel. Your own needs, your family's, the frustrations of people like you are the ones salient enough to notice and motivating enough to solve. This is the old observation from the user-innovation literature that much invention begins with someone scratching their own itch; Mendonça and Reis (2020) find, consistent with it, that gender differences in who innovates are large while differences in how they innovate are small. And it matters that this travels with the person, not the employer: Bhaskarabhatla, Cabral, Hegde, and Peeters (2021), using 37 years of U.S. patent data, estimate that an inventor's own human capital is five to ten times more important than the capabilities of the firm employing them in explaining inventive output. If the inventor — not the firm — is the engine, then the menu of what gets invented is, in a real sense, the aggregate of who the inventors happen to be.

I'd put the supply-side identity channel at reasonably convincing: the within-industry result and the leadership asymmetry are hard to explain with demand or sorting alone. It is not a randomized experiment, so I stop short of "unambiguous."

Objection! It's sorting, or it's measurement

Two more deflations deserve a hearing.

Maybe it's just sorting. Women with the relevant interests choose obstetrics or gynecology; men choose urology; the patents follow the specialty, and "identity" does no independent work. This is real and surely explains part of the raw gap. But it cannot easily explain the within-area and within-industry findings, where the comparison is between men and women in the same field. Nor can it explain why leadership is the hinge. Sorting moves people between fields; it does not tell you why a female-led team inside a field invents differently from a male-led one beside it.

Maybe it's measurement. Koning et al. infer "focuses on women's health" from patent text, which is noisy, and Einiö et al. infer customers from industry sales, which is coarse. Fair. But measurement error of this kind usually attenuates effects toward zero — it makes real patterns harder to find, not easier to manufacture — and the effects here are large, stable across five decades, and replicated in a second design that shares none of the first's measurement choices. I don't think the result is an artifact of how "female-focused" was coded.

What I will concede is that none of this is a clean experiment in which inventors' identities were randomly assigned. We are reading consistent patterns across observational designs, each closing a different loophole. That is good social science; it is not a lab result, and I hold it accordingly.

Missing inventors, missing inventions

If you grant the claim, the cost follows — and it compounds.

Start with how few of the inventors are women. Hunt, Garant, Herman, and Munroe (2013) found that women held just 5.5% of commercialized patents; more recent counts put women at roughly an eighth of inventors. Their decomposition is the uncomfortable part: the patent gap is concentrated among people who already hold science and engineering degrees, driven by women's underrepresentation in engineering and in the development-and-design jobs where patentable ideas are born. Fixing the pipeline at school is necessary but, on this evidence, far from sufficient.

Now combine the two facts — women are a small minority of inventors, and inventors tilt toward problems they know — and under-invention in women's needs follows almost mechanically. A crude back-of-the-envelope makes the stakes concrete (treat it as illustration, not estimate): if women are about an eighth of inventors and all-female teams target women's health at roughly a 35% higher rate, then moving toward parity in the lab would visibly tilt the biomedical portfolio toward women's health — with no change in anyone's talent and no change in the underlying biology. Koning et al.'s own phrase, "thousands of missing female-focused inventions since 1976," is the headline version of that arithmetic.

And here is the part that should worry you most: the supply gap and the demand gap point the same way. The demand-pull literature says we invent for big, well-funded markets. The identity literature says we invent for the people in the lab. For much of the period studied, women had both less voice as inventors and less market power as buyers — so women's needs were under-weighted twice over, once on each side of the ledger. These two market failures are not independent; they reinforce each other.

That reframes diversity in invention from a fairness question into an efficiency one. Oksenberg and Zehavi (2025) make the policy version of the argument: they document the beginnings of what they call "consumption-targeted innovation for women" in the EU, Austria, and Israel, and frame the status quo as a market failure — "innovation designs that systematically cater to male needs and preferences while mostly ignoring those of women." Their sharpest observation is that almost all the policy energy goes to the production side (get more women inventing) and almost none to the consumption side (invent for women, whoever does it). The identity evidence suggests those are linked: the most reliable way to get more invention aimed at women may simply be to put more women in charge of inventing.

Bottom line

Does who invents shape what gets invented? I read the evidence this way. The basic correlation — inventor identity tracks invention direction — is fairly convincing, because two unlike designs (biomedical patent text; consumer-product sales) find it at similar magnitudes. That this reflects a genuine supply-side channel, separate from demand, is reasonably convincing, resting on the within-industry result and the finding that only female leadership moves direction. The women's-health case is the cleanest instance; the generalization to age and income is suggestive (Einiö et al. see it, in one dataset), and the further leap to race, class, or disability is plausible but not yet well evidenced — that part is more speculation than finding.

Let me bound it. I am not claiming inventors only serve their own kind; demand is real and often dominant, and most invention is bought by people unlike its inventor. I am not claiming the "missing inventions" toll is precisely thousands, or precisely anything — that number is a model-based direction, not a measured quantity, and it deserves wide error bars. The defensible claim is narrower and still important: a measurable, replicated slice of what society invents is set by who society lets invent, and the shortfall in women's health is the clearest place we can see it.

What we don't know

  • How big the shortfall is. "Thousands of missing inventions" is a sign with a plausible direction, not a measured magnitude. Nobody has a credible point estimate of the welfare cost of under-inventing for women, and the macro growth counterfactuals in this literature are model-based — I would not bank on a specific number.
  • How far it generalizes. Gender and health are nailed down best. The extension to age and income rests largely on one dataset; race, class, and disability are plausible but understudied. The effect could be far broader than women's health, or unusually concentrated there.
  • Which mechanism. "You invent for problems you know" lumps together lived need, accumulated knowledge, and plain attention. We don't know the mix, and the mix matters: a knowledge story and a motivation story imply different fixes.
  • Whether the lever works as advertised. The counterfactual — add women inventors, get more women-focused invention — assumes new inventors would behave like today's women inventors. Selection could break that. The clean test would randomize who gets to lead.
  • The other identities. If inventors invent for people like themselves, then the dominance of any narrow group — by nationality, discipline, or class — quietly bends the global menu of invention in ways we have barely begun to measure.

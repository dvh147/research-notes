"We sent them children fleeing the war and they returned gang members to a country that needed to build peace… The gangs that arrived with the deportees grew at an unstoppable speed." — Carlos Dada, Salvadoran journalist, 2018 (epigraph to Sviatschi 2022)

MS-13 — Mara Salvatrucha — was not born in San Salvador. It was born on the streets of 1980s Los Angeles, among the children of families who had fled El Salvador's civil war. Those kids formed gangs for the usual reasons that immigrant kids in rough neighborhoods always have; the gangs were not, at first, especially violent. Then many of their members cycled through California prisons, where they picked up something more dangerous than a criminal record: working knowledge of extortion, drug distribution, and organized violence. And then, after a 1996 law made any non-citizen who had served more than a year deportable, the United States began shipping them "home" — to a small, post-war country many of them barely remembered.

What happened next is the subject of one of the more unsettling natural experiments in recent economics. The short version: the gangs did not stay deported. They put down roots, recruited a new local generation, and helped turn El Salvador into one of the most homicidal places on earth — and the violence then pushed a fresh wave of children north, back toward the border the policy was meant to seal.

The naive theory of deporting criminals is close to a free lunch for the sending country: subtract the bad actors, subtract their crime. This post is about why that theory is wrong often enough to matter. The claim I want to defend is narrow:

Deporting criminals can backfire. Removing offenders from a rich country does not destroy crime so much as relocate it — and when the people you remove carry transferable criminal skills into a weak state, they can seed violent organizations whose fallout, including migration, flows back to you.

One honest qualifier up front, because it does a lot of work: this is not the claim that deportation always backfires, or that immigrants are dangerous. The backfire needs two ingredients — offenders who carry genuinely transferable criminal capital, and a receiving state too weak to contain them. Take either ingredient away and, as we'll see, the story weakens. With that caveat planted, let me march through the chain link by link: (1) did deportation really seed the gangs? (2) why does crime travel with people at all? (3) why do weak states catch fire? (4) how does the fallout boomerang back as migration? and (5) the honest other side — doesn't removing criminals at least cut crime somewhere?

The experiment we ran by accident

Start with the anchor. In "Spreading Gangs: Exporting US Criminal Capital to El Salvador" (American Economic Review, 2022), Maria Micaela Sviatschi turns the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act into a quasi-experiment. The Act drastically raised criminal deportations; between 1998 and 2014 US authorities logged almost 300,000 deportations of people with criminal records to Central America, El Salvador chief among the destinations.

The identification problem is obvious — gangs might settle in already-troubled places — and Sviatschi's fix is clever. Deported Salvadorans mostly returned to their municipality of birth, so she instruments where US gangs landed in El Salvador with the birthplaces of the gang members the US deported, and she compares municipalities that did versus didn't receive them, before versus after 1996. Because gangs recruit the young — more than 60% of Salvadoran gang members join before age 15 — she can also compare cohorts who were different ages when the deportees arrived. Pre-trends in the receiving and non-receiving municipalities look similar before the shock.

The results line up disturbingly well with the gang-origin story. Municipalities that received deportees saw a large jump in gang activity and in the crimes gangs specialize in — extortion and drug trafficking — and a rise in homicide. Crucially, there was no parallel rise in crimes unrelated to gangs, which is the kind of placebo you want: it says the effect is about gangs specifically, not a general municipal collapse. And the damage propagated to people who had never set foot in the US: Salvadoran children who were in their early teens when the deportees arrived were more likely, as adults, to end up incarcerated for gang-related crime. The gangs didn't just relocate; they reproduced.

A single paper, however careful, is a single paper. So it matters that an independent study, by a different author with a different design, finds the same thing. In "The impact of US deportation of criminals on gang development and education in El Salvador" (Journal of Development Economics, 2018), Priti Kalsi notes that gangs make their money mainly by extorting businesses, so they should put down roots where there are businesses to shake down. She finds exactly that: regions with higher pre-deportation business density became disproportionately more violent as criminal deportations rose, and — her main outcome — children there lost schooling, boys most of all, as a weakened local economy lowered the returns to staying in class. Two independent natural experiments, pointing the same way, on a genuinely surprising causal claim. I read this as fairly convincing evidence that, in El Salvador, criminal deportation helped manufacture the gang problem. Not "unambiguous" — it is one country, and the designs are observational — but well past "one study finds."

Crime is portable

Why should deporting a person move his crime with him? The answer is that criminal ability is not a fixed trait stapled to a place; it is human and social capital — skills, contacts, reputations — built up, carried around, and taught to others. This is an old idea with a clear lineage: ever since Gary Becker's economic approach to crime (Journal of Political Economy, 1968), economists have treated offending as a choice shaped by skills and opportunities rather than as a fixed type. The natural extension is that those skills are capital — and capital travels. Once you see crime that way, "export the criminal, export the crime" stops being a paradox.

The cleanest demonstration that criminal skill is real and transmissible comes from prisons, which double as schools. In "Production and persistence of criminal skills" (Journal of Development Economics, 2023), Escobar, Tobón and Vanegas-Arias use the universe of incarceration spells in Colombia and find that property crime spikes around prisons on the exact days inmates are released. Two groups drive it: "specialists" with prior theft convictions, and "trainees" — inmates with unrelated records who happened to do time alongside the specialists. Rehabilitation programs and longer sentences don't undo it. Prison, in other words, can produce the very skills it is supposed to deter — and the trainees carry them back out into the world.

This isn't a one-country curiosity. In a now-classic study, Bayer, Hjalmarsson and Pozen showed that juveniles held in the same correctional facility influence each other's later crime, reinforcing the specific offense types they already knew (QJE, 2009) — peer effects in the schooling of crime. Megan Stevenson finds a complementary channel: in juvenile jails it isn't only skills that spread but crime-oriented attitudes and norms (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2017), a social contagion that persists even among youths unlikely to meet again. Anna Aizer and Joseph Doyle, using randomly assigned judges as an instrument, show the institutional version of the same tragedy: juvenile incarceration lowers high-school completion and raises adult recidivism (QJE, 2015). Lock kids up among other offenders and you don't reliably deter them — you train and credential them.

Sviatschi herself supplied the developmental microfoundation in a second paper, "Making a Narco" (Econometrica, 2022). When US antidrug policy raised the returns to coca in suitable parts of Peru, parents pulled children into the illegal trade, and those children — having accumulated industry-specific criminal capital early — were about 30% more likely to be incarcerated for violent and drug crimes as adults. Tellingly, there was no such effect where the local coca fed the legal sector, and a cash-transfer program that kept kids in school blunted the effect. Criminal capital is built by exposure, it is specific to a trade, and schooling is a lever on it.

Put these together and the recruitment finding in El Salvador stops being mysterious. A deported gang "specialist" is a teacher. Drop him into a town full of teenagers with weak schools and few jobs, and he can convert local children — who never went anywhere — into a homegrown gang. One transplanted carrier, many new trainees: criminal capital comes with a multiplier. That crime is portable and transmissible I'd rate well established — it shows up across prisons, neighborhoods, and labor markets, in several quasi-experiments.

Why weak states catch fire

A seed needs soil. Transplanted criminals don't take over functioning states with capable police and courts; they take over places where the state is thin. The historical record on this is strikingly consistent.

Daron Acemoglu, Giuseppe De Feo and Giacomo De Luca trace the rise of the Sicilian Mafia to exactly such a vacuum: facing a socialist peasant movement and a state too weak to keep order, landowners turned to private enforcers. Using a 19th-century drought as an instrument for where the peasant movement organized, they estimate large, persistent negative effects of the Mafia on literacy, public goods and political competition (Review of Economic Studies, 2020). Criminal organizations thrive where the state has abdicated. The contemporary version is documented by Blattman, Duncan, Lessing and Tobón in "Gang Rule" (Review of Economic Studies, 2025): criminal groups govern millions of people, settling disputes and providing security — they fill vacuums of official power (and, the authors show in Medellín, can even rule alongside a present state to protect their rents). El Salvador in the late 1990s — emerging from civil war, institutions hollowed out — was about as fertile as soil gets.

We even have refugee evidence that exposure matters in a rich, capable state. Anna Piil Damm and Christian Dustmann exploit Denmark's quasi-random assignment of refugees to neighborhoods and find that boys placed in areas with more convicted youth are more likely to be convicted themselves later (AER, 2014), through social interaction. If proximity to crime breeds crime even in Denmark, it is no surprise that concentrating hardened deportees in a war-torn country with weak policing did far worse.

Here is where it helps to step outside El Salvador entirely — because the single most persuasive thing about this whole argument is that the "relocate criminals, seed organized crime" pattern keeps recurring in settings with nothing else in common. The most vivid is Jakub Lonský's study of the origins of the Russian mafia (Journal of Public Economics, 2025). Scraping biographies of more than 5,000 vory-v-zakone crime bosses, he shows the Russian mafia was effectively incubated in the Soviet Gulag, and that the towns near former camps still had mafia presence in the 1990s. Instrumenting mafia presence with proximity to the camps, he finds that when the Soviet state collapsed in 1991, those communities saw a dramatic surge in violent crime. A state concentrated criminals in one place; when the state later weakened, organized crime erupted from precisely those places. And in Italy, researchers document the mafia spreading into northern towns "with no mafia tradition," infiltrating healthy firms for money laundering and rent extraction (Mirenda, Mocetti and Rizzica, AER, 2022) — the organization migrating into new territory much as it did in El Salvador. (Alessandra Fenizia and Raffaele Saggio give a complementary account of mafia-infiltrated municipalities and their economic trajectory, AER, 2024.)

Soviet gulags, Sicilian villages, Italian boomtowns, Salvadoran barrios: four utterly different settings, one regularity. Concentrate or relocate criminal capital, supply a weak or weakening state, and you get organized crime. That the general mechanism holds I'd put at the best evidence suggests — convergent across cases, though each is a different margin and none is a randomized trial.

The boomerang

Now the part that makes deportation potentially self-defeating rather than merely cruel to the receiving country. Sviatschi's chain doesn't end with violence in El Salvador; the violence pushes people to flee, and many of them flee toward the United States. The policy meant to reduce pressure at the border helped generate it.

The crime-to-migration link is one of the better-identified pieces of this story, partly because we can watch it run in reverse. In 2022, El Salvador's president Bukele declared a state of exception and arrested suspected gang affiliates en masse; homicides plummeted. Abreha, Johnson and Robertson ask what happened to migration and find a striking answer: US border encounters with Salvadorans fell by roughly 45–67% relative to other nationalities (Journal of Development Economics, 2025). Cut the violence and the migration falls by something like half — which is the mirror image of Sviatschi's "raise the violence and migration rises."

It generalizes beyond El Salvador. Studying Mexico's drug war, Orozco-Alemán and González-Lozano find that local violence pushed migration to the US up by about 1.53 percentage points over 2007–2012 (Journal of Human Resources, 2018) — though violence along the route deters it, a nice reminder that these effects have moving parts. And Leo, Jurado and Ramírez-Álvarez show drug violence reshaping internal migration in Mexico, with a welfare cost they put on the order of 10% of GDP a year (Journal of Development Economics, 2024). Violence moves people; that part is fairly convincing.

So the loop closes: deport gang members → seed gang violence in a weak state → drive migration north → trigger more deportations. It is not a perpetual-motion machine — plenty leaks out at each step — but it means the border-control benefit a sending country books from deportation is partly clawed back by the migration its own policy helps create abroad.

"But surely removing criminals cuts crime somewhere?"

Here is the strongest objection, and it deserves a fair hearing. Removing active, violent gang members from Los Angeles surely made Los Angeles a bit safer. Isn't the policy at least locally rational, whatever the spillovers? Three honest concessions and three honest qualifications.

Concession: yes, at the source. Pulling genuinely dangerous, active offenders out of a community plausibly lowers that community's crime in the short run. Nothing here denies that. The El Salvador disaster is an externality of the policy, not proof the policy did nothing useful at home.

But — "deport people to cut crime" mostly misfires, because immigrants aren't the problem. The premise that removing immigrants is a broad crime-control tool runs into a wall of evidence. In "Do Immigrants Cause Crime?" (JEEA, 2012), Bianchi, Buonanno and Pinotti instrument immigrant inflows across Italian provinces and find an effect on robberies only, with the net effect on overall crime statistically indistinguishable from zero. Ajzenman, Domínguez and Undurraga study a tripling of the immigrant population in Chile and likewise find no effect on actual crime — only a rise in fear and in spending on home security (AEJ: Applied, 2023). El Salvador "worked" as a backfire precisely because the 1996 policy removed a selected, hardened margin — convicted gang members — not immigrants in general. Generalize it to "deport immigrants and crime falls" and the evidence says you'll mostly be deporting people who weren't raising crime.

And — what actually reduces immigrant crime is opportunity, not removal. Mastrobuoni and Pinotti exploit the 2007 EU enlargement and find that gaining legal status cut immigrants' recidivism by about 50%, explaining one-half to two-thirds of the gap between legal and illegal immigrants' crime (AEJ: Applied, 2015). Running the other way, Freedman, Owens and Bohn show that when a US law cut off legal employment for some immigrants, felony charges among the affected group rose (AEJ: Policy, 2018). Access to legal work is doing the heavy lifting; closing it off backfires too.

And — enforcement tends to displace crime, not erase it, and carries collateral costs. Dean Yang's study of a Philippine customs crackdown is the clean template: tighten enforcement on one method of dodging duties and importers simply switch to another, leaving total evasion essentially unchanged (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2008). Crime, like water, finds another channel. We see the same shape in Rio's slum-pacification program, which Bellégo and Drouard find cut murders and robberies but pushed criminals toward assaults and threats (AEJ: Policy, 2024). And the home-country costs of immigration enforcement aren't confined to immigrants: East and coauthors show the US "Secure Communities" program reduced employment and wages of US-born workers too, by cutting local labor supply and demand (Journal of Labor Economics, 2023).

The objection survives in a narrow form — removing a truly dangerous person from your own streets can help your own streets — but it does not rescue deportation as a general crime policy. The lever is leaky at home, mostly aimed at the wrong people, and capable of detonating abroad.

A back-of-the-envelope on why the same policy helps a little and hurts a lot

The asymmetry at the heart of this is really about denominators. Around 2000, the United States had roughly 280 million people; El Salvador had about six million. Suppose — purely to fix orders of magnitude — that tens of thousands of gang-experienced deportees were absorbed by each country's gang ecosystem over those years. The same absolute transfer of criminal capital is diluted across a giant, well-policed population on one side and concentrated, in per-capita terms, into a tiny, war-shattered one on the other — and the ratio of their populations is roughly forty-five to one. A drop that the US barely registers is a flood for El Salvador. You don't need precise figures to see the point: when you move a stock of criminal capital from a large strong state to a small weak one, you should expect a small benefit there and a large harm here — which is exactly the pattern the studies above describe.

Bottom line

I read the evidence like this. In the one place we can study it cleanly, mass deportation of criminals into a weak post-war state helped manufacture one of the deadliest gang problems in the world, and that violence then pushed migration back toward the United States. That specific causal story rests on two independent natural experiments and is fairly convincing. The deeper reason — that crime is portable human and social capital, so relocating offenders relocates and can amplify crime — is well established in its own right and recurs across settings as different as Soviet gulags and Italian towns.

But calibrate carefully, because the title says "can," not "always." The backfire is conditional. It needs offenders who carry genuinely transferable criminal skills, and a receiving state too weak to absorb them. Strip out either ingredient and the mechanism should fade: deporting non-criminal immigrants doesn't move much crime in the first place, and even hardened offenders sent into a capable state have less room to seed an organization. So "deporting any criminal anywhere backfires" is not something the evidence supports — that would be overselling, and I want to be honest that it's more an extrapolation than a finding.

What the evidence does support is a humbler, sturdier claim: deportation of criminals is not the clean, free-lunch crime-reduction lever it is often imagined to be. Its ledger can run negative once you count the crime it relocates, the organizations it can seed in fragile places, and the migration that flows back. A policy sold as removing crime can, under the wrong conditions, end up exporting it.

What we don't know

  • The cross-border welfare sum. Nobody has credibly added up the US safety gains, El Salvador's catastrophic losses, and the boomerang migration into a single ledger. Until someone does, "net effect on the US" is genuinely open.
  • How much of the boomerang nets out. We know violence raises migration and that cutting it lowers migration sharply; we don't have a tight estimate of how much of the original deportation "savings" the return flow erases.
  • Whether better-targeted modern deportation backfires at all. If a state deports only truly dangerous individuals, into stronger receiving countries, the weak-state ingredient is missing. The El Salvador case may be closer to a worst case than a typical one.
  • Whether reintegration blunts it. Sviatschi and others gesture at reintegration programs for deportees and ex-convicts; we have little causal evidence on whether they work, though the "Making a Narco" result that schooling transfers undo criminal-capital formation is a hopeful hint.
  • The exact homicide magnitude. I've described the El Salvador violence effect as "large" because that is what the studies establish directionally and robustly; readers wanting a single elasticity should go to the source.
